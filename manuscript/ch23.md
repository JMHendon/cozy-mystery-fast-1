# Chapter 23

## The Wrong Woman

They let him go at five-thirty. At five thirty-one he was running.

He had spent the afternoon in the select board room giving a formal recorded statement to a trooper with a head cold. They had asked him the same question four ways, and he'd given the same answer four times, because the truth didn't change when you turned it over: he had seen Ivy Crane wipe the small end of the trumpet with her scarf and push the scarf into her coat pocket, and he'd told Sergeant Calloway so that same night. Then they'd opened the door, and he had come out onto the town hall steps just in time to see two cars turn off Main Street at the bottom of the hill — a gray Interceptor and a State Police cruiser, no lights, no hurry — onto Water Street.

He knew what was on Water Street.

The hearse was in the lot behind the building. It took two tries to start on a cold night and woke every dog it passed. He ran.

Main Street went down to the harbor in one long hill, and he went down it in his velvet jacket with no coat, past the hardware store and the dark ice-cream stand with its sign that said SEE YOU IN MAY, his breath going out ahead of him in clouds. He was forty-seven and he had legs like a heron's, which were wonderful for stepping over things and no use at all for this. Below him the harbor was black, with the riding lights of the moorings strung across it.

He'd been rehearsing it all afternoon. *I told them why. I told them it was a reset — that every performer alive puts the props back clean, that I'd have done exactly what you did.* He'd said all of it into the trooper's recorder. He wanted her to hear it from him first. It wasn't much. It was what he had.

The Spindrift Inn stood at the corner with every window lit. The front door was open, letting the heat out. Both cars sat in the curved drive with their engines running, and on the porch steps, in a Merrow PD jacket with her thumbs hooked in her belt, stood Officer Kayla Bissonnette.

"Mr. Fairweather." She came down one step. "You can't go in."

"I'm not going in." He was breathing like a bellows. "I'm going to stand here."

"Then stand there."

He stood there. Kayla was twenty-four and had been told to hold a door by someone she admired, and she was holding it. Past her, in the front hall, Pru Haskell stood in the parlor doorway with a dish towel in both hands, and two troopers came down the stairs carrying cardboard cartons and a laptop in a plastic bag.

Then Tess came down the hall from the back of the house with Ivy.

Ivy's hands were behind her. She was in the big gray sweater and canvas sneakers with no socks, and Tess had a hand on her elbow, not gripping it, and was speaking in a low, even voice — words Archie had heard on television his whole life and never once heard a person he knew say to another. Ivy nodded at each sentence.

Her throat was bare. The police had the scarf; they'd come for it Monday night with a warrant. Her neck looked thin and much younger without it.

They came down the hall. Kayla stepped aside for them at the door, and that left Archie at the foot of the steps in the porch light with nothing between him and them but the cold.

"Ivy," he said. "I told them why. I told them it was a reset—"

"Mr. Fairweather." Tess didn't raise her voice. "Step back."

He stepped back one step. "—that every performer in the world—"

"Further."

He went further. Ivy stopped at the top of the steps, and Tess let her stop. For the first time since the day they'd met, Ivy Crane wasn't looking at his hands. She was looking at his face.

"You told them about the scarf," she said.

"I told them what I saw."

"You told them the truth." She said it flatly, a fact she was filing. "Mom always said that about you people. You tell the truth, and then you stand there waiting to be thanked."

Tess moved her on. Down the steps, across the gravel, to the back door of the gray Interceptor, which Kayla had gone ahead to open.

"Sorry — sorry, Archie, one sec." Teddy Marchand had come out the side door by the parking lot with a phone at his ear and a mango-smelling cloud of vapor around his head, and he gave Archie the little chin-lift that men in fleece vests give each other at airports. Into the phone he said, "No, I'm here. I'm literally watching it. It's awful. It's genuinely awful, nobody's saying it isn't." He listened. "Sure. But between us? The shot of her coming down those steps? The numbers are going to be insane."

Archie had never hit anyone in his life. He stood in the drive of the Spindrift Inn and found he was considering it with real interest, like a new trick.

He took out his phone instead.

Margaret Okafor practiced criminal defense on Exchange Street in Portland and was, by the unhappy agreement of every prosecutor in New England, the best there was. Twelve years ago, at her own written request, Archie had made her wedding cake disappear — six tiers, commissioned by her mother-in-law without consultation — in front of a hundred and forty guests. Margaret had cried, and told him, *Anything, Archie. Ever.* She had been on his speed dial since, between June and Lenny.

She picked up on the second ring. "Who's dead?"

"Nobody new." He said it loudly, in Teddy's direction, the way you play to the back row. "Margaret, a woman named Ivy Crane is about to be driven to the jail in Rockland. Tampering with evidence and falsifying it — that's what they're saying tonight. They'll say more tomorrow. She's twenty-nine and she hasn't got anybody. Her mother was Lorelei Crane."

"The medium."

"The medium. I want you to represent her. I'll pay. Whatever it costs. I don't want to know what it costs."

"You'll know. I'll send you a letter and you'll need to sit down." He heard a drawer, then keys. "Knox County, by nine. Don't speak to her. Don't speak to the police about her. Don't call me on anything but my office line. Is she going to want me?"

Archie looked across the gravel. Tess had her palm on top of Ivy's head, guiding it in under the roof of the car.

"No," he said.

"Fine," said Margaret Okafor. "They never do." She hung up.

Teddy had lowered his own phone. "Buddy. Are you paying for her lawyer? Because the optics of that—"

Archie walked past him.

Ivy was in the back seat with her hands behind her, sitting very straight, the way she'd sat in the blue plastic chair in Rockport. The door was still open. Tess was saying something to Kayla across the roof. Ivy turned her head and found him at the edge of the gravel.

"Don't help me," she said.

"Her name's Margaret," said Archie. "She'll be there at nine. Don't say a word until she comes."

Ivy looked at him a moment longer. Then Tess shut the door.

Tess came around the car and stopped beside him, close enough that he could see something in her face that wasn't the cold.

"Go and get some sleep," she said.

"Is that an order, Sergeant?"

"It's advice. The orders start tomorrow." She got in behind the wheel.

Pru Haskell had come out onto the porch with the dish towel. "Can I get you something?" she said. "Coffee?"

"No. Thank you."

"I'll make it anyway," said Pru, and went back in.

The Interceptor pulled out of the drive, and the cruiser after it, and Archie stood alone at the edge of the gravel and watched the two pairs of taillights go down Water Street along the harbor — past the dark co-op, past the fuel dock, red and small and getting smaller — until they turned at the corner by the Galley and were gone.

Then he went back up the hill for the hearse.
