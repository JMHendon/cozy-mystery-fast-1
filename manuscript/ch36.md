# Chapter 36

## The Great Fairweather

By a quarter to seven there were three hundred and eleven people in the Merrow Opera House, which was eleven more than the fire marshal allowed and roughly a fifth of everybody who lived in the town.

Archie watched them come in through a gap in the stage-left curtain, which Gus had taught him never to do. The black drapes were gone from the windows now, and the tall arched panes were full of the dark and, far down in it, the lights of the moorings. The hall was warm for once — three hundred bodies and the new radiator valves — and loud, and it smelled of wet wool and popcorn and the cider Bev had brought up in two enormous coffee urns and was selling at the top of the stairs for a dollar a cup, for the roof.

Half the children were in costume: three ghosts, a pirate, a very small hot dog, and Rosie Mayo in the fourth row on her mother's lap, dressed as a lobster in a red felt suit with claws that went past her hands. Beside them Liam wore a black cape and a mustache drawn on with eyeliner, and Archie understood, with a feeling in his chest he didn't have time for, that Liam had come as him.

In the front row, by their own insistence, sat the liars' table — Elwin Coombs, Dickie Thibodeau and Pickle, side by side in their good coats with their arms folded, in the attitude of men who had come to see a boat launched and would believe it floated when it floated. Warren Gilley was in the sixth row on the aisle in his bow tie, and beside him, in a wheelchair with a pass from Sea View pinned to her coat, was Carol, holding a playing card in both hands. Kev Duchesne was in the balcony with a camera on a little tripod and a press pass Hollis had written out by hand.

At the very back of the hall, against the wall by the stairs in a gray parka, stood Kayla Bissonnette — not in uniform, and not watching the stage.

Archie's shoulders came down. Stepping out the stage door at half past five for air, he'd seen the Merrow PD cruiser parked up on Main Street with its lights off and somebody in it. Now here was Kayla, twenty feet below the booth, with the balcony stairs at her elbow. Tess had read his text. It was Tess's now, and the woman in the booth was watched.

Above Kayla, up in the horseshoe of the balcony, in the little plywood booth with its window full of soft amber light, a small square shape in a cardigan was bent over the dimmer board.

He let the curtain fall closed.

The envelope was in the inside pocket of his tailcoat, under the opera cloak. He could feel its edge when he breathed.

"Places," said Lenny behind him.

Lenny was in a headset over his sweaters, holding a clipboard he didn't need. Behind him in the wing stood June, in Gus's dark blue velvet jacket with the silver frogging, her hair up, her face white. Hollis had been right; she looked like a queen. Beside her, Vivienne, in black with diamonds that might even have been real, was doing breathing exercises from 1962.

"Junebug," Archie said.

"Don't," said June. "I'll throw up."

"You look—"

"*Dad.*"

He went out.

---

The house went dark. The talk died in a wave from the back, people hushing each other, a child saying *is it starting* and being shushed.

Then a single narrow spot from high at the back of the hall, from the booth, found the painted harbor curtain, and the curtain rose, and there, alone on the bare stage in the circle of light, a silver ball the size of a grapefruit was hanging in the empty air.

Nobody had seen it arrive. It was simply there, shining, turning slowly, four feet above the boards.

The hall made the sound Archie had spent his life working for. It isn't a gasp. It's quieter than that — three hundred people breathing in at the same time.

He walked out of the dark at the side of the stage in Gus's cloak, and the spot widened to take him in, and he did not look at the audience. He looked at the ball.

"My grandfather opened every show on this stage with this," he said. "From 1949 to 1988. He called it the Silver Moon."

He held a square of white silk up in front of the ball by two corners, and the ball rose behind the silk and settled against it, glowing through the fabric. When he walked, it came with him. He turned a slow circle and it went around him, under his arm, over his shoulder, along the line of his outstretched hand like a cat along a fence. He went down to the front of the stage and it went ahead of him, out over the footlights, over the front row, and hung there above the liars' table.

Elwin Coombs looked up at it with his mouth slightly open. Dickie Thibodeau leaned back in his chair to get away from it. Pickle, who had not spoken one word in Archie's hearing in nine weeks, slowly raised one finger and pointed at it.

Archie drew the silk away. The ball hung above Pickle's head by itself.

Then it drifted back across the footlights, slowly, like something tired coming home, and settled in his open palm, and was a ball. He tossed it once and caught it.

The hall applauded. Archie had heard every kind of applause there was, in every kind of room, and he stood in this one with the ball in his hand and let it come.

For the next forty minutes he forgot about the envelope.

The room took him. Three hundred people from one small harbor town, in their good coats and their children's costumes, wanting to be amazed, and he amazed them, because it was the one thing in the world he was sure he could do.

He did the linking rings with Deb Coombs, who refused to be impressed and then gasped out loud when the ring in her own hands linked through his. He swallowed a packet of needles and a length of thread and drew them out threaded, which made Bev, at the top of the stairs with her cider, say "Oh, for crying out *loud*" clearly enough to be heard on the stage. He borrowed Warren Gilley's wristwatch — asked for it, with a bow; no lifting tonight — and smashed it to pieces in a cloth bag with a hammer, and Warren went red to the tips of his ears, and Carol laughed out loud in her wheelchair, the clear delighted laugh of a girl at a show. Then Archie asked Carol to turn over the card she'd been holding all evening — the queen of hearts with WARREN across it — and Warren's watch was taped to the back of it, ticking.

Warren looked at the watch. Then he looked at his wife, who was holding the card up and showing it to the people around her, beaming. He put his hand over his eyes.

Archie went down to the front row and stood in front of the liars' table.

"Gentlemen," he said. "I'm going to need one of you."

Elwin and Dickie both looked at Pickle. Pickle looked at the ceiling.

"Sir." Archie held out a deck of cards, face down, fanned. "You don't have to touch them. You don't have to take one. Just look at the backs and think of any card in the deck. Don't tell me."

Pickle looked at the cards. Then he looked at Archie. Then, slowly, with the whole hall watching, he nodded.

"Now," said Archie, "for the first time in my presence, and I'm told for the first time in public in living memory — would you please stand up and tell everyone which card you're thinking of?"

The hall went silent. People at the back stood up to see.

Pickle unfolded himself from his chair. He was very tall standing up. He turned around and looked at the three hundred people of Merrow, Maine, every one of whom had known him all his life.

"Seven of hearts," he said, in a deep, rusty voice. And then, after a moment: "And my name's Lionel."

The opera house came apart.

It took Archie a full minute to get them quiet enough to tell them to look under their seats. Every seat in the house — all three hundred and eleven, and the folding chairs in the balcony, and Bev's stool by the cider urns — had a playing card taped underneath it. Every one of them was the seven of hearts.

Lionel looked at the card under his own seat, and at Archie, and sat back down, and folded his arms, and Archie could have sworn that under the gray stubble the man was very slightly smiling.

That was when he saw Tess.

She had come in at some point and was standing at the back by the stair door, a little apart from Kayla, in the charcoal coat with her braid over one shoulder. She wasn't laughing. She wasn't clapping. She was watching him — not the cards, not the crowd, but him — as if she were trying to work out how he did it.

He held her eyes for a second over three hundred heads. She didn't look away.

Then he went back up the steps to the stage, because there was one more number before the intermission, and it was Gus's, and the envelope was back against his chest.

---

The last number of the first act was the Sparrows.

It was the simplest thing in the show. Gus had closed his first half with it for forty years: a bare stage, a single light, the magician in his cloak, and out of the empty air of his hands, one by one, white doves. The cue was in the cue book in Hollis's handwriting, underlined: on the fourth bird, the stage goes to deep blue, and a single pin spot finds the magician's hands, and the doves fly up out of the light into the dark of the hall and settle on the balcony rail.

Archie produced the first dove from an empty silk, and the house sighed. The second from the collar of the cloak. The third from the air, bare-handed, in a flurry of white that drew a cry from somewhere at the back.

He produced the fourth, and lifted his hands, and waited for the blue.

The blue didn't come.

The stage stayed in its warm gold wash. The pin spot didn't find his hands. The light just held — steady, ordinary, wrong — the way stage lights hold when nobody is touching the board.

The doves went up out of his hands anyway, because doves don't wait for cues. They flew out over the heads of the audience in a white clatter of wings, and the people of Merrow turned in their seats to watch them, and three of them landed on the balcony rail as they'd been taught.

The fourth flew higher, and landed on the roof of the light booth.

The booth window was dark.

Archie stood at the center of the stage in the wrong light with his empty hands still raised. Out in the hall they were applauding — they didn't know; it had been beautiful anyway — and he lowered his hands and bowed, and smiled, and said something about a short intermission and Bev's cider and the roof.

In the stage-left wing, Lenny had already pulled off his headset. Archie watched him go through the side door to the balcony stairs faster than he had seen him move in thirty years.

Somebody out front found the house lights and threw them. The hall came up bright and loud and full of people standing and stretching and talking about the seven of hearts.

Archie walked off into the wing. He stood in the dimness by the rope rail in his grandfather's cloak, with the envelope against his chest, and waited.

It was a minute and a half. He counted.

Then Lenny came back through the side door, breathing hard, his reading glasses pushed up on his bald head, and crossed the wing and held out a small folded square of paper torn from the back of a cue book.

"It's for you," said Lenny.
