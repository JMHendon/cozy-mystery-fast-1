# Chapter 36

## The Great Fairweather

By a quarter to seven there were three hundred and eleven people in the Merrow Opera House, which was eleven more than the fire marshal allowed and roughly a fifth of everybody who lived in the town.

Archie watched them come in through a gap in the stage-left curtain, the way Gus had taught him never to do and every magician in history had done anyway. The black drapes were gone from the windows now. The tall arched panes were full of the dark, and in the dark, far down, the lights of the moorings and the green wink of Merrow Light out at the end of the point. The hall was warm for once — three hundred bodies and the new radiator valves — and loud, and it smelled of wet wool and popcorn and the cider Bev had brought up in two enormous coffee urns and was selling at the top of the stairs for a dollar a cup, for the roof.

Half the children were in costume. There were three ghosts, a pirate, two Harry Potters, a very small hot dog, and Rosie Mayo, aged four, dressed as a lobster in a red felt suit with claws that went past her hands, sitting on her mother's lap in the fourth row. Shannon had her arm around Rosie and her other hand on Liam's knee. Liam was wearing a black cape and a mustache drawn on with eyeliner, and Archie understood, with a feeling in his chest he didn't have time for, that Liam had come as him.

In the front row, by their own insistence, sat the liars' table. Elwin Coombs, Dickie Thibodeau and Pickle sat side by side in their good coats with their arms folded, in the attitude of men who had come to see a lobster boat launched and would believe it floated when it floated.

Warren Gilley was in the sixth row on the aisle, in his bow tie, and next to him, in a wheelchair with a blanket over her knees and a pass from Sea View pinned to her coat, was Carol. She was holding a playing card in both hands. Warren kept looking at her instead of the stage.

Kev Duchesne was in the second balcony row with a camera on a little tripod and a lanyard that said, in Hollis's handwriting, PRESS — APPROVED — BEHAVE. Deb Coombs was beside him, watching the camera as if it might steal a book.

At the very back of the hall, standing against the wall by the stairs in a gray parka with her hands in her pockets, was Kayla Bissonnette, who was twenty-four, and not in uniform, and not watching the stage.

And above her, up in the horseshoe of the balcony, in the little plywood booth with its window full of soft amber light, a small square shape in a cardigan was bent over the dimmer board.

Archie let the curtain fall closed.

The envelope was in the inside pocket of his tailcoat, under the opera cloak, against his chest. He could feel the edge of it when he breathed. He had been feeling it for four hours.

"Places," said Lenny behind him.

Archie turned. Lenny was in his fourth sweater and a headset, holding a clipboard he didn't need. Behind him, in the wing, June stood in Gus's dark blue velvet jacket with the silver frogging, her hair up, her face very white and very set. She looked, Hollis had been exactly right, like a queen. Beside her, Vivienne, in black with diamonds that might even have been real, was doing breathing exercises from 1962.

"Junebug," Archie said.

"Don't," said June. "I'll throw up."

"You look—"

"*Dad.*"

He went out.

---

The house went dark. The talk stopped the way it does in a theater, not all at once but in a wave from the back, people hushing each other, a child saying *is it starting* and being shushed.

Then a single light — a soft, narrow spot from high at the back of the hall, from the booth — found the center of the painted harbor curtain, and the curtain rose, and there, alone on the bare stage in the circle of light, a silver ball the size of a grapefruit was hanging in the empty air.

Nobody had seen it arrive. It was simply there, shining, turning very slowly, about four feet above the boards.

The hall made the sound Archie had spent his life working for. It isn't a gasp. It's quieter than that — three hundred people breathing in at the same time.

He walked out of the dark at the side of the stage in Gus's cloak, and the spot widened to take him in, and he did not look at the audience. He looked at the ball.

"My grandfather opened every show on this stage with this," Archie said. "From 1949 to 1988. He called it the Silver Moon."

He lifted a square of white silk from his cloak and held it up in front of the ball by two corners, like a man screening a candle from the wind, and the ball rose behind the silk and settled against it, glowing through the fabric. When he walked, it came with him. He turned in a slow circle and the ball went around him, under his arm, over his shoulder, along the outstretched line of his other hand like a cat along a fence. He went down to the front of the stage, and it went ahead of him, out over the footlights, out over the front row, and hung there above the liars' table.

Elwin Coombs looked up at it with his mouth slightly open. Dickie Thibodeau leaned back in his chair to get away from it. Pickle, who had not spoken one word in Archie's hearing in nine weeks, slowly raised one finger and pointed at it, as though to make sure everybody else could see it too.

Archie drew the silk away. The ball hung in the air above Pickle's head by itself.

Then it drifted back to him across the footlights, slowly, like something tired coming home, and settled in his open palm, and was a ball. He held it up to show them. He tossed it once in the air and caught it.

The applause began at the back and rolled forward and broke over the stage, and Archie Fairweather — who had stood in front of thirty thousand people at a time and heard every kind of applause there was — found, to his complete surprise, that this one went straight through him.

For the next forty minutes he forgot about the envelope.

He didn't mean to. It just happened, the way it always had, since he was four years old in his grandfather's kitchen with a silver dollar between two fingers. The room took him. Three hundred people from one small harbor town, in their good coats and their children's costumes, wanting to be amazed — and he amazed them, because it was the one thing in the world he was sure he could do.

He did the linking rings with Deb Coombs, who refused to be impressed and then gasped out loud when the ring in her own hands linked through his. He did the needle trick, swallowing a packet of needles and a length of thread and drawing them out threaded, which made Bev, at the top of the stairs with her cider, say "Oh, for God's sake" loudly enough to be heard on the stage. He borrowed Warren Gilley's wristwatch — asked for it, with a bow; no lifting tonight — and smashed it to pieces in a cloth bag with a hammer, and Warren went red to the tips of his ears, and Carol laughed out loud in her wheelchair, the clear delighted laugh of a girl at a show. Then Archie had Carol open the playing card she'd been holding in both hands all evening — the queen of hearts with WARREN across it — and Warren's watch was taped to the back of it, ticking.

Warren looked at the watch for a long moment. Then he looked at his wife, who was holding the card up and showing it to the people around her, beaming. He put his hand over his eyes.

And then Archie went down to the front row and stood in front of the liars' table.

"Gentlemen," he said. "I'm going to need one of you."

Elwin and Dickie both looked at Pickle. Pickle looked at the ceiling.

"Sir." Archie held out a deck of cards to him, face down, fanned. "You don't have to touch them. You don't have to take one. All you have to do is look at the backs and think of any card in the deck. Don't tell me. Any card at all."

Pickle looked at the cards for a long time. Then he looked at Archie. Then, slowly, with the whole hall watching, he nodded.

"Now," said Archie, "for the first time in my presence, and I'm told for the first time in public since about 1994 — would you please stand up and tell everyone which card you're thinking of?"

The hall went absolutely still. Several people in the back stood up to see better.

Pickle unfolded himself from his chair. He was very tall standing up. He turned around and looked at the three hundred people of Merrow, Maine, every one of whom had known him all his life.

"Seven of hearts," he said, in a deep, rusty voice. And then, after a moment, as if it had been building up for thirty years: "And my name's Lionel."

The opera house came apart.

It took Archie a full minute to get them quiet enough to tell them to look under their seats. Every seat in the house — all three hundred and eleven of them, and the folding chairs in the balcony, and Bev's stool by the cider urns — had a playing card taped underneath it. Every one of them was the seven of hearts.

Lionel looked at the card under his own seat, and at Archie, and sat back down, and folded his arms, and Archie could have sworn — though he'd never have said so out loud — that under the gray stubble the man was very slightly smiling.

That was when Archie saw Tess.

She'd come in at some point and was standing at the back by the stair door, a little away from Kayla, in the charcoal coat with her braid over one shoulder and her hands in her pockets. She wasn't laughing. She wasn't clapping. She was just watching him — not the cards, not the crowd, but him, the way she'd watched him across her father's wheelchair at Sea View on the first day. As if she were trying to work out how he did it.

He held her eyes for a second over three hundred heads. She didn't look away.

Then he turned and went back up the steps to the stage, because there was one more number before the intermission, and it was Gus's, and the envelope was suddenly back against his chest as if it had never left.

---

The last number of the first act was the Sparrows.

It was the simplest thing in the show. Gus had done it to close his first half every year for forty years: a bare stage, a single light, the magician in his cloak, and out of the empty air of his hands, one by one, white doves. The cue was written into the cue book in Hollis's handwriting in three places, underlined: on the fourth bird, the stage goes to deep blue, and a single pin spot finds the magician's hands, and the doves fly up out of the light into the dark of the hall and settle on the balcony rail.

Archie produced the first dove from an empty silk, and the house sighed. The second from the collar of the cloak. The third from the air, bare-handed, in a flurry of white that drew a cry from somewhere in the back.

He produced the fourth, and lifted his hands, and waited for the blue.

The blue didn't come.

The stage stayed in its warm gold wash. The pin spot didn't find his hands. The light just held — steady, ordinary, wrong — the way stage lights hold when nobody is touching the board.

The four doves went up out of his hands anyway, because doves don't wait for cues. They flew up over the heads of the audience in a white clatter of wings and out across the dark hall, and the people of Merrow turned in their seats to watch them go, and three of them landed on the balcony rail just as they'd been taught.

The fourth flew higher, and landed on the roof of the light booth.

The booth window was dark.

Archie stood at the center of the stage in the wrong light with his empty hands still raised. Out in the hall the audience was applauding — they didn't know; there was no reason for them to know; it had been beautiful anyway — and he lowered his hands and bowed, because his body knew how to do that without him, and smiled, and heard himself say something about a short intermission and Bev's cider and the roof.

At the edge of his vision, in the stage-left wing, Lenny had already taken off his headset. Archie watched him go — moving faster than Archie had seen him move in thirty years — through the side door to the balcony stairs.

Somebody out front found the switch for the house lights and threw it. The hall came up bright and loud and full of people standing and stretching and talking about the seven of hearts.

Archie walked off into the wing. He stood there in the dimness by the rope rail in his grandfather's cloak, with the envelope against his chest, and waited, and did not let himself think anything at all.

It was a minute and a half. He knew, because he counted.

Then Lenny came back through the side door, breathing hard, his reading glasses pushed up on his bald head, and crossed the wing to him, and held out a small folded square of paper torn from the back of a cue book.

"It's for you," said Lenny.
