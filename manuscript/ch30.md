# Chapter 30

## Visiting Hours

The Knox County Jail was a low brick building on the edge of Rockland with a flagpole, a lot full of pickup trucks, and a lobby that smelled of floor wax and burnt coffee. At five minutes to ten on Thursday morning Archie stood in it with his pockets on a plastic tray — keys, wallet, a deck of cards, two half-dollars, a roll of peppermints and a lock pick shaped like a hairpin — while a corrections officer named Doucette looked at the lock pick and then at him.

"It's a hairpin," said Archie.

"It's not a hairpin."

"It's a very ambitious hairpin."

Doucette put it in an envelope and wrote on the envelope.

"He's with me," said Margaret Okafor, behind him. "God help me."

She had driven up from Portland at six. She was in her fifties, compact, in a black wool coat and flat shoes, with close-cropped hair gone silver at the temples and a leather briefcase older than her client. Eleven years ago, at her own wedding, she had taken Archie aside during the speeches and asked him, as a personal favor, to make the cake disappear, because her mother-in-law had chosen it and it was lemon. He had made it disappear. The hotel kitchen staff had eaten it. She'd sent him a thank-you note on letterhead.

She walked him down a corridor of painted cinderblock to a door with a narrow window in it, and stopped.

"Ground rules," she said. "You are a witness for the State. You do not discuss your statement. You do not discuss the scarf. If she starts to, you stop her, and if you don't, I will, and I'm better at it." She looked at him over a pair of reading glasses she hadn't put on. "You're here because the State has a theory, my client has a confession that isn't one, and the most famous debunker in the world is paying my invoices. I want a third party, Mr. Fairweather. Somebody with a better reason than an inheritance. Find me one."

"You think there is one."

"I think the Attorney General filed a murder charge this morning on a scarf and a bad feeling." She opened the door. "Twenty minutes."

---

The attorney visiting room was the green of a hospital corridor, with a steel table bolted to the floor and three plastic chairs. Ivy Crane was already sitting in one of them, in an orange jail sweatshirt, with her hands folded on the table. Without the silk scarf her throat looked bare and young. Her hair was still scraped back. Somebody had taken her hair elastic and given her a rubber band instead.

She looked at him, and then at her lawyer.

"I said I didn't want to see him."

"And I said I decide who's useful," said Okafor, and sat down and uncapped a fountain pen.

Archie sat across from Ivy. He had driven twenty minutes down Route 1 rehearsing an opening, and when he opened his mouth it was gone.

"They charged you," he said.

"I know. I was there."

"With murder, Ivy."

"I know what they charged me with." Her voice was perfectly flat. "I told them it was my fault. It was."

"You told them that because you set out the horn."

She didn't answer.

"Every night," he said. "You lay it out. You put it in the middle of the table. And you think if you hadn't—"

"If there's no horn, there's nothing to put anything on." She said it to the table. "If I'd quit at twenty. If I'd quit at twelve. That's not a feeling, Mr. Fairweather. That's arithmetic."

"It's arithmetic with a hole in it."

"Then find the hole." She looked up. "Isn't that why she brought you?"

He wanted to argue. He had the argument ready; he could feel it arranging itself, patter and all. He looked at her folded hands, which were the hands of a woman who had spent seventeen years doing difficult things in the dark, and put the argument away.

"I taught my daughter a trick last night," he said.

Okafor's pen paused. Ivy said nothing.

"Gus's finale. The Empty Chair. She asked me on Wednesday to teach her all of it, so I did. In the carriage house, with a coat hanger and a sheet and my engineer sitting under a trapdoor on a sandbag." He shrugged. "She was terrible the first time. She nearly broke my ankle."

"All of it?" said Ivy.

"All of it."

"Including the part you didn't want to show her."

He looked at her. "Including that."

"How old?"

"Sixteen."

Ivy was quiet for a moment. Then she unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the steel.

"I was twelve," she said. "A motel in Nashua. She turned off the lights and held a hairbrush out in the dark and said, *Take my hand, baby.* Left hand under, right hand over. When she squeezes twice, you let go one finger at a time. You keep your other hand on Warren Gilley's, or whoever's, so he's sure. He's holding a medium's daughter. That's all he was ever promised." She said it like a recipe. "Then you've got a hand free, and so has she. The rod's under the cloth at my place. The glove on the end is wet. I do the bell and the touches. She does the horn."

"Ivy," said Okafor, not looking up.

"They have it on video. The fan's video. It isn't a secret anymore." Ivy didn't take her eyes off Archie. "The remote's up my left sleeve. When she lifts the horn I kill the cameras. Teddy calls it the static. Teddy has always known exactly what it is. When it's over and the lights come up, before anybody else can touch it, you wipe the horn. There's lipstick on the small end. Every séance since I was twelve. I've never once thought about it. You don't think about it. You just do it."

"It's a reset," said Archie.

"It's a reset." For the first time something moved in her face. "I wasn't hiding anything on Saturday night except her."

Okafor wrote that down, word for word, and underlined it.

Archie sat back in the plastic chair. In his head, for no reason he could have explained to her, Kev Duchesne was standing in the dark under the opera house stage with an EMF meter in one hand, telling him about the Trumpet Hiatus with the reverence of a man describing a moon landing. *Two thousand three to two thousand six, Mr. Fairweather. Sister Lori wouldn't use the trumpet in private sittings. Not once. The forums think Brother Amos told her to.*

"Your mother stopped using the horn," he said. "In the private sittings. For three years."

Ivy's eyes narrowed very slightly. "Kev Duchesne."

"Kev Duchesne."

"Brother Amos told her to put it away. That's the story."

"Brother Amos was your mother in a deeper voice."

"Brother Amos was a bathrobe and an accent she got off a record of Welsh hymns," said Ivy. "Yes."

"Then here's what I know about performers." He leaned forward. "You don't drop the best routine you've got for three years because a fan forum says so. You drop a routine for two reasons. It's stopped working, or it hurt somebody. That horn never stopped working. It's the best trumpet I ever sat with, and I've sat with forty." He kept his voice low. "What happened in 2003?"

The room was very quiet. Somewhere down the corridor a door buzzed and banged.

"I was seven," Ivy said.

Okafor's pen stopped.

"I don't remember much. I remember it was raining. We were parked outside a church. Here — in Rockland. She'd gone inside, which she never did unless she was working a church, and I was in the back seat with a coloring book. She came out with a newspaper under her coat and got into the driver's seat and didn't start the car." Ivy looked at a point on the green wall above his shoulder. "She just sat there with the wipers off and cried until the windows were all rain and you couldn't see out. I'd never seen her cry when it wasn't for a sitter."

"Did she say why?"

"She said a man had died." A small, terrible shrug. "I was seven. I wanted to know if we could still get McDonald's."

Archie didn't say anything.

"Years later," said Ivy, "in a hotel bar in Phoenix, after three of something, she told me she never did the horn on a private sitting again after the man in Maine. That's how she said it. *The man in Maine.* As if that was his name." She looked back at Archie. "That's all I know. I don't know who he was."

"Would she have written him down?"

"She wrote everybody down."

"Ivy," said Okafor again, differently.

"She kept appointment books," Ivy said. "Thirty years of them. Every sitter since the church halls — names, towns, what they wanted, what she charged them, what worked. In her own shorthand. Nobody can read it but her." She stopped. "Nobody could."

"Where are they?"

She didn't answer right away. He watched her decide.

"In a hatbox," she said. "In Pru Haskell's office safe at the inn. I moved them on Sunday. I told Pru they were Mother's private things." Her chin came up a fraction. "They are."

"Why move them?"

"Because those books are thirty years of people crying in rented rooms," said Ivy, "and I wasn't going to have them read out on a folding table by a lieutenant who hates his reading glasses."

Okafor had stopped writing. She looked at her client for a long moment, and then at Archie, and something in her face rearranged itself into the expression of a woman who had just been handed a third party with a ribbon on it.

"The estate isn't probated," she said, half to herself. "The papers belong to the estate. My client is the sole heir. And Mrs. Haskell is an innkeeper, not a probate judge." She tore a fresh sheet from her legal pad and began to write in a fast, upright hand. "Release of personal property. To Archibald Fairweather, one hatbox, contents, from the custody of Prudence Haskell. Ivy, read it before you sign it. Always read it."

Ivy read it. She signed it. Okafor signed under her, and dated it, and wrote her bar number beside her name, and tore the sheet off the pad and folded it once and held it out across the table.

Archie took it.

"Read them," Ivy said. She was looking at the paper in his hand, not at him. "Then you'll know what she was. All of it."

"I already liked her," Archie said. "That's the problem."

Ivy looked at him then. She didn't say anything. There was a knock on the door window — Doucette, holding up five fingers — and she stood, and put her hands behind her back because that was what you did here, and went out without looking back.

---

In the corridor, Okafor shook his hand and kept hold of it a second too long.

"Third party, Mr. Fairweather," she said. "Bring me one. And don't make me read about it in the *Press Herald* first."

She went off toward the interview rooms. Archie walked the other way, toward the lobby and his envelope with the hairpin in it, with Ivy Crane's signature folded in his hand.

Halfway down the corridor a door opened, and Tess Calloway came out of it with a trooper Archie didn't know and a thick file under her arm.

She was in the charcoal coat. Her braid was wet; it must have started raining again. She saw him, and her stride didn't change, and her eyes went once — quick, gray, exact — to the paper in his hand, where the fold had come open enough to show two signatures, and then back to his face.

He didn't say *Sergeant*. She didn't say anything at all.

They passed each other in the green corridor, close enough to touch, and kept walking, and behind him the door at the far end buzzed and opened and closed.
