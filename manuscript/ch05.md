# Chapter 5

## The Sign-Out Card

The Merrow Public Library closed at five on Fridays, and at half past four Deb Coombs was already looking at the clock over the circulation desk as though it owed her money.

It was a small granite building on Elm Street with a green copper dome and a brass plaque that said 1911, and inside it was all oak and radiator heat and the vanilla smell of old paper. There was one long reading room with tall windows, going blue now as the afternoon gave up; a children's corner with a rag rug and a stuffed moose; a microfilm machine under a dust cover; and, behind the circulation desk, a single glass-fronted bookcase with a hand-lettered card taped to it: FAIRWEATHER COLLECTION — LOCAL HISTORY — ASK AT DESK.

Archie asked at the desk.

"No," said Deb Coombs.

She was in her sixties, small and square, with cropped gray hair, half-moon glasses, and a cardigan the color of a tennis ball. Her husband was Elwin Coombs's nephew, which meant, as far as Archie could work out, that she was related to a third of the liars' table and disapproved of all of them.

"I haven't said what I want yet," said Archie.

"You want Gus's book. Everybody in town's been in for it since yesterday. The answer is in-library use only, it's the only copy north of Portland, the binding's going, and I don't care whose grandson you are."

"That's fair."

"I know it's fair. I made the rule."

"How about if I just look at it here? At a table. Under supervision."

Deb considered him over the half-moons. Every sixth-grader in Merrow had bargained with her for a quarter of a century, and she had won.

"What's in it for the library?" she said.

Archie leaned on the desk. "What does the library want?"

It turned out the library wanted a great many things — a new roof on the dome, a second microfilm reader, the stuffed moose cleaned, a Wi-Fi signal that reached the back of the reading room — and Archie, who had never in his life been able to resist a list of things somebody wanted, said yes to the Wi-Fi before she'd finished, and was reaching for his checkbook when she held up one hand.

"Not money," said Deb. "Everybody in this town thinks you'll fix everything with money, and you probably can, and that's what worries me. Second Saturday in November. Story hour. Ten a.m., children's corner." She pointed at the rug and the moose. "You'll read what I give you, you'll do one trick, and you will not saw anybody in half."

"Deal," said Archie, and meant it so much that she looked faintly alarmed.

She took a key from a drawer, unlocked the glass case, and drew out a thin hardback in a faded green cloth binding. The spine said *THE DEAD DON'T WHISPER* in gilt letters that had mostly worn away. Underneath, smaller: *AUGUSTUS FAIRWEATHER*.

Archie took it in both hands.

He'd read it as a boy — twice, three times — sitting on the floor of the turret room on summer afternoons while Gus worked at the rolltop desk. He hadn't held the town's copy since he was ten, and the weight of it in his hands was the weight of a summer he'd mostly lost track of.

"Reading table," said Deb. "Not the window. The radiator under the window leaks."

He took it to the long oak table in the middle of the reading room and sat. The first page had a library stamp — MERROW PUBLIC LIBRARY, a little anchor in the middle — and a bookplate in Gus's hand: *For the people of Merrow, who were never fooled for long. G.F., 1971.*

On the inside back cover there was a manila pocket, and in the pocket there was a card.

He slid it out. Archie had never in his life left a pocket unexplored. It was the old kind of library card, buff-colored, ruled in faint blue lines, with columns for DATE DUE and BORROWER'S NAME. Most libraries had thrown these out decades ago. Deb, evidently, had not. In more than fifty years, it seemed, Gus's book had left the building only a handful of times.

"Oh, that card," said Deb, who had come up behind him with a stack of returns on her hip. She leaned over his shoulder and ran a finger down it.

*H. Coombs — 2/79.*

"My mother-in-law. She wanted to know how they did the table-tipping at the Grange. Nobody ever told her."

*R. Thibodeau — 4/79.*

"Bev's father-in-law. Gus took six dollars off him at cribbage, and he wanted to know how. It isn't in there."

*W. Calloway — 11/88*, in square block capitals, pressed hard.

"Walt Calloway. He took it home after Gus went. Kept it a month. I had to go up to the station and ask for it."

"Why'd he want it?"

"He didn't say. I expect he wanted to see if there was anything in it." She shrugged. "There wasn't. It's about séances." The finger moved on. *H. Pelletier — 3/92.* "Reverend Pelletier's wife. He was preaching on false prophets, and she did his reading for him. She said the book was funnier than the sermon." *M. Pinkham — 6/95. H. Langley — 12/03.* "Dale's cousin's girl, the Pinkham. School report. She got an A, and she told me about that A every Saturday for a year. And then nobody, twenty years and more, until—"

Archie had found the bottom of the card. Three lines, all in the same neat, slanting, very familiar hand:

*J. Fairweather — Sept.*

*J. Fairweather — Oct.*

*J. Fairweather — Oct.*

"My mother?" he said.

"Your mother's name starts with a V, Mr. Fairweather," said Deb, and went off to shelve.

He sat there for a moment. Then he looked past the long table and the microfilm machine to the far corner of the reading room, where a deep old armchair stood by a bookcase of bound *Merrow Packets*. He hadn't looked at it when he came in.

The top of a dark bun with a pencil stuck through it was just visible over the back of the chair.

Archie got up quietly and walked over.

June was curled sideways in the armchair with her Doc Martens tucked under her and her chemistry textbook open on her knees. On top of the textbook was a deck of cards. She had a card in her right hand — the jack of clubs — and as he watched, she squared the deck in her left, lifted the top half with two fingers, slipped the jack into the break, closed it, and then, with a small twist of her wrists that he'd have recognized from across a stadium, brought the jack invisibly back to the top.

It was a pass. A two-handed pass, the classic one, the one magicians practiced for years in front of mirrors and still flubbed half the time. She did it again. It was clean. Not perfect — her right thumb flashed a little at the end, she'd need to fix that — but clean. Clean enough to fool a room.

"Junebug," he said.

She jumped so hard the deck went everywhere.

Cards fanned across the floor, under the chair, against the bound volumes of the *Packet*. June looked at them, and then up at her father, and her whole face went red from the collar of her cardigan to the pencil in her hair.

"What are you doing here?" she said.

"What are *you* doing here?"

"Homework."

He crouched and began picking up cards. The jack of clubs had landed face up by his shoe. He held it up.

"This is not chemistry."

"It's a break. People take breaks." She snatched the jack out of his hand. "You're not supposed to be here. You never come here."

"I came for Gus's book." The grin took over his whole face. "June. That was a *pass.*"

"It was nothing."

"It was a two-handed pass! You've been learning out of his books. You signed it out three times. You've been — how long have you been doing this?"

"It doesn't matter."

"How long?"

June looked at the cards in her lap. "Since August," she said, very low. "There's a whole shelf of his stuff in the turret. I just — I was bored. There's nothing to do here. I read some of it. It's not a big deal."

"Not a big deal? Your thumb flashes a little at the end, but you've got the hard part, that's the part people spend years on, I spent two years on that, I used to practice in the bathroom on cruise ships — June, this is *wonderful.*" He was talking too fast, and he knew it, and he kept going. "Do you know what we could do with this? The show. Halloween. You could come out in the second half, we'd do something together, something of Gus's — the Chair, we could do the Chair, nobody's done the Chair on that stage since 1988, think of it, Fairweathers three generations—"

"Dad."

"—I'd teach you the rest, the misdirection's the easy part once you've got the—"

"*Dad.*"

He stopped.

June was sitting up straight in the big armchair. Her face was still red, but it had gone set, the way it did before a debate round when she'd decided what the argument was going to be and nobody was going to talk her out of it.

"It's not for the show," she said. "It's not for you. It's not for anybody. It's mine." She gathered the cards out of her lap and squared them with a sharp tap against the textbook. "It's the one thing in this whole town that's mine. You've got the opera house and the hearse and the TV specials and the — the *bet*, and everybody knowing who you are. I've got this. So I'm not doing it on a stage, and I'm not doing it on Halloween, and I'm not doing it with you. Okay?"

The reading room was quiet. Over at the circulation desk, Deb Coombs had a book in each hand and wasn't shelving either of them.

Archie looked at his daughter. He had stood in front of thirty thousand people at a time, and walked through a wall at Fenway Park, and been locked in a safe at the bottom of Lake Tahoe with four minutes of air, and he could not remember ever in his life having been this completely without a next line.

"Okay," he said.

June looked at him suspiciously, waiting for the rest of it.

There wasn't a rest of it. He stood up, and his knees cracked, and he handed her the four of diamonds, which had gotten under his shoe.

"Your thumb," he said. "At the end. If you turn your wrist about ten degrees more toward the — sorry. Never mind. It's yours."

She took the card. After a moment she turned her wrist, very slightly, and looked at it, and didn't say anything.

He went back to the long table, sat down in front of his grandfather's book, and opened it at random, because he needed something to look at that wasn't his daughter. It fell open — as old books do, to the place where they've been opened most — at a chapter headed *THE TRUMPET IN THE DARK*, page one hundred and twelve.

*The medium,* Gus had written, in his dry, amused, unmistakable voice, *will invariably arrange to have one hand free. With it she lifts the trumpet, which has been painted with luminous bands so that the sitters may admire its flight. She places the small end to her own lips and speaks through it, moving it about as she speaks, and the voice appears to come from the air. It is the oldest trick in the room, and it works because nobody in the room wants it not to.*

Archie read the paragraph three times. Then he sat looking at the dark windows with his hand flat on the page.

At five o'clock exactly, Deb Coombs turned the lights off over the children's corner, and June came past the table with her backpack on one shoulder and her chemistry book under her arm. She paused beside his chair.

"Grandma's making soup," she said.

"I'll be right there."

She nodded. She stood there a second longer than she needed to. Then she went out, and the bell over the library door jingled behind her, and Archie sat alone in the reading room with Gus's book open in front of him and the old buff card beside it, and the unwelcome feeling that for once in his life, the right thing to do was nothing at all.
