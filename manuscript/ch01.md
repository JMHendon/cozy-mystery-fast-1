# Chapter 1

## Upside Down

Hanging upside down, Archie Fairweather had discovered, improved almost everything.

It improved the Merrow Opera House, for a start. Right side up, the old hall was a cold, high, honest room above the town office — three hundred folding wooden chairs, a pressed-tin ceiling, iron radiators that knocked like someone wanted to be let in, and tall arched windows full of gray October light off the harbor. Upside down, it became a place where anything could happen. The painted curtain behind him, an 1899 canvas of Merrow Harbor in high summer, hung the wrong way up in his eyes, so that its schooners sailed along the bottom of a blue sky and its gulls flew underneath the water.

It also improved the straitjacket. Right side up, a straitjacket was a canvas coat with very long sleeves and opinions about your elbows. Upside down, with his ankles locked into a padded harness and twelve feet of air between his head and the stage, it became a conversation, and Archie had always been good at conversations.

"One minute," said Lenny, from the rail.

"I heard you the first time."

"I didn't say it the first time. That was the first time."

Archie rolled his left shoulder, found the slack he'd stolen when Lenny buckled him in, and walked his right arm up across his own chest by about an inch. The blood was pooling in his face. His hair, too long and going silver at the temples, hung toward the stage like the bristles of a broom.

The record was two minutes and twelve seconds. His grandfather had set it on this stage in 1961, in a jacket borrowed from the state hospital in Augusta, and there was a poster about it in the stairwell — THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER DEFIES THE ASYLUM! — that Archie touched for luck every time he climbed up here. In eight days, on Halloween night, he intended to break it in front of the whole town. Today was the first day he'd gotten anywhere close.

"One-fifteen," Lenny said.

Lenny Szabo was seventy, five foot six in his boots, and built like a fire hydrant that had given up on the world. He wore three sweaters because the opera house was cold, and a fourth one around his shoulders in case the other three let him down. He had built every illusion Archie had ever performed, in thirty years and on four continents, and he had never once said a word of praise about any of them, because, as he'd once explained, they were hinges. You didn't praise a hinge. You oiled it.

Archie got his right hand over his left shoulder and seized the strap at the back of his neck with two fingers.

"One-thirty."

"Lenny, I love you, but I need you to stop telling me the time."

"You told me to tell you the time."

"I've grown as a person."

That was when the doors at the back of the hall banged open, and a woman's voice said, "— and here's the hall itself. Mind the floorboard by the door, it's been loose since Carter was president."

Archie knew the voice. Hollis Tibbetts had run the Merrow Players for thirty years and taught English at the regional high school for thirty-five, and she spoke to everyone, including the select board and the weather, as if they had not done the reading. She had been the first person in Merrow to knock on his door in August. She'd brought him a blueberry pie and the opera house keys, in that order, and told him he looked like he needed both.

Behind her, upside down, came three people Archie had never seen before.

"Oh, honey," said the one in front. "Look at you."

She was a tall woman in a long velvet coat the color of red wine, with jet-black hair and a single streak of pure white running back from one temple. Her mouth was a dark, glossy red. There were rings on most of her fingers, big pale stones that caught the window light, and she had the kind of walk that turned a drafty town hall into a red carpet whether the town hall wanted it or not.

Archie had seen her face on the sides of buses, under the words *SHE TALKS TO THEM. THEY TALK BACK.* He'd also seen it on a folder in Las Vegas labeled CHALLENGE — NO RESPONSE.

"Mrs. Crane," he said. "You're early."

"Lorelei, please." She came down the center aisle and stopped below him, tipping her head to look into his face. "And you're upside down. I'd say we're both doing what we do."

"One-forty-five," said Lenny.

"Is he all right?" asked the man behind her. He was in his forties, in a fleece vest and very white sneakers, with a phone in each hand, already raising one to film. "Can we get this? Can I just — Lorelei, this is incredible, this is the cold open."

"No," said Lenny.

The man looked around for whoever had spoken. Lenny did not help him.

"Teddy Marchand," the man said to the room in general. "Marchand Media. We produce *The Other Side*."

The third visitor said nothing. She was younger, late twenties, in an oversized gray sweater with a silk scarf knotted at the throat, her hair scraped back so tightly it looked like a decision. She stopped halfway down the aisle. Archie noticed her because she was the only one of the three who wasn't looking at his face. She was watching his hands.

Nobody watched his hands. That was the whole point of the face.

"My daughter," said Lorelei. "Ivy. She doesn't do small talk, so don't take it personally."

"I never take anything personally upside down," said Archie. "It all runs to my head."

"Two minutes," said Lenny.

Archie got the neck strap open. He felt the left sleeve go slack. Twelve seconds — he could still do it in twelve seconds, if he concentrated.

"Hollis," he said, without looking away from the medium. "Why are the dead people's representatives in my theater on a Thursday?"

"Because they're doing a walkthrough on Thursday," said Hollis, "and you were supposed to be at the hardware store. I told you on Tuesday." She was a short, sturdy woman with a white crop of hair, a men's cardigan, reading glasses on a beaded chain and fingerless gray wool gloves she wore from September to May. Her ring of keys, the size of a dinner plate, clanked at her hip. She did not look sorry.

"You told me they were *coming*. You didn't say they were coming *in*."

"How else would they get here?"

"And who," said Archie, "invited them?"

"I did." Hollis folded her arms. "Back in May, before you'd ever set foot in Merrow, when this hall was on the list to be condemned and the town didn't have two nickels for the roof. I told Mr. Marchand's company we had the most haunted theater in New England and the thirty-seventh anniversary of the Great Fairweather's vanishing coming up on Halloween. Forty thousand dollars for the location. That's a roof." She looked up at the pressed-tin ceiling, where a long brown stain mapped several decades of leaks. "You can be cross with me under a dry ceiling."

There was no arguing with a roof. Archie had tried, in his head, for about a month.

"Two-oh-five," said Lenny.

Lorelei Crane stepped up onto the first stair at the side of the stage so that her face was level with his. Up close she smelled of woodsmoke and something expensive. Her eyes were dark and amused.

"I know about your challenge, Mr. Fairweather," she said. "A million dollars to any medium who'll sit under your controls. You sent me the letter three times."

"Four."

"Four. I'm sorry. My people lose things." She smiled. "Bring your controls on Saturday. Bring all the controls you like."

That was the first moment Archie felt the floor of the conversation move. Nobody said yes to the controls. Not in thirty years. They said the energy would be wrong, or that skeptics upset the spirits, or, if they were honest, *no*.

"All of them?" he said.

"Why not?" Lorelei put one ringed hand flat against the canvas over his chest, lightly, the way you might touch a horse. "But you should know something first, honey. Your grandfather's here. He's been standing behind you this whole time." She tilted her head, listening to the air beside his ear. "He says you still haven't opened his trunk."

Archie stopped moving.

His hands quit in the middle of the left sleeve, with his elbow halfway across his own throat.

There was a trunk. It was in the turret room of his grandfather's house on Gull Point, a black steamer trunk with gold stars painted on it and seven brass letter-dials on the lock, and on its lid, under a square of old celluloid, a card in Gus Fairweather's handwriting: *To be opened when I'm gone. Not before.* Archie had tried eleven thousand and some words on those dials since August. He kept them in a notebook. He had not told anyone in Merrow about the notebook. He had barely told himself.

"Two-ten," said Lenny, in a slightly different voice.

Lorelei took her hand away. "He's proud of you," she said gently. "But he thinks you're looking in the wrong place."

Afterward, Archie could never say exactly how he got out. There was a gap in his memory between the medium's hand leaving his chest and the jacket falling past his face to the stage with a canvas slap, and in that gap something in him had refused to hang there and be read like a menu.

"Time," he said, upside down and coatless.

Lenny looked at the stopwatch. He looked at it again.

"Two-eleven," he said.

Hollis Tibbetts made a short barking sound, which was how she laughed, and began to clap. After a moment Teddy Marchand joined in, one phone still raised. Ivy Crane did not. She was still looking at his hands.

"Let me down, Lenny."

Lenny let him down. Archie came out of the harness in a crouch, stood up too fast, and waited for the blood to finish relocating before he trusted his face. Then he walked to the lip of the stage. Lorelei Crane had stepped back into the aisle and was waiting for him with every appearance of enjoying herself.

"That was beautiful," Archie said. "Truly. The trunk. You must have a very good researcher."

"I have a very good grandfather of yours."

"Here's my offer. The Fairweather Challenge, on Saturday, on this stage. My controls. My cameras next to yours. If one single thing happens at your séance that I can't show the audience how you did, I'll write a check for a million dollars to the charity of your choice. On the air."

Teddy Marchand made a small, happy noise.

Lorelei considered him, her head on one side. "Make it two," she said.

"Two million."

"Two million. And you don't stand in the corner with a flashlight, honey. You sit in the circle. Hands held, lights out, like everybody else. If you want to catch me, you have to be in the dark with me." She smiled her dark red smile. "Unless you're afraid of what you'll hear."

Archie had been afraid of a great many things in his life — sharks, opening nights, his mother — and had made a very good living facing every one of them in front of a live studio audience. He felt the old grin arrive before his brain had finished voting.

"Saturday," he said. "Bring your grandfather. I'll bring mine."

He held out his hand. She shook it. Her rings were cold.

"Wonderful," said Teddy Marchand, typing with both thumbs. "That's — honestly, that's the special. That's the whole special."

They left the way they'd come, Hollis leading, talking about fire exits. At the doors Ivy Crane looked back once — not at Archie's face, but at the hand that had shaken her mother's — and then the hall was cold and quiet again except for the radiators.

Lenny began to coil the rope.

"You agreed to sit in the dark with her," he said.

"I did."

"For two million dollars."

"For charity."

"You agreed," Lenny said, "to sit in the dark with a woman who knows about the trunk."

Archie looked out at the hall. Behind him the painted harbor hung right side up again, its gulls back in the sky where they belonged. It didn't look like a place where anything could happen anymore.

"Everybody in Merrow probably knows about the trunk," he told the hall. "It's a small town."

Lenny finished coiling the rope and hung it on its cleat. "Then why'd you stop?"

Archie didn't answer. He picked the straitjacket up off the boards. His hands weren't entirely steady, and he wanted very much to go home and try one more word on a black steamer trunk with gold stars on it.
