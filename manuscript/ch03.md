# Chapter 3

## The Liars' Table

The Galley opened at five, and by six-thirty on Friday morning every stool at the counter was taken and Archie Fairweather had been ignored by the same three lobstermen for the fifty-ninth day in a row.

He kept count. He had a system.

The diner sat at the head of the town wharf, a low gray-shingled building with a long counter, six booths along the harbor windows, a pie case lit like a jewelry store, and a coffee urn the size of a lobster pot. Outside, the sky was still dark, going to pewter at the edge. Inside, it was warm and loud and smelled of bacon and wet wool. A bell over the door rang every time someone came in out of the cold, and every time it rang, half the room looked up to see who it was and the other half didn't need to.

The round table by the kitchen door was the liars' table. It belonged to three men in their seventies who had fished out of Merrow since before Archie was born — Elwin Coombs, Dickie Thibodeau, and a narrow, silent man everybody called Pickle for reasons nobody would tell him. They sat there from five until the light came up, drinking coffee and saying things about the weather that Archie had come to believe were in code.

"Morning, gentlemen," Archie said, passing them with his mug.

Elwin looked at the window. Dickie looked at his eggs. Pickle looked at nothing, with great concentration.

Fifty-nine.

"Sit down," said Bev Thibodeau from behind the counter. "You're letting the heat out just standing there."

Bev owned the Galley. She was sixty-seven, with forearms like a stevedore, reading glasses pushed up into gray hair, and a flat Down East voice that could make *good morning* sound like a ruling from the bench. In nine weeks, Archie had tried flattery on her, and generosity, and card tricks, and a sincere and detailed appreciation of her blueberry pie. She had accepted the money for the pie.

He took the last stool, between Chief Dale Pinkham and the register.

"Archie," said the chief, glowing. "Heard about yesterday."

Dale Pinkham was the entire Merrow Police Department, if you didn't count a part-timer and a young officer named Kayla, and most people in Merrow didn't. He was sixty-three, with a walrus mustache and a uniform jacket that had stopped closing around the middle sometime during the Obama administration. He owned every one of Archie's television specials on DVD. He had opinions about the riverboat.

"Two million dollars. Upside down."

"It was mostly upside down."

"Ayuh. That's what I heard." He leaned in. "So how does she do it?"

It wasn't only the chief asking. Archie felt it move down the counter and through the booths — a little lift of heads, a slowing of forks. For two months Merrow had been polite to him: carefully, and from a distance. Now the whole room wanted something he had.

"Bev," he said. "I'm going to need your hand."

"You are not."

"Thirty seconds. For science."

Bev looked at him over her glasses. Then she wiped her hands on her apron, came around the end of the counter, and put out her right hand like a woman offering it to be fingerprinted. A small cheer went up from the second booth.

"Chief, your left. Now — at a séance everybody holds hands, in a circle. That's how you know the medium can't be ringing bells, because both her hands are being held." Archie took Bev's hand in his left and the chief's in his right. "Close your eyes. Both of you."

The chief closed his eyes immediately. Bev closed hers, eventually.

"Now. In the dark, the medium says the spirits are restless and the circle needs to tighten up. So she shifts her hands a little —" he brought them closer, murmuring, guiding Bev's fingers, adjusting the chief's wrist "— there, that's better, everybody comfortable — and you both feel a hand, and you both think it's mine."

He took his right hand away. Neither of them moved.

Then, with that free hand, he picked up a cold spoon off the counter and laid it against the back of the chief's neck.

Dale Pinkham shot about four inches off his stool and opened his eyes. So did Bev. They looked down and discovered they were holding each other's hands, and that Archie was waving the spoon.

The Galley exploded. Even Dickie Thibodeau, at the liars' table, made a sound that might have been a cough.

Bev took her hand back from the chief and looked at it.

"Well," she said, "that's disgusting," and went back behind the counter.

"The hand release," said Archie. "Every medium since 1850. Half the time the person next to her is in on it. The bells are on a stick. The cold touches are a wet glove. The voices —"

"What about the trumpet?" someone called from the booths. "She does a trumpet on her show. It floats around and the dead people talk out of it."

"Page one-twelve," said a voice from the corner booth.

Hollis Tibbetts sat alone by the window with the *Merrow Packet* folded to the crossword and her reading glasses on the end of her nose. She didn't look up.

Before Archie could open his mouth, three people in three different booths said, in ragged unison, "*Lips to the small end!*"

The room roared. Archie looked around, lost.

"Mrs. Tibbetts's séance unit," explained the woman in the second booth, wiping her eyes. "Senior English. Everybody had to do it. Gus's book, Houdini, the Fox sisters—"

"'How the Grieving Are Fooled,'" said a young man at the counter, in a fair imitation of a schoolteacher. "Five-page paper. Typed."

"Double-spaced," said Hollis, filling in a word. "And you got a C-plus, Corey, because you spelled 'medium' with two *e*'s."

"Lips right on it?" said Chief Pinkham, who had evidently gone to school before the unit existed. "In the dark? That's not sanitary."

That got the biggest laugh of the morning, and Archie let the chief have it.

The bell over the door rang. A gust of cold came in, and with it a man in a camel overcoat and a red bow tie, holding a leather portfolio under one arm.

Warren Gilley was sixty-one, chair of the select board and the third Gilley to run Gilley Insurance & Realty on Main Street, with a tidy gray haircut, a tidy gray mustache, and the look of a man who had walked in on a party he wouldn't have attended. In September he had wanted the town to sell its crumbling opera house to a Boston developer for condominiums. Archie had offered more money and a deed restriction keeping it a theater forever, the special town meeting had voted 312 to 41, and Warren had not forgiven him.

"Coffee, Bev. To go." Warren set his portfolio on the counter and looked down it at Archie. "I gather you've been entertaining."

"Educating."

"Mm." Warren accepted his coffee. "I'll be sitting in that circle on Saturday as the town's representative, so I'll say this once, in front of witnesses. That sale doesn't close until the fifteenth of November. Until it does, the opera house belongs to Merrow, and the select board will take a dim view of this town being made a laughingstock on national television." He paused. "By anyone."

"Warren," said Archie pleasantly, "the town hired her. I'm just the fella who's going to catch her."

"I'm sure that's how it seems from where you're standing."

"What's she gonna do, Warren?" Elwin Coombs said suddenly, from the liars' table — the first words Archie had ever heard him address to anyone outside it. "Bring back some drowned fella to tell us the price of bait?"

The room laughed again, and Warren Gilley's ears went pink. He picked up his portfolio and his coffee and left, and the bell rang behind him, and somebody in the booths said *Ayuh, there he goes*, and Archie thought: *I won that.* He felt good about it for nearly three seconds.

Then he saw the waitress.

She was at the end of the counter with the coffee pot in her hand — a woman in her thirties with red-gold hair pulled back in a ponytail and a Galley apron over a gray thermal shirt. Her name was Shannon; he knew that much. She'd poured his coffee every morning since August and called him *hon*, the same as everybody. She had a wedding ring on, and she had stopped pouring with the pot held out over an empty mug, and her face had gone still.

She put the pot down on the warmer. She took off her apron and hung it on its hook. Then she went out through the kitchen, and Archie heard the back door bang.

Bev was watching the kitchen door. She looked at Archie, once, and didn't say anything, which was worse than anything she could have said.

He left a twenty on the counter and went after her.

---

The Galley's back door opened onto the wharf itself: gray planks, lobster crates stacked six high, coils of pot warp, the smell of bait. The sky over the harbor had gone from pewter to a thin cold pink, and the moorings were full of lobster boats swinging on their lines, their names on their sterns — *Second Wind*, *Miss Dottie*, *Three Daughters*. A gull stood on a piling and screamed at nothing.

Shannon had walked all the way to the end of the wharf, where it stopped above the water. There was a block of granite there, waist-high and the size of a bank safe, with a bronze plaque on top — MERROW REMEMBERS — and on its seaward face, row after row of names cut into the stone. Archie had walked past it a dozen times and never read it.

He stopped now, a few feet behind her, and read it.

The oldest names, at the top, were weathered almost smooth; one line near the top was a whole family. Farther down the letters were sharper. *Arthur Coombs, 1978. Robert and James Haskell, 1991. Peter Langley, 2003. Dennis Pinkham, 2012.* And at the bottom, alone, a name cut so recently that the granite inside the letters was still pale and new, like a scrape on a knee.

*THOMAS MAYO.*

"February," Shannon said, without turning around. "Off Monhegan. The boat went down in about four minutes. That's what the Coast Guard said. About four minutes." She had her arms wrapped around herself. "Tommy. He was thirty-five. We've got two kids."

"I'm sorry," Archie said. "I didn't know."

"Everybody knows. Everybody in there knows." She jerked her head back at the Galley. "Elwin went to the service."

Archie took off his coat, the red-and-black buffalo-check wool he'd bought in Camden on his first day in Maine, and held it out. After a second she took it and put it around her shoulders without looking at him.

"I'm in the circle," she said. "Saturday."

He'd guessed. It landed anyway.

"Her people messaged me in the summer. On Facebook. They said Lorelei had felt something — a strong presence near the water, a man who wanted to reach his wife — and would I like a free reading. On the phone." She laughed, without any humor in it. "I almost deleted it. I thought it was a scam."

"Shannon—"

"He came through." She turned around then, and her eyes were red and dry and furious. "She knew things. About Tommy. About the boat, and the kids, and what he called me. She knew he hated the cold. He *said* things, through her. He told me he wasn't cold anymore." Her voice cracked on it, and she pushed straight through the crack. "I've had three readings. The first one was free. The others were three-fifty. I borrowed it against the insurance. And she said on Saturday, in the theater, with all of you there, there'd be something special. For me. From him."

Archie had spent thirty years explaining how this worked. A researcher, an obituary, a widow's public posts, the funeral notice in the *Packet*. Free the first time, because the first time was the hook. He could have drawn her a diagram on a napkin.

He didn't say any of it.

"So you all go on in there," Shannon said, "and laugh about drowned fellas and the price of bait. You hang upside down and bet your millions. But don't you *dare* make fun of it. Not to me. He talks to me. You don't get to take that away just because you know how to do card tricks."

The gull screamed again.

"I won't make fun of you," Archie said. "I promise."

It came out plainer than he meant it to. There was no patter in it. Shannon looked at him as though she were checking it for a trick, and he let her look.

Then she took off his coat, handed it back, and walked past him toward the Galley. She had six booths and a counter full of people waiting for coffee, and she was going to go and pour it.

Archie stood alone at the end of the wharf with the cold coming straight through his shirt and read the bottom name on the granite again. A free reading. Three hundred and fifty dollars, borrowed against a dead man's insurance.

Yesterday the séance had been a dare. He put his coat on, still warm from her shoulders, and went back up the wharf.
