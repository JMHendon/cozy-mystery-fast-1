# Chapter 15

## Hauling

At five-thirty on Monday morning the town float was black and slick with frost, and Archie Fairweather stood on it in his buffalo-check coat with a white plastic bucket in both arms, so happy he could barely keep still.

It was cold — real cold, the first of the year, the kind that got into the bones of your face. The harbor was flat and dark and steaming faintly. Out past the moorings the bell buoy rang once, slow, and out on the point Merrow Light swung its green flash over the water every six seconds. Up at the head of the wharf the windows of the Galley were yellow, and in one of them, by the kitchen door, three shapes sat around a round table. One of them was looking down at the float. When Archie raised his free hand, it looked away.

Sixty-two.

"You brought it," said Dale Pinkham.

"I said I would."

"Ayuh. You did." The chief came down the ramp in orange oilskin bibs and a hooded sweatshirt that said KNOX COUNTY SHERIFF'S OFFICE SOFTBALL 2004, carrying a thermos and two pairs of rubber gloves. He peered into the bucket. Harold peered back.

"Gramp, we're losing the tide," said a voice from the boat.

*Second Wind* lay against the float with her engine already rumbling, a white lobster boat with a square wheelhouse and a work deck crowded with gear: the hydraulic hauler at the starboard rail, a long wooden bait box, a seawater tank. A girl in orange bibs was standing on the rail with a line in her hand. She was sixteen, broad-shouldered, with a round freckled face and a fuzzy black hat pulled down to her eyebrows, and she looked at Archie and his bucket with the open, cheerful contempt of the young for the useless.

"You're June's dad," she said.

"I am."

"June says you can't drive stick."

"June is a gifted researcher. Shouldn't you be in school?"

"Teacher workshop." Mattie looked at the bucket. "June's asleep. June said you'd be late."

"Permission to come aboard?"

"Don't stand in the rope," said Mattie Pinkham, and jumped down onto the deck.

---

They went out through the moorings in the dark, past *Miss Dottie* and *Three Daughters*, with their running lights on and the diesel thumping under Archie's feet. The bait box held a hundred pounds of salted herring and was the worst thing Archie had ever smelled. He stood in the stern with Harold's bucket against his chest and watched the steeple and the opera house bell tower go black against a sky that was beginning, along the edge, to go gray.

Dale throttled down off the end of Shag Ledge, inside the harbor, where the water was still flat.

"Here's good," he said. "Deep enough. Rocky bottom. He'll be all right here."

Archie set the bucket on the rail and lifted Harold out. Harold raised both claws, which were no longer banded — Archie had cut the bands on Friday because it seemed rude — and waved them.

"Hold on," said Dale. "Measure him first."

"Dale, he's from the co-op. He's legal. I paid for him."

"Measure him anyhow." Dale held out a brass gauge, worn bright at the edges. "You don't put a lobster over the side of my boat without you measure him. Them's the rules."

Archie measured him, eye socket to the back of the shell. Legal. Dale nodded, satisfied, as if a small piece of the world had been put back where it belonged.

"Give him here a second," said Mattie. She took Harold from Archie with one hand, flipped him over with total efficiency, and looked at his underside. "Huh," she said. "Harold's a girl."

Archie stared at her.

"Swimmerets." Mattie pointed. "See, soft. That's a female. You named a girl Harold."

"She never said a word," said Archie. "Not one word in four days. She let me go on calling her Harold, and she never once corrected me." He took the lobster back and held her up at eye level in the gray light. "Harold. You absolute professional."

Mattie laughed out loud, a big unguarded honk that went off across the water. Even Dale's mustache moved.

Archie leaned over the rail and lowered Harold into the harbor. She hung for a second in the green-black water just under the surface, claws spread, and then she tipped forward and backed away down into the dark, tail flicking, and was gone.

He stayed leaning over the rail a moment longer than he needed to.

"Okay," said Mattie. "Can we *work* now?"

---

They worked.

It was a rhythm, and he saw it at once, because rhythm was the one thing in the world he understood completely. Dale brought the boat alongside a buoy — white with a green band, his colors — and Mattie gaffed the line and threw it over the hauler's sheave, and the hauler whined, and the line came up dripping and coiled itself on the deck, and then the trap broke the surface with water pouring out of it and Mattie swung it onto the rail. Open the door. Pick the lobsters. Measure. Throw back the shorts. Band the keepers. Bait bag out, bait bag in. Shut the door. Push it over. Next.

"Let me do one," said Archie.

Mattie looked at her grandfather. Dale looked at the sky, going pink now behind Merrow Light, and at the long string of buoys ahead of them. Three hundred traps to get up before the weather. He looked at Archie's face, and sighed.

"One," he said. "Mattie, stand by him. Archie, stay out of the bight."

"What's the bight?"

"The loop in the rope on the deck. When the trap goes over, the line goes with it. Your foot's in the bight, you go with it." Dale said it without any drama at all. "Fella over in Tenants Harbor went with it in 2011."

Archie stepped very carefully out of the bight.

He did one. He did it well. The trap came up streaming and he swung it onto the rail, and there were four lobsters inside and a crab and a sculpin, and he had his hands on them before Mattie could say anything — the gauge, short, over it goes; the gauge, keeper; the band snapping onto the claw clean on the first try. He threw the sculpin back with an apology. He stuffed a new bait bag with herring, which was cold and slippery and unspeakable, and shut the door, and slid the trap over, and kept his feet out of the bight while the line went whipping after it.

He straightened up, breathing hard, with herring scales on his coat.

Mattie looked at the two banded lobsters in the tank. She looked at him.

"Not bad," she said, as though it had been taken out of her with pliers.

Archie was so pleased he could have sung. It lasted until they cleared the ledge.

Dale opened the throttle and took them out past the end of the ledge, where the harbor stopped being a harbor. The swell came in under the boat — long and slow and gray, nothing at all, Dale said afterward; you'd hardly call it a sea. *Second Wind* lifted her bow and lay over a little and came back, and lifted, and lay over, and came back. The diesel fumes rolled back over the stern. The bait box sat in the middle of the deck and breathed.

Archie had escaped from a safe at the bottom of Lake Tahoe. He had held his breath for four minutes in a water tank on live television.

He lasted about six minutes.

He made it to the lee rail, which was the one dignity he was afforded, and hung over it and was sick into the Gulf of Maine, comprehensively, while the gulls, who knew a good thing, came in low over the wake.

"Gramp," said Mattie, with enormous satisfaction. "He's chumming."

"Leave him be."

The radio in the wheelhouse crackled. A voice came out of it, flat and amused, from a boat Archie could see a quarter mile off to the east, a green hull with its hauler going.

"*Second Wind, Second Wind. That your magician, Dale?*"

Dale picked up the handset. "Ayuh."

"*He all right?*"

"He's fine."

"*Looks like he's making something disappear.*"

There was laughter on the radio — not just from the green boat. Somebody else on the channel, and somebody else. The whole working harbor, Archie understood, hanging over the rail with his eyes streaming, was on that channel at this hour. The whole working harbor had just watched him do it.

He was sick again.

---

Dale took pity on him after a while and throttled back and let the boat lie quieter in the lee of Shag Ledge, close to the bell buoy, while Mattie baited bags. Archie sat on an upturned bucket in the stern with his back against the traps and a cup of coffee from Dale's thermos in both hands, and the sun came up red over the islands to the east, and slowly, very slowly, the sea agreed to stop moving quite so much.

The bell buoy was thirty yards off — a big red iron cage on a float, with four clappers hung round a bell, rolling with the swell. Up close it wasn't a sound so much as a feeling: a slow, iron, irregular tolling that came across the water and into the deck and up through the soles of his boots.

"Right about here," Dale said. He was standing in the wheelhouse door with his own coffee, looking at the buoy. "Where they found the skiff. Eighty-eight. I was on my father's boat, that morning. Everybody went out. Every boat in the harbor. Coast Guard come up from Rockland." He tipped his cup toward the point. "She was drifting down toward the Light on the ebb. Oars shipped. Nobody in her. His cloak folded on the thwart, neat as you please, like he'd just stepped out for a minute."

Archie looked at the buoy. The bell tolled, and tolled.

"I was there that night," he said. "At the show. I was ten. I was in the second row."

Dale didn't say anything. He nodded slowly, and kept looking at the buoy, and after a while Mattie stopped baiting bags and looked at it too.

Dale's phone rang in the pocket of his bibs.

He looked at the screen and his face changed, just slightly — the face of a man seeing a number he'd been waiting for. He stepped into the wheelhouse to take it and pulled the door to behind him. Archie could see him through the salt-streaked window: the phone at his ear, his free hand flat on the wheel, nodding. Saying *ayuh* and *ayuh* and *what time*. Not looking at the stern.

He was in there a long time.

When he came out he stood in the wheelhouse door with the phone in his hand. He looked at Mattie and said, "Go mind the wheel a minute, Matts," and she looked at his face and went into the wheelhouse without arguing, which Archie gathered was rare. Then Dale came and sat down on the bait box beside Archie's bucket, heavily, and put his elbows on his knees.

"I'm not supposed to tell you this," he said.

"Then don't, Dale. I mean it. I don't want to get you—"

"That was the sergeant." Dale looked at his big hands. "The blood came back. From the hospital. It's what you said. Aconite." He said the word carefully, the way he'd say the name of a town he'd never been to. "They've opened it up. It's a homicide now. State Police, the AG's office, all of it."

The bell buoy tolled. Somewhere off to the east, the green boat's hauler whined.

"I shouldn't have told you," said Dale.

Archie looked down into his coffee. "Thank you."

"Lieutenant Brannock's down from Augusta. He's setting up in the select board room. He wants you in at two o'clock this afternoon." Dale turned his head and looked at Archie properly for the first time since the phone rang. "Bring a lawyer."

"I don't need a lawyer, Dale. I haven't done anything. I'll go in and explain about the trumpet and—"

"Ayuh," said Dale Pinkham. He stood up off the bait box, and put his hand on Archie's shoulder, once, and went back toward the wheel. "Bring one anyhow."
