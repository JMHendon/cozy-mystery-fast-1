# Chapter 7

## Controls

"The prop-room door was unlocked this morning," said Hollis Tibbetts, by way of hello.

It was a quarter to seven on Saturday night, and she was standing at the top of the opera house stairs with her ring of keys in one fingerless-gloved hand and a clipboard in the other.

"Unlocked how?"

"Unlocked the way a door is when somebody doesn't lock it." She lowered her voice, which for Hollis meant that only half the hall could hear. "I lent Warren my key yesterday afternoon. He wanted to do the inventory for the sale — the posters and the old fixtures and so on, for the closing. He swears up and down he locked it after." She looked at Archie over her glasses. "Nothing's missing that I can see. But I can't see everything."

"Did you tell anyone?"

"I'm telling you." She checked something on the clipboard. "Your mother's in the green room terrorizing the makeup girl. Your widow is in the front row looking like she'd rather be anywhere else. And there's a young man in a sweatshirt with his own face on it asking for you."

She went off down the aisle toward the stage, keys jingling, before he could ask anything else.

Archie stood at the top of the stairs and looked at his theater.

It wasn't his theater anymore — not tonight. In two days Marchand Media had turned the plain, cold, high hall into something out of a dream about a funeral. Black drapes hung over every one of the tall arched windows except the last one at the stage-left end, left bare so the camera could catch the harbor lights. The folding chairs were stacked against the walls. Cables ran everywhere in taped black rivers. Up on the stage, in the middle of all that bare dark wood, under a single soft work light, stood a round oak table with seven chairs pulled up to it, and on the table, laid out like the place settings for a very strange dinner, were a brass hand bell, a tambourine, a child's chalk slate in a wooden frame, and a long cone of dull silver aluminum with three bands of pale green paint around it.

The trumpet.

Two cameras on tripods faced the table from either side of the stage, each with a fat black lens and a ring of small dark lights around it — infrared, for the dark. Their cables ran off to the stage-right wing, where Teddy Marchand sat in a folding chair in front of a monitor, a headset around his neck and a phone in each hand. A third camera — a handheld, carried by a broad, quiet young man in a black watch cap and black gloves — was drifting around the edge of the stage, filming the candles that hadn't been lit yet and the painted harbor curtain and the empty chairs.

It was cold. The radiators were knocking, doing their best. Archie could see his breath, faintly, in the work light.

"Mr. Fairweather!"

The young man in the sweatshirt was coming up the aisle at something close to a run. He was about thirty, round-faced, with a scruff of beard, a lanyard that said SITTER — CONTEST WINNER, and a black hoodie that said SPECTRAL KEV across the chest in dripping green letters over a cartoon of his own face. He was holding an orange plastic gadget with a row of little lights on it.

"Kev Duchesne," he said, breathless. "Haverhill, Mass. I'm the contest. I've seen every one of your specials. And every one of *hers*, which is sort of the problem—" He held out the orange gadget. "Would you sign my EMF meter?"

Archie looked at it. "What does it do?"

"It detects fluctuations in the electromagnetic field associated with spirit activity."

"It detects the wiring in the walls, Kev."

"I know," said Kev miserably. "That's why I need you to sign it. Then at least it's worth something."

Archie signed it. Kev looked at the signature with his mouth open, and then over at the stage, where Lorelei Crane had just come up the stairs from below in a long black velvet dress, her white streak gleaming, her rings catching the light — and his face did something complicated and torn.

"She's signing it too," he whispered. "After."

"Kev," said Archie, "you're going to have a very confusing night."

---

At seven-thirty, the cameras rolled.

That was what Teddy called it — *we're rolling on controls, people!* — and the hall went quiet, and Archie Fairweather walked out into the work light at the center of the stage in a black shirt and black trousers, because a medium had told him white shirts flared, and he hated that she was right.

"Ladies and gentlemen," he said to the camera, to the empty chairs, to the knot of sitters by the stage steps, "nothing up my sleeves."

He took the sleeves of his shirt and pushed them up to the elbow. It got a laugh from Vivienne, who had come out of the green room in black velvet and pearls looking like the widow of a minor Romanov, and a nervous noise from Kev.

Then he went to work.

He did it the way Gus had taught him: slowly, out loud, touching everything. He looked under the table. He rapped the tabletop all the way around, listening for hollow places. He turned each chair over. He rang the bell and shook it, and showed both sides of the tambourine and the slate to the camera.

Then he picked up the trumpet.

It was lighter than it looked, about two feet long, aluminum, flaring at one end to the width of a dinner plate and narrowing at the other to the size of a thumb. He held the wide end up to the work light and looked through it, like a man looking for stars through a telescope. He turned it over and tapped it. He held the narrow end to his ear.

"Hello?" he said into it. "Anybody home?"

Then, because it was too good to resist, and because Lenny wasn't there to stop him, he put the small end to his own lips and blew the loudest, wettest, most disrespectful raspberry ever heard on the stage of the Merrow Opera House.

*PPPBBBTHHHHT.*

Kev laughed out loud and clapped his hand over his mouth. Somebody in the crew snorted. In the wing, Teddy Marchand gave him two thumbs up without looking away from his monitor.

"No spirits in here, folks," said Archie, and set the trumpet back down in the center of the table, exactly where it had been.

Lorelei Crane was watching him from the edge of the light with her arms folded and a look of deep, fond contempt.

"Are you finished, honey?"

"Not quite." He turned to her. "I'd like you searched, please. Pockets, sleeves, the hem of that very beautiful dress. A woman of your choosing — Hollis, or my mother, or anybody on your own crew. On camera."

"No."

"Then I'd like to sit beside you."

"No." She smiled. "Your energy's hostile. The spirits won't come near it. You'll sit across the table where I can see you."

"Then I'd like to choose who does."

That made her pause. It was a small pause; he'd been watching her for two days and he saw it.

"My daughter sits on my left," Lorelei said. "She always sits on my left. She's my anchor. That's not up for discussion."

"Then I choose your right." Archie looked past her, to the front row of the stacked chairs, where Shannon Mayo was sitting very upright in a good gray sweater with her red-gold hair down for once, twisting her wedding ring around and around her finger. "Mrs. Mayo. If she's willing."

Shannon looked up, startled. She looked at Lorelei. Lorelei looked at her, and something passed across the medium's face — calculation, or kindness, or both — and then she held out one ringed hand.

"Of course," she said warmly. "Shannon, honey. Come sit by me. Tommy would like that."

Shannon came up the steps like a woman walking onto ice. As she passed Archie, she said, very low, without turning her head: "You picked me to catch her."

"I picked you because you'll tell the truth about what you feel," he said. "Whatever it is."

She didn't answer. She sat down at Lorelei's right.

The rest of them took their places for the camera's sake, to be filmed in the light before it went dark: Ivy Crane at her mother's left, in the gray sweater and the silk scarf, expressionless; Warren Gilley beside her, in a bow tie, sweating gently in the cold; then Vivienne; then the empty chair across from Lorelei, which was Archie's; then Kev, with his orange meter in his lap; then Shannon. Seven.

Lorelei leaned across Ivy, as everyone was settling, and murmured something to Warren — something short, with her hand on his sleeve and a confiding smile.

Warren Gilley went the color of skim milk.

Archie saw it. He saw Warren pull his sleeve back and look straight ahead at nothing, and he filed it, and then he forgot about it, because it was seven-forty and he had one more thing to do on camera.

He'd brought it up from the Galley at six: Bev Thibodeau's big dented steel thermos, with masking tape on the side that said BEV — RETURN IT, and two cups nested on the top. He unscrewed the cups, filled both with chamomile tea that steamed in the cold air, and held one out across the table to Lorelei Crane.

"A truce," he said. "For the cameras. Whatever happens tonight, it's been a pleasure."

Ivy's hand came out flat over her mother's place before Lorelei could reach. "She doesn't drink anything I don't pour."

"Oh, for heaven's sake, baby." Lorelei laughed, and moved her daughter's hand aside, and took the cup. "It's chamomile. From the diner. If the Great Fairweather's grandson wants to poison me on network television, I think he's earned it." She raised the cup to him. "To the vanishing man."

"To the other side," said Archie.

They drank. Archie drank all of his; it was hot and grassy and Bev made it too strong, the way she made everything. Lorelei drank half of hers and set it down.

"Cut controls!" called Teddy. "Beautiful. Beautiful. Fifteen minutes, everyone. Lorelei, sweetheart, makeup."

The work light went down. The hall went dim. Lorelei rose with a rustle of velvet and went off toward the stairs to the green room, and Ivy rose with her, carrying her mother's water bottle and a little tin of throat lozenges, and they disappeared below the stage together.

Archie screwed the cups back onto Bev's thermos, set it down by the wall, and went to the back of the hall, because he needed, for a few minutes, to not be looking at the trumpet.

Chief Dale Pinkham was standing by the door to the stairs in his uniform jacket, which didn't close, with his hands in his pockets and the expression of a man who had been told he could stay if he was quiet and intended to be very quiet indeed.

"Archie," he said reverently. "Can I ask you something?"

"Anything, Chief."

"The riverboat."

So they talked about the riverboat. Dale Pinkham had a theory that involved a second, identical riverboat and the cooperation of the Army Corps of Engineers, and it was wrong in every particular and so lovingly reasoned that Archie couldn't bring himself to say so. While they talked, he watched the stage from a long way off.

Things were happening up there in the dimness, the way they always do in the last quarter hour before a show. Warren Gilley got up from his place, said something to nobody, and went past Archie and the chief and down the stairs to the men's room, very fast, holding his stomach. "Nerves," said the chief, with sympathy. Kev Duchesne had his phone out and was taking pictures of the table, until Teddy's voice came out of the wing, sharp — *Phones away, please, sir, that's in your contract* — and Kev jumped and put the phone in his pocket and went to stand by the radiator, looking guilty.

Hollis was up there in a pair of black stage gloves, the kind the crew all wore, lighting the candles — the town's open-flame permit was in her name and folded in her cardigan pocket, and she'd told Teddy on Thursday that nobody else was striking a match on her stage. There was a tall white pillar candle in the center of the table and smaller ones around the edge. Then she went around the circle with a tray, setting a glass of water at every place. Vivienne was at her chair, arranging a black shawl over the back of it with great care, as though the spirits might judge her on it. Shannon was sitting at her place already, alone in the candlelight, turning her ring. The cameraman swung away toward the one bare window, where the lights of the moorings were coming on out in the dark harbor.

"Hollis," came Teddy's voice from the wing, "sorry — can you slide the horn left, it's fighting the candle."

Hollis slid the trumpet a few inches left, away from the tall candle, and went off into the wing with her empty tray. The cameraman swung back to the table.

At seven fifty-five, Archie left the chief to his riverboat and went up to his mother.

"You don't have to do this," he said quietly.

Vivienne looked up at him from her chair. In the candlelight, in her black velvet and pearls, she looked very beautiful and suddenly very old.

"Neither do you, darling," she said.

He sat down beside her. After a moment she put her hand over his on the table, and he turned his hand over and held hers.

At seven fifty-eight, Lorelei Crane came back up the stairs from the green room with her daughter behind her. She'd redone her mouth; it was darker and glossier than before, almost black in the candlelight. She smelled of menthol. She took her place at the head of the table, upstage, facing the empty dark of the hall and the little glowing window of the light booth high at the back, and she smiled around the circle at each of them in turn.

"Hands, please, everybody," she said softly. "Left hand under, right hand over. Don't let go, no matter what you feel. The spirits don't like a broken circle."

Archie took his mother's hand on one side. On the other, he took Kev's, which was damp and trembling, and which had, a moment before, been fumbling with something in the pouch pocket of his hoodie. Across the table Shannon put her hand in Lorelei's like someone putting her hand into a fire. Ivy took her mother's left without looking at her.

"Thank you, Hollis," Lorelei called into the dark. "Whenever you're ready, my dear."

High at the back of the hall, in the booth, a small shape lifted a hand.

One by one the candles were pinched out, until there was only a single dim red bulb glowing on a stand beside the table, like the light of a darkroom.

Then the house lights went out.
