# Chapter 42

## Keys

There was a state trooper on a chair outside the door of room 214, reading a paperback, and he stood up when he saw Tess and looked at Archie for slightly too long, the way people did.

"Twenty minutes," Tess said to Archie. "I'll be right here." She sat down in the trooper's chair. "And if her lawyer comes up those stairs, I wasn't."

The room was small and warm and very bright. It had a window over the parking lot and, beyond the parking lot, a long gray stripe of Penobscot Bay going silver in the early afternoon. Hollis was sitting up in the bed in a hospital gown and somebody's blue cardigan, with an IV in the back of one hand and her reading glasses on their beaded chain around her neck. Somebody had brought her the glasses. Somebody had brushed her hair.

She looked at him over the top of the glasses as he came in.

"Sit up straight," she said. "You look like you were dragged through a hedge backward."

"I was dragged through a harbor."

"That's no excuse for slouching." She pointed at the chair by the bed with the hand that didn't have the IV in it. "Sit. You've got a fever. I can see it from here."

He sat.

For a moment neither of them said anything. Out in the hall, someone's cart rattled past. The heating ticked.

"How's June?" said Hollis.

"Mad at me."

"Good. She should be." Hollis nodded, satisfied. "Was she good? Last night? Before you came up through the floor like a drowned rat?"

"She was wonderful."

"I knew she would be. She has your hands and her own head." Hollis looked out the window at the bay. "I'd have liked to see it."

He didn't know what to say to that, so he didn't say anything.

"You want to know," said Hollis. "Go on. Ask me. I've told Tess and a man from the attorney general's office and a lawyer who says I'm making a terrible mistake, and I'll tell you, too, and then I'm done telling it."

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't." She folded her hands on the blanket, IV line and all. "That's why I'm going to."

She told him.

She told it plainly, the way she'd taught a thousand teenagers to tell a thing: beginning, middle, end, no adjectives you couldn't defend. Danny at the quarry, the summer he was fifteen, on a hot afternoon in August, showing off for a girl. Peter after — how he'd stopped talking, and stopped eating, and then one day in October started going to Rockland on Thursday nights and wouldn't say why, and came back lighter, and she'd been so grateful for the lightness that she hadn't asked. The notebook she found after he drowned, in his oilskin pocket in the shed: forty-one Thursdays in Peter's careful lobsterman's printing, every word the trumpet had said in their son's voice. *Dad, it's warm here. The water's warm. I'm not cold anymore. Come see me on the water.* The second mortgage. The flyer from the Grange with the woman's face on it.

"I took it all to Walt Calloway," Hollis said. "He was so kind. He sat with me for two hours. He called Augusta in front of me. There wasn't anything anybody could do." She looked at her hands. "So I went to the library."

Gus's book. Page one-twelve. She'd sat in the reading room at the long oak table and read how a woman in the dark with one hand free could lift a tin horn to her lips and become your dead child, and she had understood exactly how it had been done to her husband, forty-one times, and she had put her head down on the table and cried until Deb Coombs came out from behind the desk and put a hand on her back.

"After that I taught it," she said. "For sixteen years. Every senior who came through my room. The Fox sisters and Houdini and Gus and page one-twelve. *How the Grieving Are Fooled*, five pages, typed." She almost smiled. "I thought if I put it in enough heads, it would never happen in Merrow again. Nobody ever asked me why I'd added it. They just thought I liked ghost stories."

Then the woman on the television, eleven years of her. Then Tommy Mayo, in February. Then Shannon, in April, at Hollis's kitchen table, telling her about a message on Facebook.

"I wrote to that producer to *expose* her," Hollis said. "I want you to know that. In May I didn't mean anything else. I was going to stand up in Gus's theater on her own camera with Peter's notebook in my hand and read it out. Every word. I'd have done it, too. And then you came to town, and I thought — oh, *better*. Archie Fairweather. He'll catch her red-handed in front of the whole country, and I'll read the notebook after, and that will be that."

"What changed?"

"Shannon came for coffee. In September." Hollis's voice didn't change at all. "She'd had another reading. She sat at my kitchen table and told me, so happy, that Tommy had said he missed her on the water." She looked at Archie. "*Missed her on the water.* That's how it starts. That's exactly how it started with Peter. And I gave her the speech, Archie. I gave her the whole unit. I gave her Gus's book. She said, *Mrs. Tibbetts, I know it's probably not real, but it's the only time I get to hear his voice.* And she put Gus's book on her shelf and paid that woman another three hundred and fifty dollars." Hollis looked out at the bay. "You can't debunk a widow. I'd been trying for twenty years. You can't. Exposing her wouldn't have stopped Shannon. It wouldn't have stopped Peter. It would only have made her famous for a week, and then she'd have been back on the telephone."

"So you dug up the garden."

"So I dug up the garden." Hollis nodded. "I thought it would look like her heart. That's what it does, you know — it stops your heart. A woman her age, in the dark, at a séance. Her fans would have said the spirits took her. Some of them still will." She looked at him. "I didn't think anybody in Merrow would know what a numb mouth meant. And then on Sunday morning I was unlocking the radiators and I heard Tess say *wolfsbane* to you, and I went down to the green room and was sick in the sink."

"And Ivy?"

Hollis's face changed then, for the first time.

"I told myself they'd let her go," she said. "On Tuesday. I told myself it was the scarf — tampering, a fine, a night in jail, she'd be back in California by the weekend. I told myself that for two days. And then on Thursday they said *murder*, and I came home from your rehearsal and sat down at my kitchen table and wrote it all out, and sealed it, and I've never in my life been so ashamed of anything as I was of those two days." She was quiet a moment. "Tell her that. Will you? I'd like her to hear it from somebody."

"I'll tell her."

The heating ticked. Out on the bay, a lobster boat was coming in, very small, leaving a white line behind it on the gray.

"Would you do it again?" Archie said.

Hollis didn't answer for a long time.

"I'd like to tell you I wouldn't," she said finally. "I'm a schoolteacher. I know what the right answer is. I've graded it." She turned her head on the pillow and looked at him over her glasses. "Ask me in a year."

He nodded.

"I watched you do it," he said. "That's the thing I can't get past. On Saturday night I was standing at the back of the hall with Dale, talking about a riverboat, and I watched you walk around that table with a tray, and set out the glasses, and slide the horn when Teddy asked you to. I *watched* you. I was looking right at you, Hollis. It was the best trick I've ever seen in my life, and I never saw it."

"Nobody looks at the stagehand, dear," said Hollis. "Gus taught me that. In that very building. I was twenty-nine." She smiled a little, tiredly. "And everybody said nobody fools Archie Fairweather. It was on the posters for your challenge. I used to like that."

"Well," said Archie. "You did."

"I did." She didn't sound proud. She didn't sound sorry, either. She sounded like a woman stating the date. "I'd rather it had been anybody else."

She reached over with the hand that had the IV in it, slowly, and patted his wrist, twice.

"Now listen," she said, in a different voice — the clipboard voice, the general's. "I have three things for you, and then you're going to go home and take something for that fever. One. The Players. Vivienne runs the costume shop already; she can run the rest. *Our Town* in March, and don't let them talk her into a musical. Two. Rosie. I'm her godmother. Somebody's got to do it properly now, from closer than Knox County. I'd like it to be your mother, if Shannon will have her. Ask Shannon, not Vivienne; Vivienne will say yes before she's finished hearing the question."

"And three?"

"Malvolio."

"Hollis, I don't—"

"He's already decided. Dale said he went in your front door like he owned it." Hollis looked at him. "He likes the radiator in the kitchen. He'll eat anything except fish. He'll sleep on your chest if you let him, and on your face if you don't. Keep him, Archie."

"I'll keep him."

"Good." She let her head go back against the pillow. She looked, suddenly, very tired, and every one of her sixty-six years. Her eyes closed.

He thought she'd fallen asleep. He stood up, carefully, so the chair wouldn't scrape.

"Archie."

Her eyes were open again, looking at the ceiling.

"There's one more thing. It's nothing. It's just something I've wondered about for thirty-seven years, and I'd like to give it to somebody who'll wonder about it properly." She turned her head and looked at him. "That last night. Gus's last show. I was in the booth. I ran the board for the Chair. And afterward, when he came off, he went up the center aisle in that cloak, the way he always did, shaking hands, and at the back door Walt Calloway was standing where he always stood, in his uniform, in case." Hollis paused. "And Gus stopped in front of him. And looked at him. And winked."

Archie stood very still.

"Just winked," said Hollis. "And went out. And walked down to the harbor, and we never saw him again." She closed her eyes. "I always wondered what that was about."

At the door, the trooper was standing, and Tess was standing, and the twenty minutes were up.

Archie walked out to Tess's car in the cold bright afternoon with his hands in the pockets of his buffalo-check coat. In the right-hand pocket, where he'd put it that morning without thinking — the way you'd carry anything you'd promised somebody you'd look after — was an iron ring the size of a dinner plate, with a hundred keys on it. It knocked against his hip every step he took. It was heavier than anything he'd ever carried, and he carried it all the way home.
