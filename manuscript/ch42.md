# Chapter 42

## Daylight

The fog had burned off by nine, and at noon, when Tess let him out at the head of the wharf and drove away without a word, the Galley was so full that people were eating standing up at the pie case.

When Archie came in, the bell over the door rang and the room went quiet, heads lifting, forks slowing. Then somebody at the counter started clapping, and somebody else did, and Bev Thibodeau said, "Oh, for heaven's sake, let the man sit down," and it stopped.

The liars' table had a chair pulled out.

Archie looked at it. He looked at Elwin Coombs, and Dickie Thibodeau, and Lionel, who was eating eggs and did not look up. Elwin pushed the chair another three inches out from the table with his boot.

Archie sat in it. Nobody said anything. Bev put a coffee in front of him and a piece of blueberry pie, at noon, without being asked.

"I didn't order pie."

"You're eating pie," said Bev.

He ate the pie.

---

Ivy Crane came in at a quarter past twelve, alone.

She was in the oversized gray sweater she'd been arrested in, two hours out of Knox County Jail, paler than ever, with a manila property envelope under one arm. She didn't look at anybody. She walked the length of the counter to where Shannon Mayo stood with a coffee pot, and the room watched her do it.

"Mrs. Mayo," said Ivy. "I'm Ivy Crane."

Shannon put the coffee pot down on the warmer.

Ivy took a check out of her sweater pocket and laid it on the counter between them. It was for one thousand and fifty dollars. "That's what you paid," she said. "For the readings. I did the research for them. I found Tommy's obituary, and the boat's name, and your Facebook page, and the pictures of your kids." She didn't look away. "Everything my mother told you about your husband, I gave her. None of it came from him. I'm sorry."

Shannon looked at the check. She didn't pick it up.

"I'm going to write to everyone," Ivy said. "Everyone who ever sat with her. There are thirty books. I'm going to give them back their money out of hers and tell them the truth. It'll take a long time." She took a breath. "I wanted you to be first. Because it was here."

When Shannon spoke, it was barely above the noise of the grill.

"Hollis did it for me. That's what they're saying. Because of what that woman told me on the phone."

Ivy didn't answer. There wasn't an answer.

Shannon looked down at the check. Then she folded it in half and put it in the pocket of her apron, and picked up the coffee pot, and went down the counter to refill the man at the end, who didn't need it. Her hand was shaking. She didn't spill a drop.

Ivy turned to go, and saw Archie, and stopped.

He got up. It hurt. He went down the counter to her, and said it low, so that it was hers.

"I saw Hollis this morning. She asked me to tell you something." He gave it to her plainly, all of it: the two days she'd told herself it was only the scarf, the kitchen table on Thursday night, and that she had never in her life been so ashamed of anything. "She wanted you to hear it from somebody."

Ivy didn't move. Her thumb moved once along the edge of the property envelope.

"Tell her I heard it," Ivy said, to the floor. "That's all. I don't have anything else yet."

"I'll tell her."

"Margaret Okafor says you're paying her."

"I am."

"I told you not to help me."

"You did," Archie agreed.

Her face moved toward a smile, and didn't get all the way there, and stayed halfway.

"Vermont," she said. "In January. Vet tech school. I'm going to clean out kennels and get bitten by Labradors." She shrugged. "Nobody's going to hold anybody's hand in the dark."

"Good," said Archie.

She went out. The bell rang behind her.

---

Shannon came to the liars' table ten minutes later with the coffee pot, and refilled his cup, and stood there.

"Liam wants to know if you'll teach him the card one," she said. "The one you did for him on Sunday."

"I will. Any time."

"I said I'd ask. That's all I said." She looked out the window at the moorings, bright in the cold sun. She didn't go.

"Hollis asked me something too," Archie said. "For Rosie. She said somebody ought to do it properly now, from closer than Knox County. She'd like it to be my mother." He kept his eyes on Shannon. "She said to ask you, not my mother. My mother would say yes before she'd finished hearing the question."

Shannon's mouth went tight, and stayed that way for a while.

"Rosie's got a godmother," she said at last. "She's in Rockport with a trooper on her door."

"I know."

"I'm not choosing another one on a Saturday in the Galley." She shifted the pot to her other hand. "Your mother can come Sunday and help with Rosie's hair. Rosie likes her hats. We'll see after that."

She went back to the counter.

---

Warren Gilley came in at half past twelve in his camel coat and his bow tie and stopped in the doorway when he saw Archie.

Archie got up again and went to him before he could leave.

"I'm sorry," he said, low. "For what I said to you in here. There's no excuse, and I'm not going to offer one."

Warren's face stayed set like plaster. "Noted."

Archie held out the cardboard tube he'd had under his arm all morning. Inside, rolled in tissue, were two posters seventy years old: THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER WALKS THROUGH WALLS! and RADIO'S MASTER OF MYSTERY. A man in Philadelphia and a woman in Connecticut had sold them back in the last week, and been very reasonable about it.

"These are from my mother," Archie said. "They were Gus's, and they're hers to give, and she wants Carol to have them. For her room. She says Carol used to sit in the front row every Halloween when she was a girl, with her hair in braids."

"I won't take anything from you," said Warren.

"They're not for you," said Archie. "They're for Carol."

Warren looked at the tube. Then he took it and held it in both hands against the front of his good coat.

"For Carol," he said, and went out without his coffee.

---

At one o'clock the liars' table stood and put on its caps.

Elwin Coombs stopped beside Archie's chair. He looked down at him, and over at Dickie, and at Lionel, who gave the smallest nod.

"That trick," said Elwin. "The chair. Last night."

"Yes?"

"How'd you do it?"

The Galley went quiet again. Archie looked up at the three of them — Elwin's weathered red face, Dickie's suspenders, Lionel, who had stood up in front of the whole town last night and told them his name — and remembered a crawlspace forty feet long, and a girl's voice coming down through the floorboards, and an old man's hand on his arm in the dark.

"I'll never tell you," Archie said. "Not as long as I live."

Elwin considered that. Then he nodded, once, with deep satisfaction.

"Correct answer," said Elwin Coombs, and the liars' table went out into the sunshine.
