# Chapter 29

## The Method

The carriage house had been built for horses, then for the hearse, and since September it had belonged to Lenny, who had filled it with a drill press, a band saw, two welding tanks chained to the wall, and a workbench with every tool hung inside its own painted outline, so you could tell at a glance which one had been borrowed. At the far end, under two hanging work lamps, was the platform.

Lenny had built it the first week, out of plywood and four-by-fours, the same height as the stage of the Merrow Opera House. In the middle he had cut a trap the same size as Gus's — two feet by four, two leaves hinged at the center — and underneath it, where no audience would ever see, he had put three wooden steps, a counterweight on a rope, and a sandbag to sit on.

At ten o'clock on Wednesday night it was cold enough in there to see your breath, in spite of the space heater ticking by the bench. Archie still had paint under his fingernails and, he had been told twice, a stripe of it in his hair. He had set a ladder-back chair from the kitchen in the middle of the platform, folded a white sheet over its back, and swung one of the lamps down so it fell on the chair in a hard, bright circle.

It looked, he thought, terrific.

June sat on an upturned bucket in front of the platform with a notebook on her knee. She had already ruled the page into columns. She was wearing her oldest cardigan and a pair of Lenny's work gloves, because Lenny had told her nobody touched his trap with bare hands, and she had taken him at his word.

"Ladies and gentlemen," said Archie.

"Dad."

"I'm setting the scene."

"I've seen the scene. There's a video of Gus doing it in 1979, shot from the balcony by somebody's uncle. You can hear a man unwrapping a sandwich." She clicked her pen. "You said all of it. That's what I'm writing down. All of it."

She had asked that evening, on the scaffold, with the jack of clubs under her hand, and he'd said yes. Now, with the sheet in his hands, meaning it and doing it turned out to be two separate tricks, and he had only ever practiced one of them.

"All right," he said. "The count."

"The count is everything. One, two, three — that's what they hear. But Gus's count took eleven seconds. You say *one*, and then you tell them something. He always started by saying every trick was a promise." June wrote that down. "Then you tell them about the chair, or who used to sit in it, or what it's like to wait for somebody. You make them lean in. Then *two*—"

"What holds the sheet up?"

"In the video," June said. "When he puts it on, it doesn't collapse. It's wide at the top. Like there's already somebody under it." She looked at him over the notebook. "You skipped that."

At the workbench, Lenny, who had been filing something small, stopped filing.

"I skipped that," Archie admitted.

"Why?"

He had a dozen reasons. He'd been giving them to reporters for thirty years, and they were all charming, and they all meant *no*.

"Habit," he said.

He turned the sheet inside out and showed her.

Sewn into the muslin, about two feet below the top, was a channel of doubled cloth, and inside the channel was a long oval of spring steel no thicker than a coat hanger. He dropped the sheet over the chair. The steel caught on the top of the ladder-back and held the cloth out at the width of a man's shoulders, and the rest fell away in folds to the floor, pooling at the back. Under the lamp it looked like someone sitting under a sheet.

"That's the secret," he said. "From the second it goes on, the chair looks full. They think they're watching an empty chair. They've been watching a shape, and when somebody comes up into it, the shape doesn't change."

June stared at it.

"It's a coat hanger," she said.

"It's a very good coat hanger."

"Spring steel," said Lenny.

"The chair stands on the front leaf of the trap. The back leaf drops. Whoever's under there comes up the steps behind the chair, inside the sheet — that's why it's long at the back — and sits. Eight seconds if you're quick. Lenny holds the counterweight so the leaf doesn't bang, and there's felt on every edge. On three you pull up and back, and the hoop goes with it, and you throw it behind you and never look at it again. Nobody looks at a sheet once there's a person."

"And the person is Grandma."

"The person is Grandma." That had been settled over lobster rolls, with Vivienne holding court from a stepladder. "She comes up. I stand by the chair and put the sheet on and hold the corner so it doesn't move. You do the patter. You do the count. You pull."

"Me," said June.

"You."

She wrote it down, pressing hard.

"She's seventy-three," said Lenny. "She comes up slow."

"Then I count slow," said June.

Lenny looked at her for a moment. Then he went back to filing.

---

"Tonight," said Archie, "your grandmother will be played by me."

It was dark under the platform and smelled of sawdust and cold concrete, and he had to fold himself onto the steps like a deck chair. Lenny lowered himself onto the sandbag, put one hand on the rope, and took out his stopwatch.

"Don't step on the front leaf," he told June, through the boards. "You'll go through."

"I wasn't going to."

"Everybody isn't going to. Then they do."

Overhead, the sheet went over the chair. June took a breath.

"This is a chair," she said. "It's — an ordinary chair. One."

Lenny pulled. The back leaf swung down without a whisper, and Archie went up into the muffled white of the sheet and got one knee onto the platform, and had his other foot still on the top step when June said, briskly, "Two, three," and the sheet went away and the lamp hit him in the face.

He was on his hands and knees behind the chair with one leg in a hole in the floor. He looked up at his daughter. She looked down at her father.

"Ankle," said Lenny, from below. "That's his ankle."

June put both hands over her face.

"Again," said Archie, backing down the steps. "You counted it like a fire drill. And you were looking at the chair."

"How do you know?"

"I could hear it. Your voice was pointed at the chair, and if your voice is pointed at the chair, so is everybody's attention. Where you look, they look. You have to look at them, Junebug. The whole count. You can't check."

"I *know*."

They did it twice more, and the second time it worked, more or less. Neither of them said so.

---

Afterward she sat on the edge of the platform with her boots hanging over, and he sat beside her. Underneath them Lenny was doing something to the counterweight that involved a wrench and a good deal of breathing.

"The last time he did it," June said. "Did you know it was the last time?"

"No."

"What do you remember?"

On Monday, on Dale Pinkham's boat, he had said it in one sentence: ten years old, second row. It came out differently now.

"I had a seat on the aisle," he said. "Second row, on the left. Your grandmother wasn't next to me, because she was under the stage. I had a bag of peppermints somebody's husband had bought me at the Galley, and I ate every one before the intermission. The radiators were going. The whole hall smelled like wet coats." He looked at the chair. "He did the Chair last. He always did. And during the count, the whole eleven seconds, he looked straight at me. Straight down into the second row. I thought it was because I was his grandson. I thought it was a secret between us."

"It was the misdirection."

"It was the misdirection," he agreed. "It was probably also because I was his grandson. Both things can be true. Then the sheet came off, and it was your grandmother in the chair, in her old sequins, and the whole place stood up. They hadn't seen her on that stage since she was twenty-four. He kissed her hand. The curtain came down. And I went down to the green room to wait for him, because he'd said he'd walk me home, and I fell asleep on a pile of coats."

June looked at her hands.

"Do you think he drowned?" she said.

The heater ticked. Under the platform, the wrench had stopped.

"I think he did the trick," Archie said.

"Then where did he go?"

"I don't know." He held his hands out and looked at the paint under the nails. "I really don't know, June."

She sat beside him for a while. Then she got up, pulled the work gloves back on, and picked the sheet up off the boards.

"Lenny," she said. "Will you go under?"

"No," said Lenny.

"Please."

A long silence from beneath the platform. Then the sandbag creaked.

"I'm going to watch from the house," Archie said, and went and sat on the bucket.

---

She began with Gus's line, the one about promises, and then she left it behind. She told the bucket, the hearse and her father that this chair had come out of a kitchen in this house, and that a great many bad breakfasts had been eaten on it, most of them cooked by her father. She put the sheet on in one clean movement, and the hoop caught, and the shape sat there.

"One," June said.

She looked at him.

He could not afterward have repeated a word of what she said next, because she was looking at him the whole time — not at the sheet, not at the trap, not at the place where every instinct she had must have been telling her to check — straight down at the bucket in the cold, for all eleven seconds. Behind her the back leaf dropped without a sound. The sheet never moved.

"Three," said June, and pulled it up and back and threw it behind her without a glance.

Lenny Szabo sat in the chair in all three of his sweaters, with his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and his arms folded, wearing the expression of a man waiting for a bus he did not expect to enjoy.

It was perfect.

Archie stood up. He couldn't say anything. June's face changed and started to ask, and he held up one hand.

"I'll be right back," he said, and went out.

---

The dooryard was dark and cold. The fog hadn't come in yet, and the sky over the harbor was full of stars. He stood on the frozen gravel in his shirtsleeves with his breath smoking and let the cold have his face for a minute.

Across the dooryard the kitchen light was on, and his mother was at the window with the phone at her ear and her back to the glass. She had taken two calls into the pantry at supper and come out both times saying it was nobody. His mother had never in her life called anyone nobody. A black shawl on the back of a chair, two feet from the horn. He made himself stop.

Through the carriage-house window he could see the lamp, and the platform, and his daughter shaking out the sheet to do it again. Lenny's bald head rose out of the trap. He said something. June laughed — short and surprised, the kind she usually caught before it got out — and didn't catch it. She put the sheet over the chair, and the hoop caught, and the shape sat under the lamp, waiting for somebody to come up into it.

His phone buzzed. TESS CALLOWAY.

"Sergeant."

"This call isn't happening." Her voice was low, with an engine under it; she was parked somewhere. "The AG's office is filing on Ivy Crane in the morning. Murder."

Archie closed his eyes.

"Okafor's put you on the jail's visitor list for ten tomorrow. She cleared it with the AG this afternoon. Don't be late."

"I won't."

The engine idled. He waited for the rest of it, the voice from the parking lot, and it didn't come.

"I watched your toot forty times," Tess said. "I believe you."

Then she hung up.
