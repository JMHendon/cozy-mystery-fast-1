# Chapter 39

## Harbor Blanket

By the time Ozzie brought the workboat in against the fuel dock, half of Merrow was on the wharf.

They had come down from the opera house at intermission, because word in Merrow traveled faster than the people carrying it, and they stood under the floodlights at the head of the wharf in their good coats and their children's costumes, three deep along the railing by the Galley: Bev in her apron with a pitcher of cider still in her hand; Elwin and Dickie and Lionel; Warren Gilley without Carol; Deb Coombs; Kev with his camera held down at his side, not filming. Dale Pinkham had strung yellow tape across the top of the fuel-dock ramp and stood in front of it with his arms spread.

The ambulance was already there, its back doors open, its lights turning red and white in the fog.

Then Archie was on the dock, on his knees, with somebody's foil blanket around his shoulders crackling every time he breathed, and his whole body shaking so hard his teeth clacked. He was in his socks. One of them was gone. An EMT, a young man with a beard, was holding his wrist and looking at his watch and saying things Archie didn't listen to.

Hollis was on a stretcher three feet away, wrapped in a gray wool harbor blanket with a foil one over it, her face blue-white under the floodlights and her hair dark with seawater. Two more EMTs were bent over her. Tess crouched at the head of the stretcher, soaked to the shoulder on one side, her braid dripping.

Hollis turned her head and found Archie.

"It was me," she said.

The EMTs went still.

"Not the girl. Not Ivy. Me. I put it on the horn, at the table, when Teddy asked me to move it. Wolfsbane. From my own garden." Her teeth were chattering, and she spoke through them, carefully. "For Peter. And for Danny. And for Shannon, because she'd started on Shannon—"

"Hollis." Tess's hand came down on the blanket over Hollis's shoulder. "Stop. Stop talking." Her voice was rough. "Hollis Tibbetts, you have the right to remain silent. Anything you say can be used against you in a court of law. You have the right to an attorney—"

"I know my rights, Theresa." A ghost of the old classroom voice, through blue lips. "I taught you to spell them."

"—and if you cannot afford an attorney one will be appointed for you. Do you understand these rights as I've read them to you?"

"Yes."

"Then I'm not going to ask you any questions. Not one. Not here." Tess didn't let go of the blanket. "You're going to the hospital, and you're going to get warm, and tomorrow when a doctor says you can, you and I will sit down with a lawyer in a room with the door shut, and you can tell me all of it then. Not on a dock. Do you hear me? Not here, Hollis."

Hollis looked up at her.

"You were always my best one at procedure," she said.

Then her hand came out from under the blankets, shaking, wet. It went into the pocket of the soaked cardigan and came out holding something that clanked: an iron ring the size of a dinner plate, with what looked like a hundred keys on it — brass and steel, long and short, every lock in the Merrow Opera House and half the doors on Main Street. Seawater ran off it onto the dock.

She held it out across the gap. Not to Tess. To Archie.

"Somebody has to run the lights," said Hollis.

He took it. It was so heavy his shaking arm dipped under it.

The EMTs moved then, all at once, and the stretcher came up, and they wheeled her up the ramp past Dale's tape and the people of Merrow standing silent along the railing. Archie saw Bev put her hand over her mouth, and Elwin Coombs take off his cap, and Shannon Mayo at the very end of the railing, with Rosie in her lobster suit asleep on her shoulder, tears running down her face and not wiping them away.

The ambulance doors closed. The lights turned away up Main Street into the fog.

The young EMT was trying to get Archie onto a second stretcher.

"No," said Archie.

"Sir, you were in fifty-degree water for—"

"Three minutes and forty seconds. I know. I counted." He could barely get the words out. "I'm all right. I've done worse." He got one foot under him. "I have a show."

"Sir—"

"I have a *show.*"

Tess was standing over him. She had Hollis's envelope in one hand, dark with seawater at one corner, and her other hand pressed against her own chest, and she was looking down at him with an expression he had never seen on her and didn't have time to read.

"You're out of your mind," she said.

"Probably." He held out his hand. "Phone. Please. Mine's in the harbor."

She gave him her phone.

He dialed with a thumb that didn't want to work. Lenny answered on the first ring. Behind him, faint and tinny, Archie could hear an audience laughing, and a piano, and his mother's voice, enormous, doing a bit of Gus's patter he hadn't heard since he was nine.

"She's alive," Archie said. "She's going to the hospital. Lenny, where are we?"

"Your mother's doing the Floating Lady with Corey from the Galley," said Lenny. "He keeps waving to his mother."

"How long to the Chair?"

"Seven minutes. Maybe eight. Your mother's milking it."

"Put the chair on the trap. Keep Mother by it. I'm coming in from the alley."

A short silence on the line.

"You'll never make it," said Lenny.

"I've made it from worse. Seven minutes. Don't tell June."

He handed the phone back to Tess. Ozzie was at his elbow, still in his float coat, with his truck keys in his hand and the folded opera cloak over his arm.

"Truck's at the head of the wharf," said Ozzie. "I'll take you to the alley." He held out the cloak. "You'll want this."

Archie looked at it, folded square over Ozzie's arm, and had no time to wonder who had folded it.

"Keep it for me," he said. "Give it to my mother."

Then he got up off the dock in one sock and a foil blanket and a soaking-wet tailcoat, with a ring of a hundred keys clutched against his chest, and he ran — staggered, mostly, but forward — up the ramp past Dale's tape and through the people of Merrow, who stood aside for him without a word and closed in behind him to watch him go.

At the top of the ramp, Bev Thibodeau stepped out in front of him and put the pitcher of hot cider into his free hand.

"Drink that," she said. "Then go."

He drank. It burned all the way down. He gave her back the pitcher, and she pulled his foil blanket closed at the neck with one hard tug, like a mother at a school bus, and stepped aside.

Ozzie had the truck at the curb with the passenger door open. Archie fell into it, and Ozzie pulled out into the fog toward Main Street before the door was shut.
