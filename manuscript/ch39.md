# Chapter 39

## Harbor Blanket

By the time Ozzie brought the workboat in against the fuel dock, half of Merrow was on the wharf.

They'd come down from the opera house at intermission — word had gone through the hall the way word went through Merrow, faster than the people carrying it — and they stood in their good coats and their children's costumes under the floodlights at the head of the wharf, in the fog, three deep along the railing by the Galley: Bev in her apron with a coffee urn's worth of cider still in a pitcher in her hand; Elwin and Dickie and Lionel; Warren Gilley without Carol; Deb Coombs; Kev with his camera held down at his side, not filming. Dale Pinkham had strung a line of yellow tape across the top of the fuel-dock ramp and was standing in front of it with his arms spread like a man holding back a tide.

The ambulance was already there, its back doors open, its lights turning red and white in the fog.

Archie didn't remember climbing the ladder. He remembered being on the dock, on his knees, with somebody's foil blanket around his shoulders crackling every time he breathed, and his whole body shaking so hard his teeth clacked. He was in his socks. One of them was gone. Somebody — an EMT, a young man with a beard — was holding his wrist and looking at his watch and saying things Archie didn't listen to.

Hollis was on a stretcher three feet away, wrapped in a gray wool harbor blanket with another foil one over it, her face blue-white under the floodlights and her white hair flat and dark with seawater. Two more EMTs were bent over her. Tess was crouched at the head of the stretcher, soaked to the shoulder on one side, her braid dripping.

Hollis turned her head and found Archie.

"It was me," she said.

Her voice was very small and very clear. The EMTs went still.

"Not the girl. Not Ivy. Me. I put it on the horn, at the table, when Teddy asked me to move it. Wolfsbane. From my own garden." Her teeth were chattering, and she spoke through them, carefully, like a woman reading aloud. "For Peter. And for Danny. And for Shannon, because she'd started on Shannon—"

"Hollis." Tess's hand came down on the gray blanket over Hollis's shoulder. "Stop. Stop talking." Her voice was rough. "Hollis Tibbetts, you have the right to remain silent. Anything you say can be used against you in a court of law. You have the right to an attorney—"

"I know my rights, Theresa." A ghost of the old classroom voice, through blue lips. "I taught you to spell them."

"—and if you cannot afford an attorney one will be appointed for you. Do you understand these rights as I've read them to you?"

"Yes."

"Then I'm not going to ask you any questions. Not one. Not here." Tess's jaw was set. "You're going to the hospital, and you're going to get warm, and tomorrow when a doctor says you can, you and I will sit down with a lawyer in a room with the door shut, and you can tell me all of it then. Not on a dock. Do you hear me? Not here, Hollis."

Hollis looked up at her for a long moment.

"You were always my best one at procedure," she said.

Then her hand came out from under the blankets, shaking, wet. It went into the pocket of the soaked cardigan, which they hadn't gotten off her yet, and came out holding something that clanked: an iron ring the size of a dinner plate, with what looked like a hundred keys on it — brass and steel, long and short, every lock in the Merrow Opera House and half the doors on Main Street. Seawater ran off it onto the dock.

She held it out across the gap. Not to Tess. To Archie.

"Somebody has to run the lights," said Hollis.

He took it. It was so heavy his shaking arm dipped under it.

The EMTs moved then, all at once, and the stretcher came up, and they were wheeling her up the ramp past Dale's yellow tape, past the people of Merrow standing silent along the railing — and Archie saw Bev put her hand over her mouth, and Elwin Coombs take off his cap, and Shannon Mayo, at the very end of the railing, with Rosie in her lobster suit asleep on her shoulder, standing perfectly still with tears running down her face and not wiping them away.

The ambulance doors closed. The lights turned away up Main Street into the fog.

The young EMT with the beard was trying to get Archie onto a second stretcher.

"No," said Archie.

"Sir, you were in fifty-degree water for—"

"Three minutes and forty seconds. I know. I counted." He was shaking so hard he could barely get the words out. "I'm all right. I've done worse. I did the Arctic special." He got one foot under him. "I have a show."

"Sir—"

"I have a *show.*"

Tess was standing over him. She had Hollis's envelope in one hand, sealed, dark with seawater at one corner, and her other hand was pressed flat against her own chest, and she was looking down at him with an expression he had never seen on her, and couldn't read, and didn't have time to.

"You're out of your mind," she said.

"Probably." He held out his hand. "Phone. Please. Mine's in the harbor."

She looked at him a second longer. Then she gave him her phone.

He dialed with a thumb that didn't want to work. Lenny answered on the first ring. Through the phone, faint and tinny, Archie could hear an audience laughing, and a piano, and his mother's voice, enormous, doing a bit of Gus's patter he hadn't heard since he was nine.

"She's alive," Archie said. "She's going to the hospital. Lenny, where are we?"

"Your mother's doing the Floating Lady with Corey from the Galley." Lenny's voice was perfectly flat. "He keeps waving to his mother."

"How long to the Chair?"

"Seven minutes. Maybe eight. Your mother's milking it."

"Put the chair on the trap. Keep Mother by it. I'm coming in from the alley."

There was a short silence on the line.

"You'll never make it," said Lenny.

"I've made it from worse. Seven minutes. Don't tell June."

He handed the phone back to Tess. Ozzie was already there, at his elbow, with his truck keys in his hand and the folded opera cloak over his arm, and his float coat still on.

"Truck's at the head of the wharf," said Ozzie. "I'll take you to the alley." He paused. "You'll want this."

He held out the cloak.

Archie looked at it, folded square over Ozzie's arm, and had no time to wonder who had folded it.

"Keep it for me," he said. "Give it to my mother."

Then he got up off the dock in one sock and a foil blanket and a soaking-wet tailcoat, with a ring of a hundred keys clutched against his chest, and he ran — staggered, mostly, but forward — up the ramp past Dale's tape and through the people of Merrow, who stood aside for him without a word, every one of them, and closed in behind him and watched him go.

At the top of the ramp, Bev Thibodeau stepped out in front of him and put the pitcher of hot cider into his free hand.

"Drink that," she said. "Then go."

He drank it. It burned all the way down. He gave her back the pitcher, and she took it, and pulled his foil blanket closed at the neck with one hard tug like a mother at a school bus, and stepped aside.

Ozzie's truck was idling at the curb. Archie fell into the passenger seat. The truck pulled out into the fog, and through the open window, as it went, he heard the bell buoy off Shag Ledge still ringing, out in the dark, for nobody.
