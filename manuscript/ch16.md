# Chapter 16

## Person of Interest

The select board room was on the ground floor of the town hall, and it was the coldest room Archie had ever been in that wasn't a walk-in freezer or a lake.

It was a long, plain room with an oak table, five high-backed chairs for the select board, a flag on a stand, and a wall of framed photographs of past selectmen, most of them in beards. The radiator under the window was clanking with enormous energy and producing no heat at all. Out the window, across Main Street, the white steeple of the Congregational church stood up into a hard blue two o'clock sky.

The State Police had moved in. There were two laptops on the table, a printer on a folding chair, a whiteboard on an easel with nothing on it yet, three banker's boxes, and a large Dunkin' cup in front of the man in the middle chair.

"Mr. Fairweather," said the man. "Sit down. Thank you for coming in."

Lieutenant Neil Brannock was in his fifties, tall even sitting down, with a gray crewcut, a wedding ring, and a pair of reading glasses that he took off as Archie came in and put on again a moment later, irritably, to read something. He wore a navy State Police fleece zipped to the chin over his suit. Tess sat at the end of the table to his left with a notebook and her charcoal coat still on. Her breath showed faintly in the air.

Archie sat. The folding chair was so cold he could feel it through his trousers.

"You don't have a lawyer with you," said Brannock.

"I don't need one."

"Everybody says that." Brannock pressed a button on a small digital recorder. He gave the date and the time and the names of the people in the room, in a flat, efficient voice. "Mr. Fairweather, you're here voluntarily. You're not under arrest. You can leave whenever you want, and you can have a lawyer whenever you want. Do you understand that?"

"Yes."

Brannock sniffed. He looked up over his glasses. "Have you been fishing?"

"Hauling. With Chief Pinkham. This morning." Archie had showered twice. The coat had not. "It's herring. I'm told it doesn't come out."

"It doesn't," said Tess, without looking up.

"Lieutenant, before we start, may I just say what I came here to say? Because I think it'll save us all a great deal of time." Archie leaned forward. "It wasn't the tea. It was the trumpet. She had a hand free in the dark — her left — and she picked up the trumpet and put the small end to her lips to do the voices, and somebody had put the aconite on the mouthpiece. Your sergeant saw a reconstruction yesterday. I can show you in thirty seconds."

He had a deck of cards in the inside pocket of his coat. He took it out, plainly, and fanned it on the oak.

Nobody had asked him to. Brannock looked at the cards and then at Archie, and didn't stop him, which Archie took for encouragement.

"Watch my right hand," Archie said. He picked a card out of the fan with his right — the eight of clubs — and showed it to the recorder, and to Tess, and to Brannock, and slid it into the middle of the deck with a great deal of fuss and a squared tap. "Now. Where's the eight?"

"On top," said Brannock.

Archie stopped.

"Your left thumb," said Brannock. He hadn't moved. "When you squared the deck. Your right hand made all the noise and your left thumb pushed the card back up. I don't know what that's called. I saw it because I was watching your left hand, because I was told that's what you'd do." He took his glasses off. "Do it again. Slowly. And tell me, the whole time, where your left hand is."

Archie looked at Tess. Tess was writing.

"That's the problem I have, Mr. Fairweather," Brannock said. "You've just shown me — voluntarily, for the tape — that you can make two people look at one of your hands while the other one does what it likes. That's not a thing I had to prove. You came in and did it." He tapped the deck. "Put those away, please."

Archie put them away.

There was a knock, and the door opened before anyone answered it. Two large young men in canvas work jackets came in backward, carrying an object the size of a jukebox wrapped in blankets. Behind them came a third man with a dolly, on which was strapped a tall finned oil-filled radiator, the industrial kind.

"Delivery," said the first young man, consulting a clipboard. "Fairweather? From Portland. Espresso machine, two-group, and the heater. Where do you want it?"

Brannock looked at the espresso machine, and at the heater, and for a long moment at Archie.

"Dale told me this room was freezing," Archie said. "And I've had the coffee at the Dunkin' on Route 1. Lieutenant, I'm not trying to bribe anybody, I'm trying to keep you all from getting pneumonia. It's paid for. It's a gift to the town. You can leave it here when you go."

"Take it out," said Brannock.

"It's paid for," said the young man.

"Then take it out paid for." Brannock didn't raise his voice. "All of it. Thank you."

The young men looked at each other. They began, with some difficulty, to back the espresso machine out the door.

"Leave the heater," said Tess.

Brannock turned his head.

"It's forty-eight degrees in here, Lieutenant," Tess said. She hadn't stopped writing. "I checked."

Brannock looked at her for a second. Then he looked at the radiator, which had chosen that moment to clank twice like a man hitting a pipe with a wrench. He said nothing. The third young man plugged the heater into the wall by Tess's chair, turned the dial to high, and left, and the door shut on the sound of the espresso machine going down the hall on its blankets.

Brannock opened the laptop in front of him and turned it so Archie could see.

"I've been through every frame Marchand Media has given us," he said. "There's a lot of it. This is the part I keep coming back to."

It was the stage in the work light. Archie in his black shirt, sleeves pushed up, picking up the trumpet, looking through it, holding the small end to his ear. *Anybody home?* Then the small end to his lips. The sound came out of the little speaker thin and tinny. The crew laughing. *No spirits in here, folks.* The trumpet going back down in the center of the table, exactly where it had been. The time code in the corner said 19:35:12.

Brannock clicked. Now it was Archie unscrewing the two cups from Bev's thermos, filling both, holding one out across the table. Ivy's hand coming flat over her mother's place. Lorelei laughing, taking the cup. *To the vanishing man.* Lorelei drinking. 19:41:50.

"You poured her tea," said Brannock.

"I drank mine. The same tea. From the same thermos. I drank all of it."

"You drank from a cup you poured. She drank from a cup you poured." Brannock leaned back. "We went to collect the thermos this morning. Mrs. Thibodeau washed it yesterday afternoon. The thermos and both cups, with soap. Somebody brought it back to her at about noon on Sunday. Who was that?"

The radiator clanked. The new heater ticked as it warmed.

"Me," said Archie.

"Noon on Sunday. Twelve hours after you told Sergeant Calloway, in the hospital, that Lorelei Crane had been poisoned with aconite. Before anybody else in the state had used that word."

"My mother told me to take it back."

It came out before he could stop it, and it sounded exactly as bad as it was. He heard it land on the recorder. Across the table, Tess's pen stopped for a moment and then went on.

"Mr. Fairweather," said Brannock, quite gently, "you knew the poison before the lab did. You poured her the only drink on that tape. You had two million dollars on her being a fraud, and forty minutes later she was doing your grandfather's voice in the dark. And the morning after she died, the cup she drank from went back to a diner sink."

Archie sat in the cold folding chair and felt the room tilt. It wasn't a trick. It was just the shape of things, laid end to end by a man who was good at his job.

He thought about June counting on her fingers at the breakfast table. Then he told them the rest, because it was true and because they'd find it anyway.

"There's wolfsbane in my garden," he said. "Monkshood. A whole stand of it, along the fence at the bottom, where it goes down to the rocks. My grandfather planted it. He thought the name was funny, for Halloween. My mother cut some on Thursday and put it in a jug on the kitchen table." He looked at Brannock. "Half the gardens in this town have it. But mine's one of them, and I'd rather you heard it from me."

Brannock had stopped writing. He looked at Archie for a long time with no expression at all. Then he wrote something down, carefully.

Tess put her pen flat on the notebook.

"Lieutenant," she said. "For the recording. Mr. Fairweather's here voluntarily, and he's now said three times he doesn't want a lawyer. Before we go any further I'd like him to hear again that he can stop this and have one."

Brannock turned his head and looked at her. Something passed between them that Archie couldn't read and didn't need to.

"He's heard it, Sergeant."

"I'd like him to hear it again. On the tape."

Brannock took his glasses off. "Mr. Fairweather, you can stop this interview at any time and consult a lawyer. Do you want to?"

"No," said Archie. "I want you to listen to me about the raspberry."

"The raspberry."

"Seven thirty-five. On your laptop. I put the small end of that trumpet in my mouth and blew, as hard as I could, in front of two cameras and a dozen people. And I'm sitting here. I'm fine. I've never felt better in my life, apart from this morning on the boat." He tapped the table. "That horn was clean at seven thirty-five. Whatever was put on it was put on after. That's your window, Lieutenant. Seven thirty-five to eight o'clock. That's who you want."

Brannock actually thought about it; Archie watched him do it.

"Or," he said, "it's the one moment on that tape when a professional sleight-of-hand man had his hands, and his mouth, on the thing that killed her." He put his glasses back on. "Mr. Fairweather, your whole career is getting a camera to record something that didn't happen. You're very good at it. I've seen the riverboat. I'm not going to take the tape's word for what was or wasn't on your lips."

Archie opened his mouth and found he had nothing to put in it.

"Sergeant Calloway let you onto a sealed scene yesterday," Brannock went on, in the same flat voice, "and you picked a lock, and then you picked up a piece of evidence with your bare hands and put your prints all over it."

"That's on me, Lieutenant," said Tess.

"Yes," said Brannock. "It is." He didn't look at her. "We'd like to search your garden. And your kitchen. You can make us get a warrant."

"Search it," said Archie. "Search the garden. Search the house. Lenny'll show you the trapdoor in the pantry. Search the trunk in the turret, if you can get it open. I've been trying since August, and if you crack it I'll owe you dinner for the rest of your life."

Brannock almost smiled. It was there and then it wasn't.

"Sergeant Calloway will bring a consent form out to the house this afternoon." He clicked the recorder off. "And Mr. Fairweather. Don't go near my witnesses. Ivy Crane. Shannon Mayo. Warren Gilley. The production company. Kevin Duchesne. All of them."

Archie stood up. His knees were stiff from the cold; the heater by Tess's chair had only just begun to breathe warm air into the corner of the room.

"Lieutenant," he said, "I live here. I buy my coffee from Shannon Mayo. Warren Gilley is selling me the building you're sitting in." He buttoned his coat, which smelled of herring. "Where exactly would you like me to go?"

Brannock looked at him over his glasses.

"Somewhere I can find you," he said.
