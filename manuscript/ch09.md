# Chapter 9

## The Waiting Room

The emergency department waiting room at the hospital in Rockport had eleven blue plastic chairs bolted together in rows, a television showing a cooking competition with the sound off, a vending machine that sold coffee and hot chocolate and something it called tea, and a fish tank with one large, depressed-looking goldfish in it. At nine o'clock on a Saturday night it was empty except for a man with a bandaged hand asleep in the corner, and Ivy Crane, and Archie.

Ivy had come in the hearse.

He hadn't planned it. When the medics had carried Lorelei down the opera house stairs and out into the cold on Main Street, and the ambulance doors had opened on all that white light and equipment, Ivy had walked to the back of the ambulance behind the stretcher and then simply stopped. The medics were already working — two of them crouched over her mother, one calling numbers — and there was no room, and somebody said *Follow us, ma'am, it's the hospital in Rockport*, and the doors shut, and Ivy stood there on the pavement in her gray sweater with no coat, holding her mother's handbag, not moving at all.

Teddy Marchand had been on the steps of the town hall with a phone to each ear. Hollis had been coming down the stairs. Archie had taken off his buffalo-check coat, put it around Ivy's shoulders, steered her across the street to the hearse, put her in the passenger seat and done up her seat belt, as he would have done for June. She hadn't said a word the whole twenty minutes down Route 1. She'd sat with the handbag on her knees and her hands folded on top of it and looked straight ahead at the ambulance's taillights.

She was sitting the same way now, in the blue plastic chair, his coat still around her shoulders.

Archie brought her a cup from the vending machine. It was tea in the sense that it was hot and brown.

"You don't have to drink it," he said. "I wouldn't. But it's something to hold."

She took it. She held it. She didn't drink it.

He sat down one chair away from her, because sitting right beside her felt like too much and sitting across the room felt like abandonment, and for a while neither of them said anything. On the television a man in a white jacket was setting fire to a dessert. The goldfish turned around in its tank and swam back the other way.

Ivy was rubbing the tips of her right fingers against her thumb — slowly, over and over, as if there were something on them she couldn't get off.

"Is somebody coming for you?" Archie asked. "Teddy? Anybody?"

"Teddy's calling the network." Her voice was perfectly flat. "Teddy's always calling the network."

"Family?"

"She's my family." She stopped. "Was. Is. She's my family."

He let that sit.

Here was the thing he hated about himself, sitting in that blue plastic chair: he couldn't stop thinking. He wanted to be the man who just sat there and was kind, and he was being that man, he hoped, on the outside. But inside, the part of him that had spent thirty years taking things apart to see how they worked was running the whole séance back and forth like tape. The bell. The touches. The static. The trumpet floating up on the left. *Her* left. The side where Ivy sat.

And Ivy's hands, at the end — one holding her mother's, one wiping the narrow end of a tin horn with a silk scarf, twice, hard, while her mother was dying on the floor.

He knew why. He was almost sure he knew why. He'd done something like it himself, a thousand times, after a thousand shows: you put the props back where they were. You clean them. You reset. You never let anyone pick up a prop you haven't cleaned, because the prop is where the method is.

He didn't mean to ask. He'd told himself in the car he wasn't going to ask. It came out anyway, quiet, before he could stop it.

"Ivy. In the dark. Was your mother holding your hand the whole time?"

Ivy turned and looked at him for the first time since the opera house. Her eyes were dry and pale and absolutely empty.

"She held my hand," she said.

"Ivy—"

"She held my hand." She turned back to the television. "You can stop now."

He stopped.

At nine-fifty, a pair of double doors at the end of the room opened, and a doctor came out. He was in his forties, slight, with dark circles under his eyes and a stethoscope around his neck, and he walked across the waiting room in a way Archie recognized from every hospital he'd ever been in — not fast, not slow, choosing the speed carefully. His badge said DR. A. RAO.

Ivy stood up. The coat slid off her shoulders onto the chair.

"Ms. Crane?"

"Yes."

Dr. Rao told her. He did it well — plainly, without hurrying, looking at her face the whole time. Her mother's heart had gone into a dangerous rhythm on the way in. They had tried everything, many times, but the rhythm wouldn't come back. She had died at 9:40. He was very sorry. They didn't know yet what had caused it. There would have to be an examination, because of the circumstances, and somebody would explain all of that to her.

Ivy listened to all of it standing perfectly straight with her mother's handbag in both hands.

"Can I see her?" she said.

"In a little while. Someone will come and get you."

"Thank you," said Ivy, as though he had given her directions.

Dr. Rao glanced at Archie, and something flickered across his tired face — the look of a man who has just realized who is sitting in his waiting room — and then he put it away, because it didn't matter, and went back through the double doors.

Ivy sat down. She picked his coat up off the chair and put it around her shoulders again, carefully, and folded her hands on the handbag, and went back to looking at the television.

Archie sat beside her. He didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't be for him instead of for her.

At five past ten the outside doors hissed open on a gust of cold air, and Tess Calloway came in.

She was in the charcoal coat over the navy fleece, with her hair in its braid and a lanyard with a badge on it around her neck, and she had the same look she'd had at Sea View — the whole room in a second and a half. She saw Archie. Something happened to her eyebrows. Then she went to the nurses' desk and spoke quietly for a minute, and went through the double doors, and came back out ten minutes later, and crossed the room to Ivy and crouched down in front of her chair so their faces were level.

"Ms. Crane. I'm Detective Sergeant Calloway, from the State Police. I'm very sorry about your mother."

"Thank you."

"I'm going to need to ask you some questions, but not tonight. Is there someone I can call?"

"No."

"All right." Tess's voice was quieter than Archie had heard it, and much kinder. "I'll be right over there if you need anything."

She stood, and looked at Archie, and tilted her head very slightly toward the vending machine.

He went.

"Chief Pinkham called us," she said, low, with her back to the room. "He says you told him on the stairs it wasn't natural."

"It wasn't."

"She's a fifty-eight-year-old woman under a lot of stress who collapsed with a heart arrhythmia. The doctor says that happens."

"She said her mouth was numb." Archie kept his voice down. "Before she collapsed. Her mouth and her lips — she said she couldn't feel them. And her voice went thick, slurred, right before the trumpet fell. Sergeant, there's a poison that does that. Numbness in the mouth first, tingling, burning, then the heart goes wrong. It's in half the gardens in Maine. Monkshood. Wolfsbane. Aconite." He saw her face. "I did a whole Borgia routine in 2009 — poisoned goblets, the works. I read everything. The Victorians used to call it the queen of poisons. It doesn't show up unless you look for it."

Tess Calloway looked at him for a long moment.

"Go on," she said.

He told her about the trumpet. About how the medium speaks through the small end in the dark — Gus's book, page one-twelve — and how, if anyone wanted to hurt her, that was the one thing in the room that would go to her mouth and nobody else's. And then he stopped, because the next thing was the thing he'd seen from the floor, and he turned his head and looked across the waiting room at Ivy Crane, sitting very straight in his coat with her mother's handbag on her knees, watching a man on television set fire to a dessert.

It would cost her. He knew it would cost her. He didn't know what it would cost yet, but he'd been reading people for a living since he was nineteen, and he knew the shape of a thing that was going to cost.

The other thing was a lie.

"When the lights came up," Archie said, "I was on the floor with her mother. I saw Ivy pick up the trumpet and wipe the small end. With her scarf. Twice. Then she put the scarf in her coat pocket."

Tess didn't write it down. She didn't need to; he could see her put it somewhere and close the drawer.

"Wipe it," she said.

"It's — there's a reason a performer might do that. A completely innocent reason. It's called a reset—"

"I didn't ask for a reason." She was already turning. She crossed the room and crouched down again in front of Ivy's chair, and Archie watched Ivy's face as Tess spoke to her quietly — watched it go from empty to something else, something very still and very careful.

"Ms. Crane. The scarf you were wearing tonight. I understand it's in your coat. Would you be willing to let me take it, just for now? I'll give you a receipt."

Ivy's eyes went past Tess, across the room, and found Archie by the vending machine. They stayed on him for a moment. He made himself not look away.

"No," Ivy said to Tess, very politely. "It was hers. It was my mother's. I'd like to keep it."

Tess looked at her for a second. Then she nodded, once. "All right," she said, and stood up. That was all. Archie understood: an unexplained death in a hospital wasn't a crime scene, and a grieving daughter's scarf wasn't evidence of anything, yet. Not yet.

Tess went to the nurses' desk again. He heard her ask for Dr. Rao, and heard her say, when he came out, *a specific toxicology screen — I'll have the medical examiner's office call you — aconitine, A-C-O-N-I* — and then she had her phone out and was talking to somebody about the opera house. *Has Dale still got the table roped off? Good. Nobody touches anything on that stage until the evidence tech gets there. Especially the trumpet. Yes, Dale, the trumpet.*

She hung up and came back to the vending machine. She bought herself a cup of coffee, which came out of the machine looking like something drained from a crankcase, and drank some of it without wincing, and looked at him over the rim.

"Mr. Fairweather," she said. "How many people in Merrow, do you think, would know the symptoms of aconite poisoning off the top of their heads?"

He opened his mouth.

He shut it again.

Across the room, Ivy Crane sat in his coat with her mother's handbag in her lap, watching them both. Somewhere behind the double doors, a woman who had raised a glass to the vanishing man two nights ago was lying under a sheet. And Archie Fairweather, who had spent his whole adult life making sure that every eye in the room was exactly where he wanted it, realized that he had just made himself the most interesting man in the building to the Maine State Police.
