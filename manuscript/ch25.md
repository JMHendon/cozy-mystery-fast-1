# Chapter 25

## Owls Head

Knox County Regional Airport was the smallest airport Archie had ever loved on sight.

It sat on a flat point of land in Owls Head, south of Rockland, with the bay on two sides and a windsock standing straight out over the runway. The terminal was one story of brown clapboard with a flagpole and a bench. Inside, a woman in a fleece weighed bags on a scale that might have come from a feed store, one officer staffed a security lane consisting of a folding table, and a vending machine had run out of everything but peanut-butter crackers. Out on the apron, a white twin-engine plane about the size of a pickup truck with ambitions sat in the gray morning while a boy in ear protectors fueled it. Nine seats. Archie had counted the windows.

It was ten minutes to eight on Wednesday. The fog was coming off the water in rags, and the sky was low and white, the kind that meant rain by noon. He'd parked the hearse across two spaces in the short-term lot, where a family from Connecticut was now photographing it, and come in with nothing in his pockets but a deck of cards, a pen, and the watch.

He stood at the glass and wound the watch. It had been Gus's. It lost four minutes a day no matter what anyone did to it, and he loved it more than anything he owned that wasn't alive. Twelve turns. In the glass he could see Teddy Marchand at the end of the second row of chairs, with a roller bag between his white sneakers, a phone in each hand, and an unlit vape between two fingers that he kept raising to his mouth and lowering again with a look of real grief.

Teddy saw him in the glass.

"Archie!" He was on his feet before Archie had turned around. "Oh, wow. I was literally about to call you. This is — honestly, this is a sign. Sit. Sit, sit." He patted the chair beside his.

Archie sat.

"Documentary," said Teddy. "Ninety minutes. Prestige. Not the show — the show's done, God rest her. The *real* story. Working title, *Crane*. The medium and the magician. The bet, the respect, the night in the dark. You held her while she—" He touched his chest with a phone. "I've got a call with a streamer at two. If I can tell them you're in, it's not a pitch anymore. It's a deal."

"I'm in," said Archie.

Teddy's mouth stayed open.

"An exclusive," said Archie. "My first sit-down since Las Vegas. You can even ask me about the Glass Box. I won't answer, but you can ask." He smiled. "In exchange for Marco's card."

Teddy's face went still for half a second, and then went pleasant. Archie had watched four hundred mediums do that in the half second after the right question.

"What card?"

"The handheld. Saturday night. Marco shot the table for the whole quarter of an hour before the séance. I watched him do it."

"Exteriors." Teddy shook his head, sorry for him. "Harbor, lighthouse, the drive in on Route 1, the town. I told the lieutenant. That card's in Los Angeles, Archie. It went out with the drives on Sunday."

"I was there, Teddy. He shot the candles."

"He shot the candles and then he went outside and shot the harbor."

"You told a lieutenant of the Maine State Police, in a homicide, that a piece of evidence was in California." Archie said it pleasantly. "I don't know what they call that up here. I'd bet Lieutenant Brannock knows. I'd bet he can spell it."

"Are you threatening me?"

"I'm describing your weekend."

Teddy looked out at the plane. The boy in the ear protectors was coiling the fuel hose. "Get a lawyer to tell you what you'd be describing," he said. "I've got three."

"Friday night," said Archie, "I was walking out the back of the Spindrift past a half-open door, and I heard you on the phone. The key-person policy. Runs through the end of the season. That's the point."

For a second Teddy looked genuinely lost. Then he laughed, short and not happily.

"You think I — oh, Archie. Archie, Archie." He leaned back in the chair. "Okay. Here's the policy. It's on the company's books, and every dollar it pays goes straight back to the network, for the episodes I now can't deliver. It makes *them* whole. It makes me nothing. You know what her dying got me?" He counted on the edge of his phone. "A twelve-city live tour in March — arenas — canceled. A spinoff with a dead star. Seasons nine and ten in a drawer, waiting on a signature I'm never getting. I'm not a suspect, buddy. I'm a casualty."

"She was quitting," said Archie.

"She quit every August. I talked her back every September. Eight years." Teddy shrugged. "It's the job. I talk people out of things."

"Then why lie about the card?"

Teddy was quiet. He turned the unlit vape over in his fingers.

"Because I've watched it," he said. "Sunday morning, in my room, with the door locked. At seven forty-four the daughter comes back up the stairs on her own and slides a stick under the tablecloth. A telescoping rod, with a glove on the end. So she can touch Warren Gilley on the ear in the dark." He looked at Archie. "You hand that to the police, it's evidence. It's in a courtroom. It's on the news, frame by frame, with a little red circle around her hands. Eight seasons, a hundred and four episodes, eleven international territories, and the whole library turns into a girl with a stick. That's not a show anymore. That's a class action."

"And Ivy sits in a cell."

"Ivy wiped the thing with her scarf in front of you. That's not on me."

They sat. A man in a Patriots jacket came in out of the cold and asked the woman at the counter if the Boston flight was going, and she said it was, and he went and sat by the vending machine.

"I found her, you know," said Teddy. He wasn't looking at Archie. He was looking at the little plane. "Worcester. A Knights of Columbus hall. Folding chairs, a raffle table. She did forty minutes for maybe sixty people, and then she sat with every one of them after, and she remembered all their names. I'd driven out from Boston to laugh at her." He turned the vape over again. "Eleven years. I liked her, okay? That's the stupid part. I really liked her."

Archie had liked her too, and he'd known her two days.

Teddy put the vape in one vest pocket and unzipped the other. He took out a little hard plastic case, the size of a matchbook, and opened it on his knee. Inside, on a bed of gray foam, sat a black memory card.

"Here's what happened," he said, in a flatter voice, one that had spent a lot of time on the phone with lawyers. "I was packing this morning, and I found this in Marco's kit. I'm a private citizen about to get on a plane, handing it to a witness. What you do with it is between you and the State of Maine." He closed the case. He didn't hand it over. "You never got it from me. You got it from Marco's kit."

"Fine."

"And I want something."

"The documentary. I said yes."

"There's no documentary. Not after this." Teddy held the case up between two fingers. "How'd you do the Glass Box?"

Archie had seen it coming since he'd made the joke himself, ten minutes earlier. You didn't mention the thing a man wanted most unless you were ready to be asked for it. He had seen it coming, and it landed anyway.

The Glass Box was the last thing he'd done in Las Vegas: a clear case on a bare, bright stage, three thousand people watching him step into it, and then a clear case with nobody in it, and Archie on a plane to Boston to sit in the third row of his daughter's debate final. Four people on earth knew how it worked — Lenny, who had built it; two stagehands who had signed papers thicker than the box; and Archie. That night in Boston, an hour before he promised her a real year, June had looked up from her room-service fries and asked how he'd gotten out, and he'd said, *Magic, Junebug,* and thought it was charming.

"I know what I'm asking," said Teddy. "I'm not stupid. I've watched that tape three hundred times. My kid's got a theory with mirrors."

"It's not mirrors."

"Don't tell me what it isn't. Tell me what it is."

Archie looked at the case between Teddy's fingers. He thought about Ivy in the back seat of the Interceptor with her hands behind her, sitting up straight. He thought about her bare throat.

He got up and went to the coffee counter by the security lane and came back with a paper napkin. He clicked his pen and wrote on it in capitals, pressing hard so it wouldn't tear:

*I, THEODORE MARCHAND, WILL NEVER TELL, SELL, FILM, SKETCH, IMPLY, HINT AT OR HUM THE METHOD OF THE GLASS BOX TO ANY LIVING PERSON, IN ANY MEDIUM NOW KNOWN OR HEREAFTER DEVISED.*

"'Hereafter devised,'" said Teddy. "Who's your lawyer?"

"The best one in New England. I'm paying her a great deal of money as of last night." He held out the pen. "Sign it."

Teddy signed it. Archie signed under him, folded the napkin twice, and put it in his breast pocket. Then he leaned in close, so that the man in the Patriots jacket couldn't have heard if he'd tried, and told Teddy Marchand how the Glass Box worked.

It took about ninety seconds.

He watched Teddy's face while he talked. He'd seen it before, on the very few people he'd ever told anything: first the hunger, then the attention, the eyes narrowing, following it, keeping up — and then, right at the end, when the last piece went in, something that fell. Every method ever invented was smaller than its trick. It had never once stopped hurting to watch.

"That's it?" said Teddy.

"That's it."

"Huh." Teddy sat back. He looked out at the plane, and at Archie, and at the plane again. "Huh."

He held out the little case. Archie took it.

Over the speaker, the woman in the fleece announced that Boston was boarding. Teddy gathered his phones and his roller bag and went through security at the folding table. At the door onto the apron he turned around, and for a second Archie thought he might say something else.

"Huh," said Teddy, and went out across the wet tarmac to the plane.

Archie stood at the glass and watched the little white plane run, lift off over the bay, and go up into the low cloud.

The card was in his breast pocket, behind the napkin. He could feel the corner of the case through his shirt. Lieutenant Brannock was going to kill him.

Archie walked back out to the hearse past the family from Connecticut, who asked if they could get one with him in it, and he said yes, and stood by the gold lettering and smiled for them while the first rain came in off the bay.
