# Chapter 4

## Sea View

On Friday afternoons, Archie Fairweather did magic for people who would not remember it.

Sea View sat on the hill above town, a long white 1920s summer hotel with green shutters and a porch full of rocking chairs that nobody rocked in after Labor Day. Inside, it had become a memory-care residence — locked doors with keypads, a nurses' station where the front desk used to be, a smell of coffee and lemon cleaner and, faintly, of the sea. The common room had been the hotel's ballroom. It still had the tall windows, and on a bright cold October afternoon like this one, the light came off the bay in sheets and lay across the old maple floor like something spilled.

He'd started coming in September. Nobody had asked him to. He'd been walking past one afternoon and seen through the windows a room full of people sitting in front of a television showing a cooking competition, and he'd gone in and asked the woman at the nurses' station if he could do a few card tricks, and she'd looked at him and said, *You're him, aren't you,* and then, *Are there going to be cameras?* There weren't. There never were. That was the rule, and it was the only rule in his life he had never once broken.

Today there were eleven residents in the semicircle of armchairs, an aide named Marisol doing a word search by the window, and a piano nobody played.

"Pick a card," Archie said to a tiny woman in a pink cardigan. "Any card. Don't let me see it."

She picked one, studied it with tremendous seriousness, and showed it to the man beside her, who announced, "Seven of hearts," to the room.

"Thank you, Raymond," said Archie. "Very helpful."

Everybody laughed except Raymond, who didn't know why everybody was laughing, and then laughed anyway because they were. In this room it didn't matter if they followed the trick. It mattered that for a few minutes something was happening, and it was happening for them.

He put the seven of hearts back in the deck, shuffled, and asked the tiny woman to say her husband's name.

"Bernard," she said, without hesitation, and then her face clouded. "He's not here. He went to get the car."

"Then we'll do it for Bernard." Archie fanned the deck face down. "Tap one."

She tapped one. He turned it over: the seven of hearts. Across the back of it, in ballpoint, in handwriting that was clearly not Archie's, someone had written *BERNARD*.

The tiny woman put both hands over her mouth.

Down at the end of the semicircle, a different small woman — younger than the others, in her early sixties, with neat gray hair and a cardigan buttoned wrong — had begun to cry, quietly, without any change in her face. Marisol got up from the window and went to her.

"Is Warren coming?" the woman asked. "Is it Tuesday? He comes on Tuesday."

"It's Friday, Carol," said Marisol gently. "He'll be here Tuesday."

"He went to get the car."

"I know, hon."

Archie went on with the next trick, a little louder, because sometimes that helped. He didn't know Carol's story. He knew a few of their names, and which ones liked cards and which liked coins, and that the big man in the wheelchair by the piano liked best of all to talk about Archie's grandfather.

"Gus," said Walt Calloway, as Archie came over. "Gus Fairweather."

"That's right, Chief."

"You're his boy."

"His grandson. Archie."

"Archie." Walt nodded slowly. He was seventy-four, and had been the police chief of Merrow for twenty-nine years, and even sitting down he was enormous: broad shoulders under a plaid flannel shirt, a big square head with white hair cut short, and hands like catcher's mitts that shook a little now, all the time. His eyes were blue and clear, and when they focused on you, it was like being looked at by a lighthouse. When they didn't, he was somewhere else. Usually 1988.

"He did the Chair," said Walt. "The last time. At the opera house."

"I was there," said Archie. "Second row."

"I was in the back. I always stood in the back. In case." Walt's big hand moved on the chair arm. "He came out after. In the cloak. He shook my hand." The blue eyes went somewhere past Archie's shoulder, to the window, to the bay. "Gus Fairweather still owes me a boat."

Archie laughed. He'd heard Walt say a lot of things about Gus on Fridays — that Gus could hold his breath longer than any man alive, that Gus had once made a police cruiser vanish from the lot behind the town hall as a birthday present for the dispatcher — and this was a new one. "Does he? I'll see what I can do."

"He knows," said Walt, and closed his eyes for a moment, and opened them again, and was back. "Show me one."

Archie showed him one. He did the thing with the three coins that turn into four and then into two, slowly, and Walt watched with his lips moving, trying to catch it, and didn't, and grunted with satisfaction. Archie was shuffling for the next one when the door from the hall opened and a woman came in fast, pulling off her gloves.

He knew she was a cop before he knew anything else about her. She came through the door and had the whole room in about a second and a half — exits, aide, residents, him — without moving her head. She was in her late thirties, tall, with dark-blond hair in a single braid down her back, a charcoal wool coat over a navy fleece, jeans and good boots. No jewelry but a plain steel watch. Gray eyes.

The gray eyes found him and stayed there.

"Dad," she said, without taking them off Archie. "It's Tess."

"Tessie." Walt's whole face changed. "You're early. Is school out?"

Her mouth tightened and let go. "School's out," she said, and bent and kissed the top of his head, and then straightened up and looked at Archie across her father's wheelchair as though he was something that had been left on the floor.

"Can I talk to you?" she said. "In the hall."

"I'm in the middle of a—"

"In the hall."

He went into the hall. She followed, shut the door, and stood with her back to it.

"I'm Detective Sergeant Calloway," she said. "Maine State Police. That's my father."

"Archie Fairweather." He put out his hand. She looked at it until he took it back. "Your father's a great guy. He's been telling me about my grandfather. He says Gus owes him a boat."

"How long have you been coming here?"

"Since September. Fridays."

"Who set it up?"

"Nobody. I walked in."

"Who's filming it?"

"Nobody's filming it."

"Mr. Fairweather." Her voice was level. "I know who you are. Everybody in Maine knows who you are. You walked out of your own show in Las Vegas and turned up in my hometown and bought the opera house and you've been in the *Portland Press Herald* three times since August. My father has Alzheimer's. Most days he doesn't know what year it is. He thinks I'm fifteen. He is not a — prop. He's not a heartwarming segment. If there's a camera crew somewhere in this building and I find it, I'm going to—"

"There's no camera," said Archie.

She looked at him. Then she walked past him into the common room, and he watched through the glass in the door as she went around it, corner by corner. She checked the window seats. She checked behind the piano. She checked the coat rack and the bookshelf and the top of the television, and she said something to Marisol, who shook her head and looked offended.

She came back out.

"There's no camera," she said.

"I told you."

"People tell me things all day." She put her gloves in her coat pocket. "Why do you come here?"

He thought about giving her the answer he gave reporters, which was charming and fifteen seconds long and true enough. Instead he said, "Because nobody here cares who I am. They don't know. They don't remember from week to week. Every Friday I walk in and I'm just a fella with a deck of cards." He shrugged. "Do you know how long it's been since anybody looked at me and didn't know the ending?"

She didn't answer. Behind her, through the glass, Walt Calloway was patting the pockets of his flannel shirt with his big shaking hands, looking for something.

"Excuse me," said Archie. "I think your dad wants his trick."

He went back in. Tess Calloway followed him and stood by the wall with her arms folded.

"Chief," said Archie, crouching beside the wheelchair, "I need to borrow something from your daughter. Something every police officer always has on them." He held up one hand and snapped his fingers in the air. "Sergeant, could you check your pen?"

She didn't move. Then, slowly, she put two fingers into the inside pocket of her coat. Her face did not change, which he had to admire. The pocket was empty.

"Chief," said Archie, "would you check your shirt?"

Walt fumbled at his breast pocket. It took him a moment. Then his big trembling fingers came out holding a black ballpoint pen, MAINE STATE POLICE stamped along the side in gold, and he held it up to the light and looked at it, and at his daughter, and at Archie.

And Walt Calloway laughed.

It came up out of him like something that had been stuck in a pipe for a long time — a huge, startled, rumbling laugh that filled the old ballroom and made the tiny woman in pink jump and then start laughing too. Raymond laughed. Marisol, at the window, laughed. Walt sat there in his wheelchair with the pen in his hand and laughed until there were tears in his clear blue eyes, and said, "Gus used to do that. Gus used to do that to me," and laughed some more.

Archie looked at Tess Calloway.

She was standing by the wall, watching her father. Her arms had come uncrossed, and one hand was pressed flat against the front of her coat, and her mouth wasn't steady.

Then she saw him looking, and it steadied.

She came over, took the pen gently out of her father's hand — "Thanks, Dad" — and put it back in her pocket. On the way past Archie, without looking at him, she said, very low, "Do that again and I'll arrest you for theft of state property."

"Noted."

"I'm serious."

"I know. That's the best part."

She gave him a look that should have frozen the bay. Then she sat down in the empty armchair next to her father and took his big hand in both of hers, and Archie went back to the semicircle, and when he next looked over she was talking to Walt about the Red Sox and Walt was answering, and it was plainly some long-ago October for both of them, and neither of them minded.

She caught him at the door when he was leaving.

"The séance," she said. "Tomorrow night. At the opera house."

"You heard."

"Everybody heard. My lieutenant heard in Augusta." She held out a business card between two fingers. "I'm going to say this once. Don't make it a circus."

"It's already a circus, Sergeant. It's got a medium, a TV crew, a ghost and a two-million-dollar bet. I'm just the guy bringing the elephant."

She didn't smile. She didn't put the card away either.

"For when somebody gets hurt," she said.

He took it. *Det. Sgt. Theresa Calloway, Major Crimes Unit,* and a cell number. He put it in his breast pocket, and she watched him do it, and then she turned on her heel and went back into the common room.

Walt Calloway was watching from his wheelchair by the piano. As Archie lifted a hand to say goodbye, the old chief beckoned him over, and Archie went.

"That's my daughter," Walt said, with enormous pride, pointing across the room at Tess, who was arguing with the TV remote. "Tessie. She's fifteen."

Archie looked at her: State Police, braid, boots, the thousand-yard stare of a woman who checked behind pianos for cameras.

"She's terrifying, Chief," he said.

Walt Calloway nodded slowly, satisfied.

"Ayuh," he said.
