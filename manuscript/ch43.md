# Chapter 43

## The Memorial

At five o'clock the sun went down behind the town, and the harbor went from gold to pewter to slate, and Archie Fairweather was standing at the end of the town wharf in front of a block of granite with his hands in his pockets.

He had walked home from the Galley and slept for three hours with the cat on his chest, and woken in a house so quiet — his mother asleep upstairs, June out with Mattie, Lenny's door shut — that he'd gotten up and put his coat back on. He'd walked down past the Galley, closed now, and the bait shed and the stacked crates and the fuel dock, where a scrap of yellow tape still fluttered on the ramp rail, and kept walking until there wasn't any more wharf.

The granite was cold. He had put his hand on it without meaning anything by it, and left it there.

*Peter Langley 2003.* It was about two-thirds of the way down the stone, in letters a little worn but still sharp. He had read it twice since August, once with Shannon and once with Tess, and he hadn't seen it either time, because there had been nothing to see. It was a name on a stone, in a town that had been cutting names into this stone since 1847.

At the bottom, alone, the newest name was still pale inside the letters.

He heard her coming before he saw her: boots on the planks, unhurried, all the way down the wharf from the lot. He didn't turn around. She stopped beside him, a little to one side, and looked at the granite, and didn't say anything while the bell buoy rang twice out in the dusk.

"They found Peter's notebook," Tess said. "In her piano bench, under the sheet music. Forty-one Thursdays." She was quiet a moment. "I read it this afternoon. I'm not going to read it again."

Archie nodded.

"I knew Danny," she said. "Danny Langley. He was a grade ahead of me. He played Puck in the summer show at the opera house when I was twelve, and he got stuck on the rope coming down out of the flies, and he just hung there in the air laughing until everybody in the building was laughing with him." She looked at the name. "I'd forgotten that. I'd completely forgotten it until yesterday."

"I'm sorry."

"I'm not telling you so you'll be sorry." She turned and looked at him then. "I'm not going to tell you what you risked, either. Bev's told you. Brannock's going to tell you on Monday, in writing, and you're going to sign it." Her voice was low and level. "I want to tell you what it was like."

"Okay."

"I had your collar in my hand." She looked down at her right hand, and opened it, and closed it. "Ozzie was on the line and the swell had us and you had her, and I had your collar. And after about a minute I couldn't feel my fingers. I couldn't feel anything. I didn't know if I was still holding on. I had to look at my hand to find out." She stopped. "I kept looking. All the way in. I looked at my hand the whole way in."

Archie looked at it now. There was a raw red line across the backs of her fingers where the gunwale had been, and the knuckles were split.

The bell buoy rang. A gull came down onto a piling at the end of the wharf and folded itself up and looked at them.

Archie didn't make a joke. He thought of three. He didn't make any of them.

"I should have called you at five in the morning from the Galley," he said. "Bev told me to. I didn't, because I wanted to hear it from Hollis first, and then because she asked me for the show and I wanted her to have it. I wanted her to have Gus's lights." He looked at the granite. "That was for me. Not for her. I'm sorry, Tess."

She looked at him. Some of the stiffness went out of her shoulders. Not all of it.

"Brannock wants to know if you'd consult," she said.

He stared at her.

"Officially. On what he calls *the weird ones*." The corner of her mouth moved. "He says if this is going to keep happening in his jurisdiction he'd rather have you inside the tent than outside it setting things on fire. He said it this morning at the command post, standing in front of your space heater, which he has not sent back to Portland." She shrugged. "I told him I'd ask."

"What do you think I should say?"

"I think you should think about it for longer than it takes you to say yes."

"Yes," said Archie.

She closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them she wasn't angry anymore.

"Hold out your hand," she said.

"Why?"

"Just hold it out."

He took his left hand out of his coat pocket and held it out. She took it by the wrist and turned it palm down. Then she reached into her own coat pocket and took out a watch.

It was his. It was Gus's — an old steel Hamilton with a cracked crystal and a worn leather strap, which had been on his grandfather's wrist at every show for forty years and on Archie's since he was nineteen. He hadn't noticed it was gone. He looked at his bare wrist, and then at the watch in her hand, and for a second he was eight years old at a birthday party, watching a man pull a dove out of an empty hat.

"You were in the water," said Tess. "When I got hold of you. You had one arm around Hollis and the other on the gunwale, and the strap was half off your wrist. It was going to go to the bottom." She was buckling it onto him as she talked, her cold fingers working the old leather through the buckle. "So I took it."

"You *lifted* it."

"I took it."

"Off a drowning man. In the middle of a rescue. With one hand." He was grinning now. "Sergeant Calloway. That's a *lift*."

"I've watched every one of your specials," said Tess. "Some of them twice." She pulled the strap through the keeper and settled the watch on his wrist and, for a second, didn't let go. Her fingers stayed on the inside of his wrist, cold, over his pulse, which he was fairly sure she could feel. She was close enough that he could see each eyelash, and the gray of her eyes, and the place at the corner of her mouth that had almost smiled.

She let go.

"Don't tell anyone," said Tess.

He had said those words a thousand times, to a thousand volunteers, at the end of a trick. They went into him now and stopped somewhere, like something he had known once and put down and couldn't, for the life of him, remember where.

"Tess—"

But she had already turned. She walked away up the wharf, unhurried, her braid swinging and her hands in her coat pockets, past the fuel dock and the crates and the dark windows of the Galley, toward the lights of the town. She didn't look back. He was almost certain she was smiling.

Archie stood alone at the end of the wharf with his grandfather's watch ticking on his wrist and his own pulse going under it where her fingers had been. Out past the point, Merrow Light flashed green, and went out, and flashed again, and the bell buoy off Shag Ledge rang once, and then again, slow, on a calm sea.
