# Chapter 43

## The Memorial

At five o'clock the sun went down behind the town, and the harbor went from gold to pewter to slate, and Archie Fairweather was standing at the end of the town wharf in front of a block of granite with his hands in his pockets.

He hadn't meant to come here. He'd meant to go home — Tess had dropped him at Fairweather House at three, and he'd gone in and stood in the kitchen with the cat winding around his ankles and his mother asleep upstairs and June out somewhere with Mattie, and the house had been so quiet that he'd turned around and gotten back in the hearse. He'd driven to the wharf without deciding to. He'd walked past the Galley, closed now, and the bait shed and the stacked lobster crates and the fuel dock, where there was still a scrap of yellow police tape fluttering on the ramp rail, and he'd kept walking until there wasn't any more wharf.

The memorial was cold to the touch. He'd touched it.

*Peter Langley 2003.* It was about two-thirds of the way down, in letters a little worn but still sharp. He'd walked past it a dozen times since August. He'd read it at least twice — once on the first Friday with Shannon, once on Tuesday morning with Tess — and he hadn't seen it, either time, because there'd been nothing to see. It was just a name on a stone, in a town that had been carving names on this stone since 1847.

At the bottom, alone, the newest name was still pale inside the letters.

He heard her coming before he saw her: boots on the planks, unhurried, all the way down the wharf from the parking lot. He didn't turn around. She stopped beside him, a little to one side, and looked at the granite, and didn't say anything for long enough that the bell buoy rang twice, out in the dusk.

"They found Peter's notebook," Tess said finally. "In her piano bench. Under the sheet music. Forty-one Thursdays." She was quiet a moment. "I read it this afternoon. I'm not going to read it again."

Archie nodded.

"I knew Danny," she said. "Danny Langley. He was a grade ahead of me. He played Puck in the summer show at the opera house when I was twelve, and he got stuck on the rope coming down out of the flies, and he just hung there in the air laughing until everybody in the whole building was laughing with him." She looked at the name. "I'd forgotten that. I'd completely forgotten it until yesterday."

"I'm sorry."

"I'm not telling you so you'll be sorry." She turned and looked at him then, and her gray eyes were very steady, and very angry. "I'm telling you so you'll understand what I'm about to say."

"Okay."

"You had a sealed confession to murder in your coat for five hours yesterday." Her voice was level and low, and every word landed. "You knew who'd done it at five o'clock in the morning. You knew, and you went to her house alone, and you let her walk into a building full of three hundred people, and you sent me two words." She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to. "Ivy Crane walked out of Knox County Jail at ten o'clock this morning. Nobody gets released on a Friday night. Nothing you did or didn't do changed one minute of that. So don't tell yourself you risked Ivy, because you didn't. That's not what you risked."

"I know."

"You risked *Hollis.* She was out on that ledge with no life jacket, Archie, an hour before we'd have had her. You risked that jar in her shed. She had the rest of the afternoon alone in that house after you drove away. She could have poured it down the sink. And you risked *you.*" Her voice went, for the first time, very slightly unsteady. "You went into that water at night with a sixty-six-year-old woman who was trying to push you away, and if Ozzie Lund hadn't put a line on you, you'd be on that stone. I had my hand on your collar. I couldn't feel my fingers. I couldn't feel anything."

The bell buoy rang. A gull came down onto a piling at the end of the wharf and folded itself up and looked at them.

Archie didn't make a joke. He thought of three. He didn't make any of them.

"You're right," he said. "About all of it. I should have called you at five in the morning from the Galley. Bev told me to. I didn't, because I wanted to hear it from Hollis first, and then because she asked me for the show and I wanted her to have it. I wanted her to have Gus's lights." He looked at the granite. "That was for me. Not for her. I'm sorry, Tess."

She looked at him for a long moment.

Something went out of her shoulders. Not all of it. Some.

"Brannock wants to know if you'd consult," she said.

He stared at her.

"Officially. On — he called them *the weird ones.*" Her mouth did something very small at the corner. "He says if this is going to keep happening in his jurisdiction, he'd rather have you inside the tent than outside it, setting things on fire. He said it this morning, at the command post, standing in front of your space heater, which he has not sent back to Portland." She shrugged. "I told him I'd ask."

"What do you think I should say?"

"I think you should think about it," said Tess, "for longer than it takes you to say yes."

"Yes," said Archie.

She closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, though, she wasn't angry anymore. She was looking at him the way she had from the back of the opera house the night before — the way she'd watched him across her father's wheelchair on the first day — as if she were trying to work out how he did it.

"Hold out your hand," she said.

"Why?"

"Just hold it out."

He took his left hand out of his coat pocket and held it out. She took it by the wrist and turned it over, palm down. Then she reached into her own coat pocket and took out a watch.

It was his. It was Gus's — an old steel Hamilton with a cracked crystal and a worn leather strap, which had been on his grandfather's wrist at every show for forty years and on Archie's since he was nineteen. He hadn't noticed it was gone. He looked at his bare wrist and then at the watch in her hand, and felt his face do something it hadn't done since he was a boy at a birthday party watching a man pull a dove out of an empty hat.

"You were in the water," said Tess. "When I got hold of you. You had one arm around Hollis and the other one on the gunwale, and the strap was half off your wrist. It was going to go to the bottom." She was buckling it onto his wrist as she talked, carefully, her cold fingers working the old leather through the buckle. "So I took it."

"You *lifted* it."

"I took it."

"Off a drowning man. In the middle of a rescue. With one hand." He was grinning now; he couldn't help it. "Sergeant Calloway. That's a *lift*."

"I've watched every one of your specials," said Tess. "Some of them twice." She pulled the strap through the keeper and settled the watch on his wrist and, for a second, didn't let go. Her fingers stayed where they were, cold, on the inside of his wrist, over his pulse, which he was fairly sure she could feel. She was very close. The dusk had gone blue around them, and the first lights were coming on along the moorings, and he could see each separate eyelash and the gray of her eyes and the place at the corner of her mouth that had almost smiled.

She let go.

"Don't tell anyone," said Tess.

And something went through Archie Fairweather that he couldn't account for at all.

It wasn't the words. They were ordinary words. He'd said them himself a thousand times, to a thousand volunteers, with a wink, at the end of a trick. But the way she said them — quietly, looking straight at him, as though she were handing them back to him after a long time — stopped him where he stood, the way a name had stopped him in the dark a week ago, the way his hands had stopped in the straitjacket. As if there was something he'd known once and put down somewhere and couldn't, for the life of him, remember where.

"Tess—"

But she'd already turned. She walked away from him up the wharf, unhurried, her braid swinging, her hands in her coat pockets, past the fuel dock and the crates and the dark windows of the Galley, toward the lights of the town. She didn't look back. He was almost certain she was smiling.

Archie stood alone at the end of the wharf with his grandfather's watch ticking on his wrist and his own pulse going under it where her fingers had been.

Out past the point, in the gathering dark, Merrow Light flashed green, and went out, and flashed again. And the bell buoy off Shag Ledge rang, once, and then again, the way it did on a calm night when there was nothing out there at all — just a little swell coming in, slow, from very far away.
