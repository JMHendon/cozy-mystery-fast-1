# Chapter 44

## Sparrow

Supper on Saturday night was just supper, which at Fairweather House had never once happened before.

Nobody tried to cook anything clever. Vivienne made a pot of the soup she'd been making since 1971, which had no name and no recipe. Ozzie Lund brought bread from the bakery in Camden and stood in the kitchen doorway holding it until somebody told him to sit down. Lenny came in from the carriage house wearing one sweater fewer than usual, which in Lenny was the equivalent of a party hat. And at seven o'clock the back door opened without a knock and Bev Thibodeau came in out of the dark with a pie in each hand and set them on the counter.

"Blueberry," she said, "and apple."

"You brought both," said Archie.

"There's no third pie," said Bev, and sat down, and let June take her coat.

They ate at the scarred farm table with the windows black, and the jug of blue flowers gone from the middle of it, and nobody mentioned why. They talked about the show. They talked about Lionel. Ozzie told a long, slow story about a summer person who had once tried to moor a forty-foot yacht to the bell buoy, and Vivienne laughed until she had to put her spoon down, and Ozzie looked at her laughing as if she'd invented it.

Malvolio sat on the table.

He had arrived at midnight the night before in a cardboard box, and in eighteen hours he had established that the radiator under the kitchen window was his, that Archie's chest was a bed, and that the farm table was a place where a large gray cat might reasonably sit among the bread and the butter dish with his paws folded under him, watching the soup.

"There is a cat," said Lenny, "on the table."

"He's not on the table," said Archie. "He's adjacent to the table."

"He's on the table."

"He's visiting."

"Hollis said he'll eat anything except fish," said June.

"Then he's the only sensible creature in this house," said Lenny, and gave Malvolio a piece of bread without looking at him.

June was sitting across from Archie. She had said almost nothing to him all day. She'd slept until noon and gone out with Mattie and come back at six with her hair blown everywhere and her cheeks red from the wind, and when he'd said *Hi, Junebug* she'd said *Hi* and gone upstairs. He'd let her. He hadn't known what else to do.

But she was watching him now, across the soup, the way she watched an opponent across a debate table in the minute before the round began.

When the pie was gone and Bev had gone and Ozzie was drying dishes he hadn't been asked to dry, June put down her fork.

"I'm still mad at you," she said.

"I know."

"I'm going to be mad at you for a really long time."

"I know. You said."

"Okay." She looked at him a moment longer. Then she stood up. "Can I try something?"

---

The turret stairs were narrow and steep and turned twice, and halfway up, on the landing under the little round window, June stopped and turned around. He was two steps below her, which made them the same height.

"The Glass Box," she said. "In Vegas. How did you get out?"

The answer was right there. It had been there in a hotel room in Boston over a plate of room-service fries, and it was charming, and it was two words long, and it meant *no*.

He sat down on the step.

He told her. It took about four minutes, and he used his hands, and the banister, and a button off his own cuff, and she stood on the landing with her arms folded and didn't interrupt once. When he was finished she was quiet for a while.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's it. And eleven months of Lenny in a garage in Henderson, Nevada."

June nodded slowly, filing it somewhere. Then she turned and went on up the stairs, and he got up and followed her.

---

The turret was cold. It always was. Archie turned on the green-shaded lamp on Gus's rolltop desk, and the round room came up out of the dark around them — the shelves of conjuring books, the framed posters of the Great Fairweather sawing ladies in half and holding doves with tremendous dignity, the black windows on every side with the harbor in them and the town's lights scattered on the water.

And the trunk, under the north window, with its faded gold stars and its seven brass dials and the yellowed card on the lid. *To be opened when I'm gone. Not before.*

June knelt down in front of it.

He knew what she was going to do before her hand went out. He'd known since Tuesday night, when he'd sat on this floor himself and spelled out three letters — S, P, A — and stopped, because he would not let a woman who'd lied to grieving people for thirty years open his grandfather's trunk with a word she'd found in an old newspaper.

"June," he said. "That's her word."

June looked up at him.

"No, it isn't," she said. She took a folded sheet out of her cardigan pocket and held it up: a photocopy, gray and speckled, off the microfilm reader at the library. "Mattie and I went this afternoon. Deb opened up for us."

He didn't need to read it. He had read it upside down, from a wall, behind a pink tab in Ivy Crane's binder. *His grandson, Archie, whom he called his "little sparrow."*

"It was never her word," said June. "A reporter wrote it down in 1988 because Grandpa Gus said it to him." She laid the photocopy on the floor beside her knee. "It was always his. She just said it out loud. That's all she did." She put her fingers on the first brass dial. "Somebody has to say it out loud, Dad. You weren't going to."

He stood in the lamplight and didn't stop her.

She turned the dials one at a time. Each one clicked under her thumb as it went past each letter, a tiny, precise sound.

S.

P.

A.

R.

R.

O.

W.

She took hold of the brass hasp, and looked up at him once, and pulled.

The lock opened.

It opened so easily, with such a small and ordinary *clack*, that for a second neither of them moved. Then the door at the top of the stairs creaked, and his mother was standing in it with one hand on the frame, in her kaftan and her tweed coat.

June lifted the lid.

The trunk smelled of cedar and mothballs and, faintly, impossibly, of pipe tobacco and Brylcreem. Inside lay a pair of white kid opera gloves, yellowed, folded one on the other. Beside them was a deck of cards in a red case, worn soft at the corners, and beside that a square of heavy cream paper folded once, with one word on the outside in a big, looping, theatrical hand that Archie had last seen on a birthday card when he was ten.

*Sparrow.*

His hands weren't steady. He let June unfold it.

*Sparrow —*
*You remembered.*
*That's the whole trick.*
*— G.*

June read it out loud, and then read it again silently, and handed it to him without a word, and he stood holding it in the lamplight and for the moment could not see it very well.

"There's something else," said June.

It had been lying on top of everything, the last thing put in, face down. A postcard. June turned it over.

It was a picture postcard of a sunset, the kind they sell in racks on boardwalks: an orange sun sinking into a flat turquoise sea between two palm trees, and across the top in yellow cartoon letters, GREETINGS FROM KEY WEST. The colors had faded. One corner was soft from somebody's pocket.

June turned it over again.

It had been mailed. There was a thirty-two-cent stamp with a flag on it, and across the stamp a round black postmark, slightly smudged but perfectly legible: KEY WEST FL. MAR 14 1996.

It was addressed in the same big, looping hand: *Master Archie Fairweather, Fairweather House, Gull Point, Merrow, Maine.*

And on the left side, four lines:

*Sparrow —*
*Still working on the trick.*
*Don't let your mother sell the house.*
*— G.*

Nobody in the turret said anything.

Archie read the postmark three times. *1996.* He'd been eighteen, a year from his folding table in Harvard Square. Gus Fairweather had been gone eight years. The whole town had stood on the wharf in the rain for the memorial service. Walt Calloway had closed the case.

And somebody who knew the word had come into this house, sometime after the fourteenth of March, 1996, and opened this trunk, and put a postcard in it, and closed it again.

"That's his hand," said Vivienne, from the doorway. "That's my father's hand."

She came into the room and took the postcard from June and held it under the lamp. Archie had seen her play Lady Macbeth and Mother Courage and, once, unforgettably, a talking teapot, and he had never seen her look at anything the way she looked at that card.

"That old *showman,*" said Vivienne Fairweather.

She sat down very suddenly on the edge of Gus's desk chair.

"I told you," she said, to nobody, to the room. "I told you all. Nobody had opened it. I told you to draw your own conclusions."

Archie looked down at the note in his own hand. *You remembered. That's the whole trick.*

And he did remember — not 1988, not a kitchen with a screen door, but the night before. The fog off Shag Ledge. His own hands, without any instruction from him, unclasping his grandfather's opera cloak and folding it in thirds and laying it on the seat before he went over the side.

Thirty-seven years ago, at dawn, Walt Calloway's men had found Gus's skiff drifting near Merrow Light with nobody in it. And on the seat, in every story anybody ever told about that night, his black opera cloak.

Folded.

His mother was holding the postcard against her chest with both hands. June was kneeling by the open trunk in the lamplight, looking up at him, and her face wasn't mad at him at all just then. It was lit up from the inside like a struck match — the way his own must have been when he was four years old and a big man with silver hair crouched down in front of him in a summer kitchen and said *Watch closely, Sparrow.*

Archie grinned.

"Junebug," he said. "Get your coat."

June was already on her feet.

"Where are we going?"

"Sea View," said Archie, reaching for the opera cloak. "There's a man there I need to see about a boat."
