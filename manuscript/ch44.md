# Chapter 44

## Sparrow

Supper on Saturday night was just supper, which at Fairweather House had never once happened before.

Nobody tried to cook anything clever. Vivienne made a pot of the soup she'd been making since 1971, which had no name and no recipe and was different every time. Ozzie Lund brought bread from the bakery in Camden and stood in the kitchen doorway holding it until somebody told him to sit down. Lenny came in from the carriage house in three sweaters instead of four, which in Lenny was the equivalent of a party hat. And at seven o'clock, the back door opened without a knock and Bev Thibodeau came in out of the dark with a pie in each hand and set them on the counter.

"Blueberry," she said, "and apple."

"You brought both," said Archie.

"There's no third pie," said Bev, and sat down, and let June take her coat.

They ate in the kitchen at the scarred farm table, with the windows black and Merrow Light sweeping its green flash across the water every six seconds out past the point, and the jug of blue flowers gone from the middle of the table, and nobody mentioned why. They talked about the show. They talked about Lionel. Ozzie told a long, slow story about a summer person who'd once tried to moor a forty-foot yacht to the bell buoy, and Vivienne laughed until she had to put her spoon down, and Ozzie looked at her laughing as if she'd invented it.

Malvolio sat on the table.

He'd arrived at midnight the night before in a cardboard box, and in eighteen hours he had established that the radiator under the kitchen window was his, that Archie's chest was a bed, and that the farm table was a place where a large gray cat might reasonably sit, among the bread and the butter dish, with his paws folded under him, watching the soup.

"There is a cat," said Lenny, "on the table."

"He's not on the table," said Archie. "He's adjacent to the table."

"He's on the table."

"He's visiting."

"Hollis said he'll eat anything except fish," said June.

"Then he's the only sensible creature in this house," said Lenny, and gave Malvolio a piece of bread, without looking at him, as if it had happened by accident.

June was sitting across from Archie. She hadn't said much. She'd said almost nothing to him all day — she'd slept until noon and gone out with Mattie and come back at six with her hair blown everywhere and her cheeks red from the wind, and when he'd said *Hi, Junebug* she'd said *Hi* and gone upstairs. He'd let her. He hadn't known what else to do. He'd had a lot of practice, this week, at not knowing what else to do.

But she was watching him now, across the soup, in the way she watched an opponent across a debate table in the minute before the round began.

When the pie was gone and Bev had gone and Ozzie was drying dishes he hadn't been asked to dry, June put down her fork.

"I'm still mad at you," she said.

"I know."

"I'm going to be mad at you for a really long time."

"I know. You said."

"Okay." She looked at him a moment longer. Then she stood up. "Can I try something?"

---

The turret was cold. It always was. Archie turned on the green-shaded lamp on Gus's rolltop desk, and the round room came up out of the dark around them — the shelves of conjuring books, the framed posters of the Great Fairweather sawing ladies in half and holding doves with tremendous dignity, the black windows on every side with the harbor in them and the town's lights scattered on the water like spilled sequins.

And the trunk, under the north window, with its faded gold stars and its seven brass dials and the yellowed card on the lid. *To be opened when I'm gone. Not before.*

June knelt down in front of it.

Archie knew what she was going to do before her hand went out. He'd known since Tuesday night, when he'd sat on this floor himself with his fingers on the dials and spelled out three letters — S, P, A — and stopped, and taken his hands away, because he would not let a woman who'd lied to a grieving town for twenty years open his grandfather's trunk with a word she'd found in an old newspaper.

"June," he said. "That's her word."

June looked up at him.

"No, it isn't," she said. "It was never her word. She found it in the *Packet*. In 1988. A reporter wrote it down because Grandpa Gus said it to him." She turned back to the trunk. "It was always his. She just said it out loud. That's all she did." She put her fingers on the first brass dial. "Somebody has to say it out loud, Dad. You weren't going to."

He stood in the lamplight and didn't stop her.

She turned the dials one at a time. Each one clicked under her thumb as it went past each letter, a tiny, precise sound, like a watch being wound.

S.

P.

A.

R.

R.

O.

W.

She took hold of the brass hasp, and looked up at him once, and pulled.

The lock opened.

It opened so easily — so quietly, with such a small and ordinary *clack* — that for a second neither of them moved. Then the door at the top of the stairs creaked, and Archie turned, and his mother was standing in it with one hand on the frame, in her kaftan and her tweed coat, very still.

June lifted the lid.

The trunk smelled of cedar and mothballs and, faintly, impossibly, of pipe tobacco and Brylcreem. Inside lay a pair of white kid opera gloves, yellowed, folded neatly one on the other. Beside them was a deck of cards in a red case, worn soft at the corners. Beside that, a square of heavy cream paper folded once, with one word on the outside in a big, looping, theatrical hand that Archie had last seen on a birthday card when he was ten.

*Sparrow.*

His hands weren't steady. He let June unfold it.

*Sparrow —*
*You remembered.*
*That's the whole trick.*
*— G.*

That was all it said. June read it out loud, and then read it again silently, and then handed it to him without a word, and he stood holding it in the lamplight and found that he could not, for the moment, see it very well.

"There's something else," said June.

It had been lying on top of everything else — on top of the gloves and the cards, the last thing put in, face down. A postcard. June turned it over.

It was a picture postcard of a sunset, the kind they sell in racks on boardwalks: an orange sun sinking into a flat turquoise sea between two palm trees, and across the top in yellow cartoon letters, GREETINGS FROM KEY WEST. The colors had faded. One corner was soft, as if it had spent some time in somebody's pocket.

June turned it over again.

It had been mailed. There was a stamp — a thirty-two-cent stamp with a flag on it — and across the stamp a round black postmark, slightly smudged but perfectly legible: KEY WEST FL. MAR 14 1996.

It was addressed in the same big, looping, theatrical hand: *Master Archie Fairweather, Fairweather House, Gull Point, Merrow, Maine.*

And on the left side, in the same hand, four lines:

*Sparrow —*
*Still working on the trick.*
*Don't let your mother sell the house.*
*— G.*

Nobody in the turret said anything at all.

Archie read the postmark three times. *1996.* He'd been eighteen in 1996, a year away from his folding table in Harvard Square and the cups and balls for tourists outside the subway. Gus Fairweather had been gone eight years. The whole town had stood on the wharf in the rain for the memorial service. Walt Calloway had closed the case.

And somebody — somebody who knew the word — had come into this house, sometime after the fourteenth of March, 1996, and opened this trunk, and put a postcard in it, and closed it again.

"That's his hand," said Vivienne, from the doorway. Her voice was very quiet and very steady. "That's my father's hand."

She came into the room and took the postcard from June, and held it under the lamp, and looked at it for a long time. Her face did several things in a row, each of them very fast. Archie had seen her play Lady Macbeth and Mother Courage and, once, unforgettably, a talking teapot, and he had never in his life seen her face do what it did now.

"That old *showman,*" said Vivienne Fairweather.

She sat down very suddenly on the edge of Gus's desk chair.

"I told you," she said, to nobody, to the room. "I told you all. Nobody had opened it. I told you to draw your own conclusions."

Archie looked down at the cream-colored note in his own hand. *You remembered. That's the whole trick.*

And he did remember — not something from 1988, not something from a kitchen with a screen door, but something from the night before. The boat. The fog. Hollis going into the black water off Shag Ledge. His own hands, without any instruction from him at all, unclasping his grandfather's opera cloak and folding it — lining out, shoulders tucked in, in thirds, the collar on top, the silver clasp centered — and laying it on the seat beside the wheel before he went over the side.

He'd done it without thinking. Because that was what you did, if you'd grown up at Gus Fairweather's knee. That was what you did with a good cloak before you went to work. You didn't leave it in a heap.

Thirty-seven years ago, at dawn, Walt Calloway's men had found Gus's skiff drifting near Merrow Light with nobody in it. And on the seat, they said — it was in every story anyone ever told about that night, it was the detail that made people shiver — his black opera cloak, folded.

Folded.

A man who goes over the side by accident in the dark doesn't stop to fold his cloak.

Archie looked up from the note. His mother was holding the postcard against her chest with both hands. June was kneeling by the open trunk in the lamplight, looking up at him — and her face wasn't mad at him at all just then. It was lit up from the inside like a struck match, exactly the way his own must have been when he was four years old and a big man with silver hair crouched down in front of him in a summer kitchen and said *Watch closely, Sparrow.*

He felt the grin start somewhere around his ribs, and he didn't try to stop it.

"Junebug," said Archie Fairweather. "Get your coat."

June was already on her feet.

"Where are we going?"

"Sea View," said Archie, reaching for the opera cloak. "There's a man there I need to see about a boat."
