# Chapter 20

## Outside. Now.

She didn't stop at the bottom of the steps. She walked him the length of the town wharf — past the bait shed and the stacked traps and the fuel dock, all the way out to the end, where the planks stopped above the water — and she didn't say a word the whole way, and for once neither did he.

The fog was starting to lift. It had pulled back off the wharf and hung over the harbor in a low white bank, so that the nearest moorings showed, *Second Wind* and *Miss Dottie* dripping on their lines, and everything past them was only shapes. It was half past seven and cold enough to see his breath, and the granite memorial stood at the end of the wharf with the wet shining on it. Tess stopped beside it and turned around.

"Half that room can read lips," she said. "Keep your voice down."

"Sergeant—"

"Who printed those?"

"I did."

"Who found them?"

He didn't answer. She looked at him, and he watched her work it out and decide not to say it.

"Okay," she said. "Go ahead. Tell me."

He told her. He meant to tell it well — he'd told it beautifully fifteen minutes ago — but it didn't come out the same at the end of a wharf to one listener who wasn't leaning in. The whisper. Warren at his place in the half-dark with the horn at his elbow. The sudden stomach at a quarter to eight. The posters. The sale. He heard it getting thinner as he went, and went faster to make up for it, and finished with, "He had six minutes at that table, Tess, and every reason in the world to want her quiet."

"Are you done?"

"I think so."

"Warren Gilley walked into my command post at half past nine on Sunday night," Tess said. "Dale's back office, an hour after I left your poker game. Nobody asked him. Nobody had any idea. He sat down across the desk from me in his bow tie and told me he'd taken a poster out of the prop room Friday, and listed it Saturday morning, and taken the listing down Sunday because he was ashamed of himself. He told me about the three in September. He told me about Carol. He told me what Lorelei Crane said to him at the table, and why he went white. He did all of that before I'd asked him a single question, because he thought it was the right thing to do." She hadn't raised her voice once. "I've known since Sunday night. You've known for about ten hours, and you used it for a card trick."

"I didn't—" He stopped. He had.

"The rest of it is your theory," Tess said. "It isn't ours. You've got her dying off that trumpet. The lieutenant's got her dying off something she swallowed, and until a lab tells me different, so have I. Warren was never within reach of anything that woman swallowed. Ivy poured every drop she drank except your tea, and Warren didn't touch your tea." She looked at him. "As for his stomach, Dale watched him go. Dale says he was green to the ears. Dale stood at that stair door until he came back up at three minutes to eight, still green, and he sat down and didn't get up again until the lights went out. You were standing next to Dale when he went. You were talking about a riverboat."

*Nerves*, the chief had said, kindly, watching Warren go by with a hand on his stomach.

Archie looked at the granite. THOMAS MAYO, at the bottom, the letters still pale inside the cuts.

"And the posters were Gus's," said Tess. "He lent them to the opera house in 1985. There's a letter in the town office files that says so, on his stationery. They were never the town's to sell and they were never Warren's. They belong to Gus's estate." She paused. "That's your mother. I called her last night and told her. It's her decision whether there's a complaint."

"What did she say?"

"She asked if Warren was eating."

He laughed, and it sounded terrible, and he stopped.

"And Carol Gilley," said Tess, "is two doors down the hall from my father."

He turned and looked at her.

She stood with her hands in the pockets of the charcoal coat and her face turned partly to the water. The fog had laid a fine wet shine over her braid. She didn't look at him.

"She sits with him at lunch. She thinks he's her brother Paul. He lets her. On Tuesdays Warren brings her flowers, and he brings Dad a roll of Necco wafers, because Dad likes the brown ones and nobody else on earth does." Her voice had gone Maine-flat, every corner sanded off. "I've known Warren Gilley my whole life. He wrote the policy on our house. He's a pompous old man with a bad haircut, and he never did a dishonest thing in his life until this year. And you stood up in the Galley in front of forty people and called him a murderer with a magic trick."

"I didn't call him—"

"You didn't have to. You built it so you wouldn't have to." She looked at him then. "I know how a trick works, Archie. I've watched yours."

There was nothing to say to that. He said something anyway.

"All right. Not Warren. I was wrong about Warren. I'll be wrong about Warren in writing if you like, in the *Packet*, a full page." He took a breath. "Kev Duchesne."

Tess closed her eyes.

"Eleven thousand subscribers on Friday. Six hundred thousand this morning. I looked. He's got a mattress sponsor. Who wins the contest to sit in the most-watched séance in America? A nobody from Haverhill with a cartoon of himself on his chest. Nobody wins those contests, Tess. They're rigged. I've rigged them. So what if he's not a nobody? What if he's a plant — a rival network, one of the streamers—"

"Kev Duchesne," said Tess, opening her eyes, "sweated through his sweatshirt in my interview on Sunday. He asked me if I could get him your autograph. He cried when I asked for his home address." She took a breath. "He's lying to me about something. I don't know what yet. He's not a spy."

She was still angry, and he couldn't bear her being angry. He stepped in close and lifted his right hand to make a point about rival networks, and his left went to the slash pocket of her coat, where the black edge of a notebook had been showing all along.

His fingers closed on it. Her hand closed on his wrist.

She didn't grab. She simply had it, gloved fingers around the bones of his wrist with his hand still in her pocket, and she held it there. He could feel the steel of her watch against the back of his hand. Neither of them moved. Out in the white, the bell buoy rang.

"Give it back," she said.

He took his hand out of her pocket empty. He hadn't even gotten the notebook clear.

"That's twice," said Tess, and let go. "The pen. And this."

"You're keeping count."

"I'm a cop. I keep everything." She moved the notebook to an inside pocket and buttoned her coat over it. Then she looked at him, gray-eyed, with the fog behind her, and seemed to come to a decision she didn't care for.

"I'm going to tell you one thing," she said, "so you'll go home and stop tearing my town apart. I asked the crime lab to do the trumpet first. Your trumpet. The lieutenant signed off on it last night. It goes ahead of everything else they've got." She turned up her collar. "That's all you get. Go home, Archie."

She went back up the wharf toward the gray Interceptor parked exactly between the lines in the little lot by the co-op. She didn't look back.

*He's lying to me about something.*

"Sergeant," he called.

She stopped halfway up the wharf and half turned.

"That man wears his own face on his sweatshirt," Archie said. "He's never kept a secret from an audience in his life."

"He's my witness, Archie."

He went the other way, up the far side of the wharf toward Main Street, fast, with his hands in his pockets and his breath going on ahead of him.
