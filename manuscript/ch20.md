# Chapter 20

## Outside. Now.

She didn't stop at the bottom of the steps. She walked him the length of the town wharf — past the bait shed and the stacked traps and the fuel dock, all the way out to the end, where the planks stopped above the water — and she didn't say a word the whole way, and for once neither did he.

The fog was starting to lift. It had pulled back off the wharf and hung over the harbor in a low white bank, so that the nearest moorings showed, *Second Wind* and *Miss Dottie* dripping on their lines, and everything past them was only shapes. Merrow Light was a green pulse somewhere inside the white, every six seconds. Off Shag Ledge the bell buoy was ringing slow. It was half past seven and cold enough to see his breath, and the granite memorial stood at the end of the wharf with the wet shining on it. Tess stopped beside it and turned around.

"Half that room can read lips," she said. "Keep your voice down."

"Sergeant—"

"Who printed those?"

"I did."

"Who found them?"

He didn't answer. She looked at him and he watched her work it out, and then he watched her decide not to say it, which was worse.

"Okay," she said. "Go ahead. Tell me."

He told her. He meant to tell it well — he'd told it beautifully fifteen minutes ago — but it didn't come out the same at the end of a wharf to one listener who wasn't leaning in. The key on Friday. The listing on Saturday. The unlocked door. The whisper. The sale. He heard it getting thinner as he went, and went faster to make up for it, and finished with, "He was selling the building out from under the town, Tess. He had the key. He had every reason in the world to want her quiet."

"Are you done?"

"I think so."

"Warren Gilley walked into my command post at four o'clock on Sunday afternoon and told me about the poster," Tess said. "Nobody asked him. Nobody had any idea. He sat down across the table from me in his bow tie and told me he'd taken it out of the prop room Friday, and listed it Saturday morning, and taken the listing down Sunday because he was ashamed of himself. He told me about the three in September. He told me about Carol. He told me what Lorelei Crane said to him at the table. He did all of that before I'd asked him a single question, because he thought it was the right thing to do." She hadn't raised her voice once. "I've known for two days. You've known for about ten hours, and you used it for a card trick."

"I didn't—" He stopped. He had.

"Now the part you'd have known if you'd asked me." She held up one gloved finger. "Warren was never within reach of anything she ate or drank. Ivy poured every drop that went into that woman except your tea, and Warren didn't touch your tea or Ivy's bottle." A second finger. "And forget the tea. Say you're right. Say it's the horn. Then Friday doesn't matter, and the key doesn't matter, and the prop room doesn't matter, because at 7:35 on Saturday night you put that thing in your mouth and blew a raspberry through it on two cameras, and here you stand. Whatever was on it went on after 7:35." A third. "Until they called cut, Warren was in his chair in the work light, on film. At 7:47 he went downstairs to the men's room. Dale Pinkham was standing at the stair door, and Dale watched that door until Warren came back up through it at 7:57." She looked at him. "You know Dale was standing there. You were standing next to him."

*Nerves*, the chief had said, kindly, watching Warren go by with a hand on his stomach. Archie had been talking about the riverboat.

"When he came back up," Tess said, "he sat down in a lit circle with six people and a cameraman around him and didn't get up again until the lights went out. He never touched it. I have the frames."

Archie looked at the granite. THOMAS MAYO, at the bottom, the letters still pale inside the cuts.

"And the posters were Gus's," said Tess. "He lent them to the opera house in 1985. There's a letter in the town office files that says so, on his stationery. They were never the town's to sell and they were never Warren's. They belong to Gus's estate." She paused. "That's your mother. I called her last night and told her. It's her decision whether there's a complaint."

"What did she say?"

"She asked if Warren was eating."

He laughed. It came out of him without permission, and it sounded terrible, and he stopped.

"And Carol Gilley," said Tess, "is two doors down the hall from my father."

He turned and looked at her.

She stood with her hands in the pockets of the charcoal coat and her face turned partly to the water. The fog had laid a fine wet shine over her braid. She didn't look at him.

"She sits with him at lunch. She thinks he's her brother Paul. He lets her. On Tuesdays Warren brings her flowers, and he brings Dad a roll of Necco wafers, because Dad likes the brown ones and nobody else on earth does." Her voice had gone flat — Maine-flat, every corner sanded off. "I've known Warren Gilley my whole life. He wrote the policy on our house. He's a pompous old man with a bad haircut, and he never did a dishonest thing in his life until this year. And you stood up in the Galley in front of forty people and called him a murderer with a magic trick."

"I didn't call him—"

"You didn't have to. You built it so you wouldn't have to." She looked at him then. "I know how a trick works, Archie. I've watched yours."

There was nothing to say to that. He knew there was nothing. So he said something anyway, because that was what he did when there was nothing to say; it was the only thing he had ever known how to do with a silence.

"All right," he said. "Not Warren. I was wrong about Warren. I'll be wrong about Warren in writing if you like, in the *Packet*, a full page. But hear me out—"

"No."

"—thirty seconds. Kev Duchesne."

Tess closed her eyes.

"Eleven thousand subscribers on Friday. Six hundred thousand this morning. I looked. He's monetized. He's got a mattress sponsor. Who wins the contest to sit in the most-watched séance in America? A nobody from Haverhill with a cartoon of himself on his chest. Nobody wins those contests, Tess. They're rigged. I've rigged them. So what if he's not a nobody? What if he's a plant — a rival network, one of the streamers, somebody who wants *The Other Side* gone and a hit of his own—"

"A deep-cover agent."

"I'm only saying it's elegant."

"Kev Duchesne," said Tess, opening her eyes, "sweated through his sweatshirt in my interview on Sunday. He asked me if I could get him your autograph. He cried when I asked for his home address." She took a breath. "He's lying to me about something. I don't know what yet. But he's not a spy, Archie. He couldn't spy on a houseplant."

Archie filed it. He felt himself file it.

Then — because she was still angry, and because he couldn't bear her being angry, and because his hands had always known what to do when the rest of him didn't — he stepped in close and lifted his right hand to make a point about rival networks, and his left went to the slash pocket of her coat, where the black edge of a notebook had been showing all along.

His fingers closed on it. Her hand closed on his wrist.

She didn't grab. She simply had it, gloved fingers around the bones of his wrist with his hand still in her pocket, and she held it there. He could feel the steel of her watch against the back of his hand. Neither of them moved. Out in the white, the bell buoy rang.

"Give it back," she said.

He took his hand out of her pocket empty. He hadn't even gotten the notebook clear.

"That's twice," said Tess, and let go. "The pen. And this."

"You're keeping count."

"I'm a cop. I keep everything." She moved the notebook to an inside pocket and buttoned her coat over it. Then she looked at him for a long moment, gray-eyed, with the fog behind her, and seemed to come to a decision she didn't care for.

"I'm going to tell you one thing," she said, "so you'll go home and stop tearing my town apart looking for a better answer. The crime lab's doing the trumpet first. Your trumpet. The lieutenant signed off on it last night. It goes ahead of everything else they've got." She turned up her collar. "That's it. That's all you get. Go home, Archie."

She went back up the wharf toward the gray Interceptor parked exactly between the lines in the little lot by the co-op. She didn't look back.

He stood by the memorial a while longer. The fog was coming apart over the harbor in long white rags, and the boats were coming out of it one at a time, and in the Galley's side window he could see a shape at the liars' table — Elwin, maybe — looking out at the water, or at him. There was no telling which.

*He's lying to me about something.*

"Sergeant," he called.

She stopped halfway up the wharf and half turned.

"That man wears his own face on his sweatshirt," Archie said. "He's never kept a secret from an audience in his life."

"He's my witness, Archie."

He was already walking — the other way, up the far side of the wharf toward Main Street, fast, with his hands in his pockets and his breath going on ahead of him. She hadn't said no. He decided that wasn't the same as no.
