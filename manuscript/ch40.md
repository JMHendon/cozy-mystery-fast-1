# Chapter 40

## The Empty Chair

Gus Fairweather had built the crawlspace in 1951, and he had built it for a man who was five foot eight.

The hatch was in the alley behind the opera house, under the iron back stairs: a square wooden door set into the stone foundation, painted the same gray as the stone, so that nobody had noticed it in seventy years. Archie had found it again in August with a flashlight and Lenny, and they'd crawled the length of it together on their elbows and come up through the trap in the middle of the stage covered in dust, and Archie had laughed so hard he'd had to lie on the boards for a minute, and Lenny had said, *Your grandfather was a short man with no regard for anybody's knees.*

Ozzie's truck stopped at the mouth of the alley. Archie got out.

The hatch was open. Somebody had propped it with a brick and left a flashlight on the ground beside it, switched on, pointing in.

He got down on his knees on the frozen gravel in his one sock and his foil blanket and his ruined tailcoat, with Hollis's keys clutched against his chest, and went in.

It was forty feet. He'd measured it in August. Forty feet of packed dirt and old stone, eighteen inches high in the low places, with water pipes and conduit running overhead and the dust of a hundred years on everything. The flashlight made a yellow tunnel ahead of him full of cobwebs. He went on his elbows and knees, dragging himself, the keys clanking on the stones every time he moved.

He was so cold. He had stopped feeling his feet somewhere on Main Street. The shaking had gotten into his jaw and his hands and, worst of all, his elbows, which kept going out from under him. Twice he put his face in the dirt.

*Get up, Sparrow,* he thought, in a voice that wasn't his. *Watch my hands.*

He got up.

Over his head, through the floorboards of the stage, he could hear the show. It came down to him in pieces through the old pine: a piano playing something slow and minor — the Empty Chair music, Gus's music, which Lenny had found on a wax cylinder in the turret in September — and under the piano, the enormous waiting silence of three hundred people in the dark. And then, clear and steady and very close, a voice he knew better than his own.

"My great-grandfather," said June, "used to say that every trick is a promise."

He stopped crawling.

"He said that when a magician asks you to watch a chair — an ordinary chair, a chair you could buy at any yard sale in Maine — what he's really asking is for you to believe him. That something that isn't there can be there. That someone who's gone can come back." It was the patter he'd given her on Wednesday night in Lenny's workshop, but she'd changed it. She'd made it hers. "He did this trick on this stage every Halloween for forty years. The last time anyone saw him do it was thirty-seven years ago tonight."

Archie crawled faster. The flashlight beam jerked ahead of him. There was a thin bright square of light outlining the trapdoor overhead, and below it, crouched in the dirt in black velvet and diamonds with a second flashlight in her hand, his mother.

Vivienne turned and saw him coming, and put her hand over her mouth.

Lenny was beside her, on his knees under the trap, one hand on the iron release lever Gus had built in 1951 and Lenny had oiled every week since September. He looked at Archie in the flashlight — the foil blanket, the one sock, the dirt, the keys — and his face didn't change.

"Twelve minutes," said Lenny.

"I said seven."

"You said seven. You took twelve. June's been stalling for five." He jerked his head upward. "She's good at it."

Above them, through the boards: "Would you all help me count?"

Vivienne was already moving. She had been crouched directly under the trap, ready — her place, her cue, her chair; she had been coming up through Gus's trapdoors since she was twelve years old. She shifted aside in the dirt without a word, in her diamonds, and took Archie's face in her two cold hands for one second, and looked at him, and let him go.

"Go on, darling," she whispered. "It's your chair."

"*One,*" said three hundred people, overhead.

Archie got onto the short iron ladder under the back leaf of the trap. There was barely room to crouch; his knees were on the rungs, his head bent against the underside of the stage. The chair stood on the front leaf, a foot from his face. Through the crack along the back one he could see a hard white line of light — the spot, on the sheet.

"*Two.*"

Lenny's hand was on the lever. His other hand came out and gripped Archie's arm above the elbow, hard, through the wet sleeve of the tailcoat, and held it.

"You came back," said Lenny.

It was the only thing he said, and he didn't look at Archie when he said it. He let go and pulled the lever.

The back leaf dropped open on its counterweight without a sound. Above Archie, fallen to the boards behind the chair, was the long tail of the white sheet, glowing with the spotlight beyond it like a lampshade. He went up into it in one smooth motion, hands on the rim, feet on the rungs, and turned inside the cloth, under the hoop, and sat, in the chair, in the hot white light, dripping. Below him the leaf swung shut.

"*Three—*"

Through the sheet he could see June's shape beside the chair. He saw her hand come up and take hold of the cloth.

She pulled.

The sheet went up and back off him in one long white sweep, hoop and all, and the light hit him full in the face, and he sat there in it.

He knew what they were looking at. A tall, wet, filthy man in a dripping tailcoat and one sock, gray dust in his hair and down one side of his face, wrapped in a crackling silver foil blanket that caught the spotlight and threw it back in a hundred pieces like a cloak made of mirrors, with an iron ring of a hundred keys in his lap. They had been promised a grandmother. They didn't know where he'd been. To them it was the best trick anyone in Merrow had seen since 1988.

The sound began somewhere in the back and came forward like weather. Then they were on their feet, the whole hall, the roar of it going up into the pressed-tin ceiling and out through the tall dark windows into the fog.

June didn't move.

She stood beside the chair with the sheet hanging from her hand, in her great-grandfather's jacket, with her hair coming down, and looked at her father. Her face was white, and wet, and absolutely still. She knew where he had been.

"You're late," said June.

Her voice cracked right down the middle.

"I know," said Archie. "I'm sorry. I came the long way."

She dropped the sheet. She put both arms around his neck, foil blanket and seawater and dust and all, and held on — in front of three hundred people, the girl who wouldn't be dropped off at school in the hearse — and Archie Fairweather sat in his grandfather's empty chair in the middle of the Merrow Opera House and held his daughter, and did not vanish.

---

Afterward, when the curtain was down and the house lights were up and the noise out front had gone on so long that Lenny finally turned off the stage lights to make it stop, Archie found his mother in the stage-left wing.

She was standing by the rope rail in her black velvet, with dirt on the knees of it and her diamonds slightly askew. Ozzie Lund stood beside her in his float coat, saying nothing, with one hand at the small of her back.

Over her arm was Gus's opera cloak.

Vivienne held it out to him. She didn't say anything at first. She looked down at it — the black wool and the wine-red silk, folded in thirds with the lining out, the silver clasp precisely centered — and then up at her son.

"Ozzie says you did this," she said. "On the boat. Before you went in."

"I don't remember doing it."

"No," said his mother. "I don't suppose you do." She laid the cloak across his arms, carefully. "It's folded exactly the way my father used to fold it, Archibald. Exactly."
