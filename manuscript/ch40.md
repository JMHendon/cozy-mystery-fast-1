# Chapter 40

## The Empty Chair

Gus Fairweather had built the crawlspace in 1951, and he had built it for a man who was five foot eight.

The hatch was in the alley behind the opera house, under the iron back stairs: a square wooden door set into the stone foundation, painted the same gray as the stone so that nobody had noticed it in seventy years. Archie had found it in August with a flashlight and Lenny, and they'd crawled the length of it together on their elbows, cursing, and come up through the trap in the middle of the stage covered in dust, and Archie had laughed so hard he'd had to lie on the boards for a minute, and Lenny had said, *Your grandfather was a short man with no regard for anybody's knees.*

Ozzie's truck stopped at the mouth of the alley. Archie got out. He didn't say thank you. He'd say it later, every day for a year.

The hatch was open. Somebody had propped it with a brick, and there was a flashlight on the ground beside it, switched on, pointing in.

He got down on his knees on the frozen gravel in his one sock and his foil blanket and his ruined tailcoat, with Hollis's keys still clutched against his chest, and went in.

It was forty feet. He'd measured it in August. Forty feet of packed dirt and old stone, eighteen inches high in the low places, under the joists of the stage, with water pipes and electrical conduit running overhead and the dust of a hundred years on everything. The flashlight made a yellow tunnel ahead of him full of cobwebs. He went on his elbows and his knees, dragging himself, the keys clanking against the stones every time he moved.

He was so cold. He'd stopped feeling his feet somewhere on Main Street. The shaking had gotten into his jaw and his hands and, worst of all, his elbows, which kept going out from under him. Twice he put his face in the dirt.

*Get up, Sparrow,* he thought, from nowhere, in a voice that wasn't his. *Watch my hands.*

He got up.

Over his head, through the floorboards of the stage, he could hear the show.

It came down to him in pieces through the old pine: a piano playing something slow and minor — the Empty Chair music, the music Gus had used, that Lenny had found on a wax cylinder in the turret in September — and under the piano, the enormous waiting silence of three hundred people in the dark. And then, clear and steady and very close, a voice he knew better than his own.

"My great-grandfather," said June, "used to say that every trick is a promise."

He stopped crawling.

"He said that when a magician asks you to watch a chair — an ordinary chair, a chair you could buy at any yard sale in Maine — what he's really asking you is to believe him. That something that isn't there can be there. That someone who's gone can come back." June's voice went out over the hall, and it was steady, and it was his own patter, the words he'd given her on Wednesday night in Lenny's workshop, but she'd changed them. She'd made them hers. "He did this trick on this stage every Halloween for forty years. The last time anyone saw him do it was thirty-seven years ago tonight."

Archie started crawling again, faster. The flashlight beam jerked ahead of him. There was light in front of him now, a thin bright square of it outlining the trapdoor overhead, and below it, crouched in the dirt in black velvet and diamonds with a second flashlight in her hand, his mother.

Vivienne turned and saw him coming, and put her hand over her mouth.

Lenny was beside her, on his knees under the trap, one hand on the iron release lever Gus had built in 1951 and oiled every October since. He looked at Archie in the flashlight — the foil blanket, the one sock, the dirt, the keys — and his face didn't change at all.

"Twelve minutes," said Lenny.

"I said seven."

"You said seven. You took twelve. June's been stalling for five." He jerked his head upward. "She's good at it."

Above them, through the boards: "Would you all help me count?"

Vivienne was already moving out of the way. She'd been crouched directly under the trap, ready — it was her place, her cue, her chair; she'd been coming up through Gus's trapdoors since she was twelve years old. She shifted aside in the dirt without a word, in her diamonds, and took Archie's face in her two cold hands for one second, and looked at him, and let him go.

"Go on, darling," she whispered. "It's your chair."

"*One,*" said three hundred people, overhead.

Archie got under the trap. There was barely room to crouch. His knees were in the dirt, his head bent against the underside of the stage. He could see, through the crack around the square of wood above him, a hard white line of light — the spot, on the chair.

"*Two.*"

Lenny's hand was on the lever. His other hand came out, and gripped Archie's arm above the elbow, hard, through the wet sleeve of the tailcoat — the way you'd grip a man to keep him from falling, or to make sure he was really there.

"You came back," said Lenny.

It was the only thing he said. His voice did something on the last word. He let go of Archie's arm and pulled the lever.

"*Three—*"

The trap dropped open on its counterweight, silent, the way Gus had built it to. Above Archie was the underside of the chair seat and, all around it, falling to the boards on every side, the white sheet, glowing with the spotlight beyond it like a lampshade. He went up through the trap the way he'd gone up through a hundred others — one smooth motion, hands on the rim, feet on Lenny's back — and turned, and sat, in the chair, under the sheet, in the hot white light, dripping. Below him the trap swung shut without a sound.

Through the sheet he could see June's shape, standing beside the chair. He could see her hand come up and take hold of the cloth.

She pulled.

The sheet went away from him in one long white sweep — he'd taught her that, the flourish at the end, the way Gus did it — and the light hit him full in the face, and he sat there in it.

He must have been quite a sight. He knew that. A tall, wet, filthy man in a dripping tailcoat with one sock, gray dust in his hair and down one side of his face, wrapped in a crackling silver foil blanket that caught the spotlight and threw it back in a hundred pieces, like a cloak made of mirrors — with an iron ring of a hundred keys in his lap.

The audience didn't know. How could they? They'd seen a chair, and a sheet, and a count of three; and where a girl in a blue velvet jacket had told them they were going to see her grandmother, there was, impossibly, a soaking-wet magician in a silver cape, as if he'd come straight up out of the sea. It was the best trick they'd ever seen. It was the best trick anyone in Merrow had seen since 1988.

The sound began somewhere in the back and came forward like weather. Then they were on their feet, all of them, the whole hall, the roar of it going up into the pressed-tin ceiling and out through the tall dark windows into the fog.

June didn't move.

She stood beside the chair with the sheet hanging from her hand, in her great-grandfather's jacket, with her hair coming down, and she looked at her father, and her face was not the face of a girl who'd just done a trick. It was white, and wet, and absolutely still, and she knew — he could see her knowing — exactly what it had taken to get him into that chair, and exactly where he'd been, and exactly what it meant that he was here.

The audience thought it was the trick. June knew it wasn't.

"You're late," said June.

Her voice cracked right down the middle.

"I know," said Archie. "I'm sorry. I came the long way."

She dropped the sheet. She put both arms around his neck, foil blanket and seawater and dust and all, and held on — in front of three hundred people, the girl who wouldn't be dropped off at school in the hearse — and Archie Fairweather sat in his grandfather's empty chair in the middle of the Merrow Opera House and held his daughter, and did not vanish.

---

Afterward, when the curtain was down and the house lights were up and the noise out front had gone on for so long that Lenny had finally just turned off the stage lights to make it stop, Archie found his mother in the stage-left wing.

She was standing by the rope rail in her black velvet, with dirt on the knees of it and her diamonds slightly askew. Ozzie Lund was beside her in his float coat, saying nothing, with one hand at the small of her back.

Over her arm was Gus's opera cloak.

Vivienne held it out to him. She didn't say anything at first. She just looked at it — at the black wool and the wine-red silk, folded lining out, shoulders tucked in, in thirds, the collar laid on top and the silver clasp precisely centered — and then she looked up at her son.

"Ozzie says you did this," she said. "On the boat. Before you went in."

"I don't remember doing it."

"No," said his mother. "I don't suppose you do." She laid the cloak across his arms, carefully, the way you'd hand someone a sleeping child. Her voice was very quiet. "It's folded exactly the way my father used to fold it, Archibald. Exactly."
