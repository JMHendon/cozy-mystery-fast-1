# Chapter 18

## Harborview Antiques

June's room was the only room in Fairweather House that didn't do anything.

She had chosen it in August for exactly that reason, after an afternoon going around the second floor with a flashlight and a butter knife, tapping paneling and pressing knots in floorboards, while Lenny followed her with his hands in his sweater pockets and said "No" at intervals, meaning there was nothing there. It was a narrow room at the back of the house, over the kitchen, with a ceiling that sloped down to a single window on the harbor. A desk made from an old door laid across two filing cabinets. A narrow bed under a quilt Vivienne had bought at a church fair. Her Doc Martens side by side, toes to the wall. And above the desk, four thumbtacks pressed into the plaster in a neat rectangle, with nothing between them.

June sat cross-legged on the bed with the laptop on her knees. Archie sat on her desk chair, which had been built for someone a foot shorter, with his knees somewhere near his chin.

"Three conditions," said June.

"I love conditions."

"One, you don't take over. Two, you don't do anything tonight. Three, whatever you do do, you tell me first. Before. Not after."

"June—"

"Those are the conditions."

He looked at her: the pencil through the bun, the freckles, the set of her mouth. It was the face she wore at the podium, and he had once watched it dismantle the state champion from Bangor in a cross-examination that lasted four minutes.

"No," he said. "No conditions, no laptop. You're sixteen. You have school in the morning. There's a lieutenant downtown who thinks somebody in this house—"

"I know what the lieutenant thinks. Grandma told me at dinner. Then Lenny told me again, because he didn't think Grandma had said it right." June didn't raise her voice. "So I'm not going to sit downstairs doing chemistry about it. Those are the conditions. You can have them or you can go to bed."

He could have gone to bed. He considered it, honestly, for almost a second.

"Fine," he said. "Show me."

She turned the laptop around.

It was an auction site, the big one, open to a seller's page: HARBORVIEW ANTIQUES · MERROW, MAINE · member since 2011 · 214 ratings · 100% positive. Below that was a grid of little square photographs — a painted lobster buoy, a ship's lantern, a stack of Grange cookbooks, a brass barometer.

"I was looking for a poster," June said. "One of Gus's."

"What for?"

"My own reasons."

He looked at the four thumbtacks over the desk, and then away again. He didn't ask. He'd been practicing not asking since a Friday afternoon in the library.

"I set an alert," June went on. "'Great Fairweather.' Mostly it's programs and reprints. But in September there were three real ones. Originals, framed. All sold by Harborview Antiques, of Merrow." She clicked. "Opera house provenance."

The first listing came up: a framed poster leaning against a wall, lit by a flash, a man in white tie striding into a brick wall with half his body already through it. THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER WALKS THROUGH WALLS! *From the Merrow Opera House (Maine), removed during restoration.* SOLD · $1,150.

Archie knew the frame. Black, with a narrow gold lip inside, the twin of the frame on the ASYLUM poster in the opera house stairwell. A few feet past that one, on the landing, there was a clean pale rectangle on the old cream paint with two nail holes along the top. He'd walked past it forty times since August. He'd assumed Hollis had taken something down to be reframed.

"Removed during restoration," he said. "I'm the restoration."

"There are two more." June clicked. RADIO'S MASTER OF MYSTERY, 1949, $1,400, to someone in Connecticut. And a third, which made him lean forward so fast the little chair squealed: a girl of about fourteen in spangles and a feathered headdress, lying in a long box with her head out of one end and her feet out of the other, while a tall silver-haired man in a cloak stood over her with a saw. THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER SAWS A LADY IN HALF — TWICE NIGHTLY! 1966. SOLD · $975.

"That's your grandmother," he said.

June looked. "Seriously?"

"Four thousand times, she says. You'll hear about every one of them." He stared at the screen. "Somebody sold my mother to a man in Ohio for nine hundred and seventy-five dollars."

"In two pieces," said June. "Keep going. This is the part."

The fourth listing had gone up on Saturday morning at 6:48. It wasn't framed. The poster lay flat on a desk under a lamp, curling at the corners, with a soft crease across the middle: THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER · HALLOWEEN AT THE OPERA HOUSE · 1962. *Rare. From storage at the Merrow Opera House.* Buy It Now, $1,200. Across the top ran a gray bar: *This listing was ended by the seller on Sunday at 12:04 p.m.*

"From storage," said Archie slowly.

There was a big gray steel flat file at the back of the prop room, wide shallow drawers full of old programs and, Hollis had told him once, who knew what else. He'd never opened it.

"Now look at the third photo," said June.

It was the same poster, closer, the lamp moved for a better angle. In the bottom left corner, half cut off by the edge of the picture, was the corner of a dark green leather desk blotter with a gold-tooled border, and a few letters stamped in gold: *—LEY INSURANCE & REA—*

June didn't say anything. She let him have it. She'd learned that from someone.

"Gilley," said Archie.

"I've been in that office. Grandma took me in September when she was fighting with him about the homeowner's policy. He's got that blotter. I think it was his grandfather's." June pulled the laptop back toward her. "And I asked the seller a question. About the cookbooks. He answered in four minutes and signed it *W.*"

"You asked about a Grange cookbook to identify a seller."

"It's a good cookbook. There's a whole chapter on aspic."

Archie got up out of the little chair. He couldn't sit in it anymore, and it wasn't his knees. It was the thing that always started in him: the small sweet click of a lock turning over in the dark. He walked the four steps the room allowed, to the window and back, winding his grandfather's watch.

"Saturday night, at the table," he said, "Lorelei leans across Ivy and says something in Warren's ear, and he goes white. I saw it, June. I was right there. Seven thirty-eight. A few minutes later they call cut, the work light goes down, Lorelei and Ivy go downstairs, and everybody else gets up and wanders around. Not Warren. Warren sits at his place in the half-dark with that horn a reach from his elbow." He turned at the window. "And then at a quarter to eight he's suddenly sick. Up and past me and the chief and down the stairs, holding his stomach. Nobody's sick on cue, Junebug. That's an exit. You get off the stage before anybody looks at the table."

"Dad."

"It isn't the key. I thought it was the key, but whatever he did in that prop room on Friday, I put my mouth on the horn at seven thirty-five on Saturday and here I am. It was after. It was at the table." He stopped at the end of the bed. "And if Lorelei knew about the posters — and she knew everything, she had Ivy, Ivy researched every one of us — then she wasn't just whispering to him. She was *telling* him. And the sale doesn't close until the fifteenth of November. Something bad enough happens in that theater before then, with the new owner standing in the middle of it, and it goes back to the town, and Warren gets his condominiums."

June looked up at him from the bed. Then she looked down at the laptop and did something unnecessary with the trackpad, and when she looked up again her face was back to normal.

"That's a theory," she said. "It's not evidence. Everything after the blotter is you."

"Everything after the blotter is *beautiful*."

"That's not a legal standard."

"It should be." He sat down on the end of the bed, which bounced her, and she grabbed the laptop. "June. You found this. In an evening. The State Police have had three days and a lieutenant from Augusta, and they're looking at *me*. You found Warren Gilley's blotter."

"I found a corner of a blotter."

"It's a beautiful corner."

She pressed her lips together hard. It didn't entirely work.

"Condition three," she said.

"I'm going to ask Warren about it."

"Where?"

He didn't answer fast enough. He was already seeing the Galley at seven in the morning: the long counter, the booths full, Warren at the end stool in his bow tie. He was thinking of how a room leans in when you lay three things face down on a counter and ask somebody to pick one. He could feel it in his hands.

June closed the laptop.

"Dad," she said. "Don't do it in public."

"Junebug. Would I?"

She looked at him a while longer. Then she lay back on the pillow, pulled the church-fair quilt up to her chin, and rolled over to face the wall with the four thumbtacks on it.

"The printer's in Lenny's workshop," she said. "Don't wake him up."

He went down the back stairs in his socks with her laptop under his arm, already deciding which stool.
