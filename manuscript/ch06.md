# Chapter 6

## Two of a Kind

The note had been waiting on the kitchen table when he got home from the library, propped against the salt shaker in an envelope of heavy cream paper that smelled faintly of smoke. *Drinks. Terms. Spindrift Inn, eight o'clock. Come alone, honey, I don't bite until Saturday. — L.C.*

"She's flirting with you," said Vivienne, reading it over his shoulder.

"She's negotiating."

"Darling, at our age it's the same thing."

The Spindrift Inn was a white sea captain's house on Water Street, built in 1840 by a man who'd made his money in ice and spent it on columns. It had a fanlight over the door and a widow's walk on the roof, and in the summer it was booked a year ahead by people from Connecticut. In late October it was booked by Marchand Media, all eleven rooms, and the little parking lot behind it was full of rental SUVs and a white production van with California plates.

The front parlor had a fire going in a fireplace big enough to roast a goat. Lorelei Crane was sitting in a wingback chair beside it with her shoes off and her feet tucked under her, a glass of red wine in one ringed hand and a paperback mystery face down on the arm of the chair.

"You came," she said, delighted, as if there'd been any doubt.

"You said come alone. I couldn't resist a dare."

"Nobody can, honey. That's my whole business." She waved at the chair opposite. "Sit. Pru's bringing you something. I told her you looked like a bourbon man."

He was a bourbon man. He sat.

Without the velvet coat, Lorelei looked smaller and more tired than she had in the opera house. She was in black slacks and a soft gray sweater, and the white streak in her hair caught the firelight. But the rings were all still there, the big pale moonstones on almost every finger, and the dark red mouth, and the eyes — amused, steady, missing nothing. She watched Pru Haskell set down a short glass of bourbon at his elbow and thanked Pru by name, and asked after Pru's daughter in Orono, whose name she also knew.

"So," she said, when Pru had gone. "Terms."

"Terms," Archie agreed. "One. I sit next to you."

"No."

"Two. Before we start, a woman of your choosing searches you, on camera—"

"No."

"Three. I bring my own camera. One. Infrared. Positioned where I like."

"No." She smiled at him over the wine. "Teddy owns every frame shot in that room, honey. It's in the contract. Union, network, lawyers, the whole circus. I couldn't say yes to your camera if I wanted to. Which I don't."

"You said this morning I could bring all the controls I liked."

"And you can. Bring them. Inspect the table. Look under the cloth. Shake the trumpet and see if anything falls out. Do it on camera; Teddy'll love it." She sipped. "But you don't sit next to me, and you don't put your hands on me, and you don't bring your own eyes. I'm not a fool, Mr. Fairweather. I've read your book."

"I haven't written a book."

"Your grandfather's book." She set her glass down. "Every medium of my generation learned the trade out of *The Dead Don't Whisper*. Did you know that? We passed it around. Church basements, Spiritualist camps, the back of the bus. He wrote the manual." She tilted her head. "He just thought he was writing a warning."

Archie had no idea what his face did. Whatever it was, it made her laugh — a real laugh, low and smoky, that turned two heads at the bar in the next room.

"Oh, I've upset you," she said. "I'm sorry. I'm not sorry. You'd have found out."

"How long have you been doing this?"

"Since I was twenty-six. Church halls first. Grange halls. Rented rooms over laundromats. All over New England — Massachusetts, New Hampshire, up here in Maine, back in the day." She waved her hand, and the moonstones flashed. "They all blur, honey. Two hundred little towns with a white church and a war memorial and somebody's mother who wanted to hear from her boy. Then a man from a cable channel came to a sitting in Worcester and here I am. Eight seasons. Billboards." She raised her glass to herself. "And you? Since you were a baby, I bet. Some old man in a cloak pulling quarters out of your ears."

"Four years old," said Archie. "A silver dollar. Out of my left ear. I thought he'd been keeping it in there for me."

"There," she said softly. "You see? We're the same."

"We are not the same."

"We are exactly the same, Mr. Fairweather." She leaned forward with her elbows on her knees and the wine glass held loose between her hands. "Here's the only difference between us. People buy a ticket to your show, and you lie to them for two hours, and they go home amazed and a little lighter in the wallet, and they're happy. People come to me, and I lie to them for two hours, and they go home with their dead." She shrugged. "Tell me which one of us they come back to."

"The ones who come to me know they're being lied to."

"Do they? Really? Every single one?" She smiled. "You've never had a lady in the front row who went home and told her sister it was real magic? Who *needed* it to be?"

He didn't answer that. He drank some of the bourbon, which was very good, and looked at the fire, and when he looked back she was watching him with her head on one side.

"You came to Merrow for your girl," she said.

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't a guess. She said it gently, the way she'd said it to Shannon Mayo on the telephone, probably, and to a thousand others. He watched himself almost fall for it.

"That's in the *Press Herald*, Lorelei."

"The girl's in the *Press Herald*. The rest of it isn't." She reached over and tapped one finger on his knee. "You don't know how to stay anywhere longer than a season, and you're scared she's going to figure that out before you do."

He sat very still. Then, because he'd been doing this for thirty years too and because he could feel the old reflex rising in him like a tide, he set down his glass and looked at her properly — the way he'd look at a volunteer from the audience in the half second before he chose them.

"You rub your wrists," he said. "Both of them. You've done it six times since I sat down. Your left shoulder sits lower than your right — you've been holding something up at arm's length in the dark for twenty years, and it hurts. You've got a paperback mystery on the arm of your chair, which means you like stories where somebody gets caught. You know Pru's daughter's name, and you know mine, and you know the bartender's, because names are the job; but you haven't said your own daughter's name once since I walked in." He picked the glass back up. "You're tired, Lorelei. You're tired of the trumpet. I think some part of you would give anything for somebody to catch you, so you could stop."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire.

Then Lorelei Crane laughed again, softly, and rubbed her left wrist, and caught herself doing it, and laughed harder.

"Oh, you're good," she said. "Oh, honey. You're wasted on rabbits." She sat back in the wingback chair and pulled her feet up under her again and looked at him with something that was almost affection. "This is my last season. Did you know that? I haven't told anybody. Not Teddy. Not the network. Eight seasons and this Halloween special and then I'm going to buy a house somewhere warm with no phone and read mysteries until I die."

"Why tell me?"

"Because you're the only person in this whole sorry business who'd understand what it means to walk off the stage." She raised her glass to him. "To the vanishing man."

He touched his glass to hers. He couldn't think why not.

"Mom."

Ivy Crane was standing in the parlor doorway. She was still in the oversized gray sweater, with the silk scarf at her throat, and she had a tablet held against her chest like a shield. She didn't look at Archie. She looked at her mother, and then at the wine, and then at her mother again.

"Teddy needs you to approve the cold open," she said.

"Tell Teddy I'm busy flirting with the competition."

"He says it has to go to the network tonight."

"Then tell him tonight's not over." Lorelei didn't turn around. "My daughter thinks I talk too much, Mr. Fairweather. She's probably right. She's the brains of the outfit. She does all the research." A small, hard smile. "Don't you, baby."

Ivy didn't answer. She stood in the doorway a moment longer, and her eyes went — not to Archie's face, but to his hands, the right one holding the glass and the left one resting on his knee. Then she turned and was gone down the hall, and somewhere a door closed, not quite a slam.

Lorelei finished her wine.

"Saturday," she said. "Eight o'clock. Wear something dark, honey — the cameras are infrared, and white shirts flare." She put her paperback under her arm. "And bring your mother. She's the one he wants to talk to."

---

He went out the back way, because the hearse was in the lot behind the inn, wedged between the production van and a rental SUV. In the front hall, as he passed, he heard Teddy Marchand's voice from behind a half-open door, pitched low and fast — "*—no, the key-person policy runs through the end of the season, that's the point, that's why I need her on the—*" — and then the door swung shut.

The night outside was cold and absolutely clear, the stars thick over the harbor, the air smelling of woodsmoke and low tide. His breath smoked. He had his keys out and was halfway across the gravel when he heard voices on the far side of the production van, and stopped without meaning to, in the dark between two cars.

"I mean it, Mom." Ivy's voice — low, flat, shaking a little at the bottom. "After this one I'm done. I'm *done*."

"Baby, you're tired. We're all tired."

"I'm not tired. I'm finished. I'm twenty-nine years old and I've never had a — I've never done anything else. I'm done after Saturday."

A pause. Then Lorelei, very soft and very clear, in a voice Archie hadn't heard her use yet.

"You're done when I say you're done, sweetheart."

The van door slid open and shut. Footsteps went away across the gravel toward the inn's back door, quick and light — Ivy's. After a moment, slower footsteps followed. The back door opened, let out a rectangle of yellow light and the sound of someone laughing in the bar, and closed again.

Archie stood in the dark between the cars for a while longer. Then he got into the hearse and sat with his hands on the wheel without starting it.

He'd come to the Spindrift Inn tonight to negotiate with a fraud. He'd been ready for that. He'd been ready to dislike her, and to beat her, and to enjoy every second of it on Saturday night with the cameras rolling. He'd been doing exactly that to people like her since he was nineteen.

What he hadn't been ready for, he thought, turning the key, listening to the old Cadillac engine cough and catch and settle, was to like her.

He drove home along Shore Road under the stars with the heater roaring, and found that he was thinking not about the séance, or the bet, or the trunk, but about a tired woman rubbing her wrists by a fire, raising a glass to the vanishing man.
