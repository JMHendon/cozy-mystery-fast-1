# Chapter 19

## Town Business

By seven o'clock on Tuesday morning the fog had come in off the harbor so thick that the Galley's windows showed nothing but white, and the bell over the door rang every minute or so to let in another wet wool coat and another person who had heard something.

Archie had been on the stool nearest the register since ten to seven, with a manila folder under his elbow and a mug of coffee he had poured himself.

That was new. Shannon Mayo was working the counter — ponytail, apron, the gray thermal shirt — and she'd come down the line with the pot, filling mugs without asking, *there you go, hon*, *morning, hon*, and when she'd reached Archie she had set the pot down on the counter in front of him and walked on. He'd poured. It was the first time in nine weeks he had poured his own coffee in the Galley, and he'd done it carefully, without spilling a drop, and nobody had seen him do it except everybody.

At the liars' table, Elwin and Dickie and Pickle looked out the window at the fog. Sixty-three.

Chief Pinkham was two stools down with a plate of corned beef hash. He'd looked at the folder twice, and he hadn't said one word about the riverboat. Hollis Tibbetts was in the corner booth by the window with the *Packet* folded to the crossword and a pot of tea, her glasses on the end of her nose. She'd lifted two fingers to Archie when he came in, and gone back to seven across.

Warren Gilley came in on the stroke of seven.

Camel overcoat, the fog beaded on its shoulders. A bow tie, navy with small white dots — a good one, a Sunday bow tie on a Tuesday. In one gloved hand he carried a bunch of yellow chrysanthemums in grocery-store cellophane. He hung the coat on the hook by the door, took the end stool by the pie case, laid the flowers carefully on the empty stool beside him, and said, "Two poached, Bev, dry wheat toast, thank you," in the voice of a man who had ordered the same breakfast at the same hour for thirty years.

"Morning, Warren," said Bev.

"Good morning."

Archie picked up his folder and his mug and moved down the counter to the stool on Warren's other side.

"Flowers, Warren," he said. "Courting?"

"Good morning, Mr. Fairweather." Warren didn't look at him. He unfolded his paper napkin and laid it across his knee.

"I've got something I'd like to show you."

"I'm having my breakfast."

"It'll take a minute. It's a trick, actually. You'll like it." Archie raised his voice — not by much; he never needed much — and the counter heard him, and the booths heard the counter. "I'll need three volunteers. Bev?"

"No."

"Chief?"

"Archie," said Dale Pinkham, low. "Maybe not here."

"Corey!"

Corey — C-plus, two *e*'s in *medium* — was already sliding out of the second booth, grinning, and the woman from the hardware store came with him, and a big lobsterman in orange bib pants whom Archie didn't know. Then there were three people standing at the counter and the whole room had turned in its seats to see what the magician was doing now, and he felt it come up through the floor into his feet: the lean. A room leaning in. There was nothing in the world like it. There never had been.

He took three sheets of paper out of the folder and laid them face down on the counter in a row, in front of Warren's coffee cup. He moved them — not fast; this wasn't Harvard Square — left over right, right over center, the old three-card shuffle, the paper whispering across the Formica.

"Find the Fairweather," he said. "Corey."

Corey pointed. Archie turned the sheet over.

It was the WALKS THROUGH WALLS listing, printed in color in Lenny's workshop at five that morning. SOLD · $1,150. *From the Merrow Opera House, removed during restoration.*

The woman from the hardware store read it out loud. Somebody in the booths said, "Isn't that the one off the stairs?"

"That's the one off the stairs." Archie turned the second sheet. RADIO'S MASTER OF MYSTERY, $1,400, Connecticut. Then the third, slowly, because it was the best one — a girl of fourteen in feathers, in two halves. "And that," he said, "is Vivienne Fairweather, sold to a gentleman in Ohio for nine hundred and seventy-five dollars. She doesn't know yet. I'm letting somebody else tell her."

The room laughed — it couldn't help itself — and Archie laughed with it, and beside him Warren Gilley sat with both hands on the counter on either side of his plate, his eggs going cold, and didn't move.

"All three sold in September," Archie said, "by a seller called Harborview Antiques, right here in Merrow. Lovely seller. A hundred percent positive. Answers your questions in four minutes and signs off *W.*" He took one more sheet out of the folder. This one he didn't lay face down. He held it up. "And on Saturday morning at 6:48 — the morning after somebody borrowed the key to the prop room — Harborview Antiques put up this. From storage. And if you look down in the corner of the photograph—" he laid it in front of Warren's plate and tapped it "—you'll see a very handsome green leather desk blotter. I wonder whose."

It went quiet in a wave, from the counter outward. Archie heard the coffee urn tick.

"Archie," said Pinkham.

He heard it. It didn't reach anywhere that made decisions. Every face in the Galley was turned his way, and he had been waiting sixty-three days for this room, and he went on.

"And Saturday night, at the table, with the cameras rolling, Lorelei Crane leaned across her daughter and whispered something in Warren's ear. I was sitting right there. I watched him go white." He turned on the stool to face Warren. "And then you sat at that table, Warren, with her trumpet a reach from your elbow, while everybody else was up and moving. And at a quarter to eight you were suddenly sick. What did she say to you? Did she tell you she knew? Because the sale doesn't close until the fifteenth — you told us that, right here, Friday, in front of witnesses — and if something terrible happened in that theater before then, with the new owner right in the middle of it—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. In one of the booths a woman said, "Oh, *Warren*," and someone else, in a voice Archie didn't know, said, "Good Lord."

Warren Gilley put down his fork.

He did it carefully, laying it across the rim of the plate at an angle, tines down. Then he picked up the sheet with the blotter on it and looked at it for a while, and set it down again on top of the others, and squared the edges.

"Carol," he said.

He said it to the counter. Then he turned his head and looked at Archie for the first time that morning, and his face wasn't white or pink or anything Archie had a word for. It was only tired.

"She told me Carol understood. About the pictures. She said Carol wanted me to know she wasn't angry." He said it quite clearly; everyone could hear. "My wife isn't dead, Mr. Fairweather. She's up the hill at Sea View. You've met her. You do your card tricks for her on Fridays. Marisol tells me she cried at one, but she cries at most things now, so I wouldn't take it to heart."

Archie could see her. Early sixties. Neat gray hair. A cardigan buttoned wrong. *Is Warren coming? Is it Tuesday? He comes on Tuesday.*

It was Tuesday. The flowers were on the stool.

"Eleven thousand six hundred dollars a month," said Warren. "That's what it costs. Her long-term care policy ran out in June. I've taken a second mortgage on a house my grandfather built. I sold the boat in July. In September I took three of your grandfather's pictures off the walls of that building and sold them to strangers, and on Friday I took one more out of the flat file in the prop room with Hollis's key, because the October bill was due Monday. I locked the door after. I thought I locked it." He took the napkin off his knee and folded it in half, and in half again. "Gus Fairweather bought her about five weeks. She doesn't know where she is for most of it. But her window looks down on the bay, and some mornings she likes to watch the boats go out. I'm sorry nobody asked him."

Nobody in the Galley moved.

Archie looked down at the counter, at his mother in two halves, at the man walking through the wall, at the corner of the green blotter. In a trick, this was where you turned the cards back over and showed them the whole thing had been undone. Gus had taught him the other thing first, at four years old, at the kitchen table. Once they've seen it, they've seen it.

"Sit down, Archie," said Bev.

He was standing. He sat down.

In the corner booth, Hollis folded the *Packet*, let her glasses drop on their chain, and got up. She took Warren's camel coat down from the hook and brought it over and held it open behind him, and after a moment he stood and put his arms into it without a word. She picked the chrysanthemums up off the stool and put them in his hands.

"Come on, Warren," she said. "I'll drive you up. I want to see Carol anyway, and she likes my car."

She didn't look at Archie. She steered Warren toward the door with a hand under his elbow, and the room made way for them, booth by booth, people suddenly busy with their eggs.

Archie went after them. He caught them at the door, with the bell still ringing overhead and the fog pouring in around their ankles.

"Warren." His voice came out wrong. He didn't own a voice for this. "Let me. Please. Sea View — all of it, whatever it costs, as long as it costs. Nobody would have to know. I'd consider it a—"

Warren stopped on the step and turned around. He was a head shorter than Archie, sixty-one years old, in a camel coat and a polka-dot bow tie, holding a bunch of supermarket flowers, and he drew himself up to every inch he had.

"No," he said.

"Warren—"

"You bought the opera house out from under this town with your checkbook, Mr. Fairweather." His voice shook, once, and then it didn't. "You are not going to buy my wife."

He turned and went down the steps with Hollis into the fog, and the white closed over them before they'd gone the length of the wharf.

The bell stopped. Archie stood in the open doorway with the cold on his face and the whole Galley at his back, not making a sound.

Then someone came up the steps out of the fog and stopped in front of him. Charcoal coat. Braid. Her phone still in her hand. She must have passed them on the wharf; he could see in her face that she had. Behind him, two stools down from the register, Dale Pinkham was looking very hard at his own phone and not up.

"Outside," said Tess Calloway. "Now."
