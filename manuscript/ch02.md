# Chapter 2

## The Trunk

The lobster's name was Harold, and that was the problem.

He had not been Harold when Archie bought him. At the co-op on the wharf that afternoon he had been one of forty lobsters in a tank, mottled green-black and anonymous, and Archie had pointed at the biggest one, intending to make a triumphant dinner for his family. Somewhere on the drive home along Shore Road — the paper sack on the passenger seat of the hearse, rustling — he had started talking to it. By the time he set the sack on the kitchen counter at Fairweather House, the lobster had a name, a personality, and what Archie privately believed was a sense of humor.

"You can't name it," said June, without looking up from her homework. "That's the one rule."

"I didn't name him. He introduced himself."

"Dad."

The kitchen at Fairweather House was the only room in the place that had never been asked to do anything clever. Gus had built trapdoors into the pantry and a staircase off the second-floor landing that climbed eleven steps and stopped against the ceiling, but the kitchen was just a kitchen: big and square and warm, with a scarred farm table, a cast-iron range nobody could work, a modern range nobody could work either, and tall windows looking down the lawn to the harbor. In the middle of the table, in a white enamel jug, stood a fistful of tall blue flowers Vivienne had cut from the garden that afternoon. At six o'clock the windows were already black. Out past the point, every six seconds, Merrow Light swept a green flash across the water and was gone.

June sat at the end of the table in her school cardigan with three textbooks open and a pencil stuck through the bun of her dark hair. She was sixteen, a junior at the regional high school twenty minutes inland, and she had been for most of her life the most sensible person in any room her father was in. She had his hands and her mother's freckles and a way of saying *Dad* that could mean nine different things depending on the weather.

Archie lifted Harold out of the sack. Harold raised both claws, which were banded shut, in what was either a threat or a blessing.

"Look at that," Archie said. "He's a showman."

"Put him in the pot."

"I'm going to."

"The water's been boiling for twenty minutes."

"I'm letting it get really ready."

June turned a page. "You did this with the turkey."

"The turkey was already dead. That was a different emotional situation." Archie carried Harold over to the stove, held him above the enormous pot, and looked at him. Harold looked back with small black eyes on stalks, which was more eye contact than Archie had gotten from most of the lobstermen in Merrow.

He put Harold in the sink instead, and ran a little cold water over him.

"Grilled cheese?" June said.

"Grilled cheese," Archie agreed.

The back door opened on a gust of cold air that smelled of salt and woodsmoke, and Lenny came in from the carriage house in all four sweaters, took one look at the steaming pot and the lobster in the sink, and said, "No."

"Nobody's asking you to eat him."

"Good." Lenny built himself a cheese sandwich standing up and left without another word. Lenny did not eat anything that came out of the sea. He said it was a matter of principle. Nobody had ever been able to find out which one.

Archie was buttering bread when his mother arrived.

Vivienne Fairweather never simply came into a room. She occurred in it. At seventy-three she had a silver bob cut sharp as a paper edge, cheekbones she'd been told about since 1966, coral lipstick, and tonight a purple kaftan under a man's tweed overcoat she'd taken from the Merrow Players' costume shop and had no intention of returning. In nine weeks she had taken over the costume shop, joined the Congregational choir, and acquired a suitor: the harbormaster, who courted her with great formality and a thermos of coffee.

"Darling," she said, kissing June on the top of the head. "Archibald." She did not kiss Archie. She looked at the sink. "Why is there a lobster in the sink?"

"That's Harold," said June.

"Of course it is." Vivienne unwound a scarf and set one of the blue flowers straight in its jug. "Dad's monkshood. He planted it along the fence for the name — wolfsbane, for Halloween." She turned. "I hear you bet two million dollars against Lorelei Crane this morning while dangling from the flies."

Archie put the butter knife down. "Who told you that?"

"Bev told Ozzie at the fuel dock, and Ozzie told me in the car. Hollis told Bev. I imagine the whole peninsula knew before you'd gotten your shoes back on." She sat down across from June and helped herself to one of June's grapes. "I've signed up."

"For what?"

"For Saturday. The séance." She said it the way other mothers might mention a book club. "The producer was very sweet. They'd been hoping for a family member. I'm to sit in the circle."

"I should have fought the town and canceled the whole thing in September."

"Hollis was quite right to talk you out of it. *Don't cancel her, Archie. Beat her. In Gus's house.*" Vivienne did a very good Hollis. "I've never liked a woman more on short acquaintance."

"No," said Archie.

"Yes."

"Mother, no. Absolutely not. She's a professional. Do you understand what these people do? They find the softest place in you and they press on it until money comes out. She's going to sit in the dark in Gus's theater and pretend to be *Gus*, and she's going to do it to *you*, on television—"

"I know exactly what these people do," said Vivienne. "I was in the room when my father exposed eleven of them, Archibald. I held the flashlight." She ate the grape. "I'm going."

"I forbid it."

June made a small sound into her chemistry book.

Vivienne regarded her son with enormous patience. "You forbid it."

"I'm — strongly against it."

"Noted." She reached over and patted his hand. "I'm still going."

Archie turned to the stove so he wouldn't have to look at either of them, and put the first two sandwiches in the pan. Behind him he heard June close a textbook. That was never a good sign.

"Can I ask a question?" she said.

"You're going to anyway."

"Is it true you bet two million dollars?"

"It's for charity."

"Is it *your* two million dollars?"

"It won't come to that."

"That's not what I asked."

He flipped the sandwiches. "Yes, Junebug. If, by some miracle, Lorelei Crane produces a genuine paranormal phenomenon under scientific controls, I will write a check for two million dollars to whatever charity she names, and then I'll become a monk, because the universe will have stopped making sense."

"You already retired from magic."

"I'm not *performing*. I'm *debunking*. It's completely different. It's practically a public service."

"Will there be cameras?"

He hesitated a fraction too long, and June put her pencil down.

"Dad." This one meant: *You promised.*

He had. Last spring, in a hotel room in Boston, the night after her debate final — the one he'd finally made it to, after vanishing from his own show to get there — he had promised her a real year. One town. One school. One house with a kitchen where people ate dinner. No crews, no tours, no press. A regular year, she'd said, like regular people have, and he'd said yes before she'd finished the sentence.

Nine weeks in, he had bought the town's opera house, booked himself a Halloween show, and bet two million dollars against a television psychic while hanging upside down.

"It's her cameras," he said. "Not mine. It's one night. I'll sit in a chair, I'll hold some hands, I'll catch her lifting a bell with her foot, and then it's over and nobody in this town will ever have to hear the word *séance* again. No circus. I promise."

June looked at him.

"Okay," she said, and opened her textbook again, and he could tell that she didn't believe him and had decided to let him find that out for himself.

The grilled cheese was good. Harold spent dinner in the sink, waving occasionally.

---

Later, when June had gone up to bed and Vivienne had gone out on the porch to say goodnight to Ozzie on the phone at some length, Archie climbed the narrow stairs to the turret.

The turret room had been Gus's study. It was round, with windows on every side, and on a clear night you could see the whole harbor from it — the moorings, the wharf lights, the dark hump of Shag Ledge and the bell buoy beyond. Archie had left it as he'd found it in August: the rolltop desk, the conjuring books, the framed posters of the Great Fairweather sawing ladies in half and holding a live dove in each hand with an expression of profound personal dignity.

And the trunk.

It sat under the north window, brass-cornered, THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER stenciled on its side beneath the faded stars. Gus had built the lock himself: seven small brass dials, each stamped with the alphabet. On the lid, under a yellowed square of celluloid, was the card.

*To be opened when I'm gone. Not before. — G.F.*

Archie sat down on the floor in front of it, as he had most nights since August, and took out the notebook.

He had tried names and places and every seven-letter word that might have meant something to a magician born in Merrow in 1925 — MYSTERY, ESCAPES, VANISHED, which was eight letters and which he'd tried twice anyway out of spite. He had tried MAGICAL, and felt ashamed of himself.

He'd brought the opera cloak up with him to air out. It was Gus's — black wool, lined in red silk that had gone the color of old wine, with a high collar and a silver clasp — and Archie meant to wear it for the Halloween show. He shook it out now and folded it without thinking: lining out, shoulders tucked in, then in thirds, collar on top, the clasp centered. He laid it on the lid of the trunk.

"Just like Dad," said his mother from the doorway.

He hadn't heard her on the stairs. She stood looking at the cloak, her arms crossed over the tweed coat.

"Like Gus what?"

"Folded it. Lining out, shoulders in, thirds. He never once left it in a heap, not even after a three-show day. He said a cloak in a heap was the sign of a man who'd given up." She sat down on the edge of the rolltop desk chair. "You were too small to remember him doing it."

"Apparently I remember."

"Apparently you do."

The wind worked at the turret windows. Neither of them spoke.

"She told me he says I haven't opened it," Archie said finally. "The trunk. This morning. Before I'd said a word about it."

He'd expected his mother to look shaken. Instead she looked amused, and very slightly sorry for him.

"Darling, everybody in Merrow knows about the trunk."

"Do they?"

"It was in the paper. The *Merrow Packet* ran a whole page after he went — 'The Great Fairweather's Final Mystery,' with a photograph of that trunk and the card on the lid. Half the people in this town have it in a scrapbook. If that woman has anyone working for her with a library card, she knew about it before she ever left California."

Archie looked at the trunk. Of course. The most powerful thing a cold reader can say is something that sounds private and is actually public. He had explained it on national television; he had a whole routine about it. He felt his shoulders come down an inch.

Then he thought of her tilting her head to listen to the air beside his ear, and they went back up.

"She said I was looking in the wrong place," he said.

"Well, you are trying VANISHED again," said his mother, glancing at the notebook. "Which has eight letters."

"I was making a point."

Vivienne stood and put one hand on his hair as she passed, as she had when he was small, and he let her.

"He said when he's gone," she said from the doorway. "Not before. Nobody's opened it in thirty-seven years, Archibald." She shrugged. She had been shrugging on stages for sixty years, and she was very good at it. "Draw your own conclusions."

Then she was gone, and he heard her laugh on the landing below — Ozzie, still on the phone — and a door close.

Archie sat on the floor for a long time.

At five minutes past one in the morning, because he was tired and because it was seven letters, he spun the dials to L-O-R-E-L-E-I and pulled the hasp.

Nothing.

He sat back against the desk, oddly relieved, and thought about a dark red smile, and a ringed hand laid against his chest, and a voice saying, gently, that he was looking in the wrong place.
