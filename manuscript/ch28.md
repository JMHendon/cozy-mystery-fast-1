# Chapter 28

## Paint Day

At four o'clock on Wednesday afternoon Archie climbed the opera house stairs with a square of cardboard under his arm that said, in black marker, SHOW CANCELLED — WITH APOLOGIES — A.F., and found the top of the stairs blocked by Bev Thibodeau.

She was in a barn coat and rubber boots, with a clipboard held against her chest, and behind her the hall was full of noise.

"What's that," she said.

He showed her. She read it. She took it out of his hands, folded it once, and put it in the pocket of her barn coat.

"No," said Bev.

"Bev—"

"Sit down. No — don't sit down. Go see Hollis. She's got you on a list."

He went past her into the hall and stopped.

There were fifty-one people in the Merrow Opera House. Ozzie Lund and Dale Pinkham were up on orchard ladders at the harbor-side windows, unhooking the blackout drapes. The liars' table — Elwin, Dickie and Pickle — had set a plank across a pair of sawhorses by the radiators and were stirring five-gallon buckets of paint with the slow seriousness of men baiting traps. Mattie Pinkham was at the top of the scaffolding along the north wall in a Red Sox cap, shouting down about rollers. Deb Coombs from the library was running blue tape along the window trim with the face of a woman asked to alphabetize the ocean. There were drop cloths everywhere, a radio playing country music, and a folding table by the door crowded with casseroles under foil. Somebody had brought a crockpot of baked beans. Somebody had brought three.

Hollis Tibbetts stood at the front of the stage with a clipboard, pointing.

"There you are," she called down. "You're late. You're on the scaffold."

"Hollis, I came up here to cancel—"

"I know what you came up here for. Bev called me from the landing." She was in her painting clothes: a man's flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled, old corduroys, the gray fingerless gloves. "Listen to me, Archie. I know about that girl, and I know about the lieutenant, and you can't fix either one from a stepladder. But you bought this building with a deed that says it's a theater forever, and it's a theater on Friday night whether you're in a mood or not." She pointed her pencil at the north wall. "Window trim. Cream, not white. You like heights."

"I don't like—"

"You hung upside down from my flies for a month. Up."

He went up.

He was on the second tier of the scaffold with a brush and a can of trim paint the color of heavy cream when Ozzie freed the last hook on the first harbor window and let the drape fall.

It came down in one long black slither and piled on the drop cloth, and the light came in.

The rain had blown through an hour before. Behind it, low over the far shore of the bay, the sun had come out under the back edge of the cloud, and it poured in through the tall arched window at a slant, gold and enormous, across the drop cloths and up the far wall to the pressed-tin ceiling, which lit up square by square. Fifty-one people stopped what they were doing. Ozzie stood at the top of his ladder with the hook in his hand and looked at what he'd done.

"Well," he said, after a while. "Next one."

They took down the next one, and the next, until the hall was so full of light that Archie could see the dust going up through the gold air.

He lasted about twenty minutes on the scaffold before he had to give somebody something.

He climbed down and found Bev on the landing with her clipboard.

"Lobster rolls," said Archie. "For sixty. People will keep coming."

"Galley's closed," said Bev. "Closes at two."

"I'll buy every lobster in the co-op tank. I'll pay you double. I'll wash the pans. Badly, but I'll wash them."

Bev looked at him over her reading glasses. Then she took out her phone and dialed a man twenty feet away.

"Dickie," she said into it. "Get down off that sawhorse. We're opening."

---

At a quarter past six, with the work lamps coming up on their stands and the harbor going dark in the windows, Bev and Dickie Thibodeau came up the stairs with a hotel pan between them, and went down and came back with another, and another, and the opera house ate.

There were sixty-two lobster rolls in split-top buns toasted in butter on the Galley's flattop, the meat in big pink-and-white pieces with nothing on it but a little mayonnaise. People sat along the edge of the stage with their legs hanging over and their paint-spattered hands full. Dale Pinkham ate his with his eyes closed. Mattie ate three and argued that the third didn't count because it had been small. Ozzie carried one to Vivienne on a paper plate with both hands, as if it were a corsage, and she accepted it the same way. Lenny had heard the word *lobster* at five o'clock and gone home to eat a cheese sandwich on principle.

Over by the radiators, Elwin Coombs took his roll from Archie's own hands. He looked at it, and at Archie, and nodded once, and said nothing whatsoever.

Sixty-four days. Archie didn't mind. He had never had a nod before.

He sat on the edge of the stage beside his mother and ate his own lobster roll, and it was without question the best thing he had eaten since August. She and Ozzie had their heads together over something on Ozzie's phone; when Archie leaned over to look, his mother put the phone face down on Ozzie's knee and asked him whether he'd tried the beans.

Shannon Mayo had come in at five with her own roller and red eyes. She'd spent an hour that afternoon in the select board room with Kayla Bissonnette and a tape recorder, answering questions about seven forty-five on Saturday night; everybody in the hall knew it, and nobody said so. She went straight to Hollis, who put her on the long south wall beside her. They'd worked side by side ever since, Hollis talking and Shannon not. Shannon didn't come for a lobster roll. Hollis brought her one. Shannon ate it facing the wall she'd painted, and when Archie came past carrying an empty hotel pan, she moved her paint tray out of his way without looking up.

He took it. It was more than he'd had on Sunday.

---

Kayla Bissonnette came up the stairs at a quarter to seven with a liquor-store carton under her arm and an errand she'd rather not run in front of fifty people who had known her since diapers.

"Sorry, everybody," she said. "The lieutenant wants every glove in the building."

"Every glove," said Dale Pinkham.

"Work gloves, rubber gloves, stage gloves, Chief." She looked wretched. "He said every."

So the town of Merrow gave up its gloves. Pinkham went first, peeling off the yellow rubber dish gloves he'd been rolling paint in and laying them in Kayla's carton with tremendous dignity. Ozzie dropped in his work gloves. Hollis came down from the stage with the shoebox of crew gloves from the light booth, set it on top of the pile, and pulled off her own gray fingerless pair and dropped them in after it.

"Those haven't got fingers, Kayla Bissonnette, so tell the lieutenant I'll want them back by Friday. My hands get cold."

"Yes, Mrs. Tibbetts."

The liars' table, after a consultation, surrendered six canvas gloves stiff enough to stand up on their own. Then Vivienne drew off a pair of long black kid gloves she had not been wearing to paint in, and had produced from somewhere for the occasion, and laid them in the box one at a time.

"I wore those to be sawn in half in Atlantic City in 1966," she told Kayla. "I'll want a receipt."

Half the hall laughed. Kayla wrote her a receipt on the back of a paint-store flyer and carried the town's gloves down the stairs with her shoulders back.

Archie watched his mother sit back down beside Ozzie with her bare hands in her lap, and in his head Brannock was saying it again in the rain: *two feet from the horn, with her hands on the back of it.* He'd handed over the card at two o'clock; it was not yet seven. The lieutenant was very good at his job. Archie would have liked that better in somebody else's case.

---

June found him on the scaffold at a quarter past seven.

She came up the ladder with a can of trim paint hooked over her wrist as a pretext, and sat on the plank beside him with her Doc Martens hanging over the edge. They were at the stage-left end now, at the window that had been bare on Saturday night. Out past it the harbor was black, and the Light on the point came and went.

June watched it go around twice.

"I want to do the Chair," she said. "Friday. With you."

He laid his brush across the top of the can.

"Not come out at the end and wave. Not the part you'd show a volunteer. I want you to teach me the method. All of it." She was looking at the window, not at him. "You told Teddy Marchand how the Glass Box works. In an airport. On a napkin."

"I know."

"So." It was the voice she used to close an argument. "Me too."

Archie looked down at the hall. Hollis was making Dale Pinkham move a ladder six inches to the left. His mother sat on the stage edge beside Ozzie with her shoes off, laughing at something he'd said in about four words. Fifty-odd people were painting his grandfather's theater for nothing, with lobster on their breath.

"It's Gus's," he said. "The Chair. It isn't mine. He showed me the summer I was nine, under this stage, and before he'd show me one thing he made me swear. Lenny builds it. Your grandmother comes up through it. Nobody else alive. There's an oath."

"I know there's an oath."

"You'd have to swear it and keep it. For the rest of your life. Not Mattie, not your mother, not some boy at Yale, not the Supreme Court—"

"Dad." She turned and looked at him then. "I know what a secret is. I kept one from you for two months." She put out her right hand between them on the plank, palm down. "Okay. What do I swear on?"

He reached over and took the jack of clubs out of the air beside her left ear.

June jumped. Then she saw which card it was — the one that had landed face up by his shoe in the library the day he caught her — and her face went red to the pencil in her bun. And then it lit up all at once, the whole of it.

She shut it off almost at once. But he'd seen it.

He laid the card face down on the plank between them. She put her hand on it, and he put his on top of hers, and up on the scaffold in the smell of paint and lobster and wet wool, Archie Fairweather said the words his grandfather had said to him on the boards beneath their feet when he was nine years old, and his daughter said them back, every one, without a mistake.

"Yes," he said, when she'd finished. "Yes. Tonight. Lenny's got the practice trap in the workshop."

"Tonight," June agreed. She put the jack of clubs in her cardigan pocket and climbed down without another word, and he watched her cross the floor to Mattie and say something that made Mattie shriek and get paint on Deb Coombs.

---

By eight o'clock the north wall was done and the south wall nearly; the tin ceiling would be another day and another argument. People were finding their coats and disputing whose crockpot was whose. Behind the stage the painted drape glowed under the work lamps — gulls and schooners and a sky far too blue for Maine.

Hollis stood at the center of the stage with her clipboard under her arm and her hands pushed into the pockets of her corduroys. She looked out at the half-painted hall, and at Archie coming down off the scaffold with cream paint to his elbows, and gave her short bark of a laugh.

"Your grandfather would have loved this," she said.
