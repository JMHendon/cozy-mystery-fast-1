# Chapter 28

## Paint Day

At four o'clock on Wednesday afternoon Archie climbed the opera house stairs with a square of cardboard under his arm that said, in black marker, SHOW CANCELLED — WITH APOLOGIES — A.F., and found the top of the stairs blocked by Bev Thibodeau.

She was in a barn coat and rubber boots, with a clipboard held against her chest, and behind her the hall was full of noise.

"What's that," she said.

He showed her. She read it. She took it out of his hands, folded it in half and then in half again, and put it in the pocket of her barn coat.

"No," said Bev.

"Bev—"

"Sit down. No — don't sit down. Go see Hollis. She's got you on a list."

He went past her into the hall and stopped.

There were fifty-one people in the Merrow Opera House. Ozzie Lund and Dale Pinkham were up on two orchard ladders at the harbor-side windows, unhooking Marchand Media's blackout drapes. The liars' table — Elwin, Dickie and Pickle — had set a plank across a pair of sawhorses by the radiators and were stirring five-gallon buckets of paint with the slow seriousness of men baiting traps. Mattie Pinkham was at the top of the scaffolding along the north wall in a Red Sox cap, shouting down about rollers. Deb Coombs from the library was running blue tape along the window trim with the face of a woman asked to alphabetize the ocean. There were drop cloths everywhere, a radio playing country music, and a folding table by the door crowded with casseroles under foil and a coffee urn Archie was nearly sure belonged to the Galley. Somebody had brought a crockpot of baked beans. Somebody had brought three.

In the middle of it all, on the stage, in front of the painted harbor drape, with a clipboard in one hand and a pencil behind her ear and her ring of keys at her hip, stood Hollis Tibbetts, pointing.

"There you are," she called down. "You're late. You're on the scaffold."

"Hollis, I came up here to cancel—"

"I know what you came up here for. Bev rang me from the landing." She was in her painting clothes: a man's flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled, old corduroys, the gray fingerless gloves. "Listen to me, Archie. I know about that girl, and I know about the lieutenant, and you can't fix either one from a stepladder. But you bought this building with a deed that says it's a theater forever, and it's a theater on Friday night whether you're in a mood or not." She pointed her pencil at the north wall. "Window trim. Cream, not white. You like heights."

"I don't like—"

"You hung upside down from my flies for a month. Up."

He went up.

He was on the second tier of the scaffold with a brush and a can of trim paint the color of heavy cream when Ozzie freed the last hook on the first harbor window and let the drape fall.

It came down in one long black slither and piled on the drop cloth, and the light came in.

The rain had blown through an hour before. Behind it, low over the far shore of the bay, the sun had come out under the back edge of the cloud, and it poured in through the tall arched window at a slant, gold and enormous, across the drop cloths and up the far wall to the pressed-tin ceiling, which lit up square by square. Fifty-one people stopped what they were doing. Ozzie stood at the top of his ladder with the hook in his hand and looked at what he'd done.

"Well," he said, after a while. "Next one."

They took down the next one, and the next, until the hall was so full of light that Archie could see the dust going up through the gold air. Nobody in the room had said one word about Saturday night, and he understood that nobody was going to.

He lasted about twenty minutes on the scaffold before he had to give somebody something.

He climbed down, went out to the stairwell, and called Bev, who was twelve feet away on the landing and answered anyway.

"Bev. How many people are in there?"

"Fifty-one. Pru's coming with the inn's ladders. Fifty-three."

"Lobster rolls," said Archie. "For sixty. People will keep coming."

Bev lowered the phone and looked at him across the landing.

"Galley's closed," she said. "Closes at two."

"I'll buy every lobster in the co-op tank. I'll pay you double. I'll wash the pans. Badly, but I'll wash them."

Bev considered him for a long moment. Then she put the phone back to her ear, though he was standing right in front of her.

"Dickie," she said into it. "Get down off that sawhorse. We're opening."

---

At a quarter past six, with the work lamps coming up on their stands and the harbor going dark in the windows, Bev and Dickie Thibodeau came up the stairs with a hotel pan between them, and went down and came back with another, and another, and the opera house ate.

There were sixty-two lobster rolls in split-top buns toasted in butter on the Galley's flattop, the meat in big pink-and-white pieces with nothing on it but a little mayonnaise. People sat along the edge of the stage with their legs hanging over and their paint-spattered hands full. Dale Pinkham ate his with his eyes closed. Mattie ate three and argued that the third didn't count because it had been small. Ozzie carried one to Vivienne on a paper plate with both hands, as if it were a corsage, and she accepted it the same way. Lenny had heard the word *lobster* at five o'clock and gone home to eat a cheese sandwich on principle.

Over by the radiators, Elwin Coombs took his roll from Archie's own hands. He looked at it, and at Archie, and nodded once, and said nothing whatsoever.

Sixty-four days. Archie didn't mind. He had never had a nod before.

He sat on the edge of the stage between his mother and an old man he'd never met, who told him in great detail about a snowmobile he'd owned in 1979, and ate his own lobster roll, and it was without question the best thing he had eaten since August.

Shannon Mayo had come in at five with her own roller and gone straight to Hollis, who put her on the long south wall beside her. They'd worked side by side ever since, Hollis talking and Shannon not. Shannon didn't come for a lobster roll. Hollis brought her one. Shannon ate it with her back to the stage, looking at the wall she'd painted, and when Archie came past carrying an empty hotel pan, she moved her paint tray out of his way without looking up.

He took it. It was more than he'd had on Sunday.

---

Kayla Bissonnette came up the stairs at a quarter to seven with a liquor-store carton under her arm and an errand she'd clearly rather not run in front of fifty people who had known her since diapers.

"Sorry, everybody," she said. "The lieutenant wants every glove in the building."

"Every glove," said Dale Pinkham.

"Work gloves, rubber gloves, stage gloves, Chief." She looked wretched. "He said every."

So the town of Merrow gave up its gloves. Pinkham went first, peeling off the yellow rubber dish gloves he'd been rolling paint in and laying them in Kayla's carton with tremendous dignity. Ozzie dropped in his work gloves. The liars' table, after a consultation, surrendered six canvas gloves that had last hauled a trap during the Carter administration. Vivienne drew off a pair of long black kid gloves she had not been wearing to paint in, and had produced from somewhere for the occasion, and laid them in the box one at a time.

"I wore those to be sawn in half in Atlantic City in 1966," she told Kayla. "I'll want a receipt."

"Yes, ma'am."

Hollis came down from the stage with the shoebox from the light booth, the one the Players' crew had been taking black stage gloves out of since the eighties, and set it on top of the rest. Then she pulled off her own gray fingerless gloves and dropped them in after it.

"Those haven't got fingers, Kayla Bissonnette, so tell the lieutenant I'll want them back by Friday. My hands get cold." She tapped the shoebox. "And if there's one pair less in there when it comes back than there is now, it's detention. I've still got the forms."

Kayla went pink to the ears. "Yes, Mrs. Tibbetts."

"And sit up straight when you carry that."

Half the hall laughed. Kayla, who had been in Mrs. Tibbetts's senior English, carried the town's gloves down the stairs with her shoulders back.

Archie watched her go from the scaffold. He knew exactly who had sent for that carton. He'd handed over the card at two o'clock; it was not yet seven. The lieutenant was very, very good at his job. Archie found he would have liked that better if it had been somebody else's case.

---

June found him on the scaffold at a quarter past seven.

She came up the ladder with a can of trim paint hooked over her wrist as a pretext, and sat on the plank beside him with her Doc Martens hanging over the edge. They were at the stage-left end now, at the window that had been bare on Saturday night. Out past it the harbor was black, and every six seconds Merrow Light flashed green on the point.

June watched it flash twice.

"I want to do the Chair," she said. "Friday. With you."

He laid his brush across the top of the can.

"Not come out at the end and wave. Not the part you'd show a volunteer. I want you to teach me the method. All of it." She was looking at the window, not at him. "You told Teddy Marchand how the Glass Box works. In an airport. On a napkin."

"I know."

"So." It was the voice she used to close an argument. "Me too."

Archie looked down at the hall. Hollis was making Dale Pinkham move a ladder six inches to the left. His mother sat on the stage edge beside Ozzie with her shoes off, laughing at something he'd said in about four words. Fifty-odd people were painting his grandfather's theater for nothing, with lobster on their breath.

"It's Gus's," he said. "The Chair. It isn't mine. He showed me the summer I was nine, under this stage, and before he'd show me one thing he made me swear. Lenny knows because Lenny has to build it. Nobody else alive. There's an oath."

"I know there's an oath."

"You'd have to swear it and keep it. For the rest of your life. Not Mattie, not your mother, not some boy at Yale, not the Supreme Court—"

"Dad." She turned and looked at him then, with her mother's level eyes. "I know what a secret is. I kept one from you for two months." She put out her right hand between them on the plank, palm down, as if she were about to lay it on a Bible. "Okay. What do I swear on?"

He reached over and took the jack of clubs out of the air beside her left ear.

He hadn't planned it; his hands did it on their own. June jumped. Then she saw which card it was — the one that had landed face up by his shoe in the library the day he caught her — and her face went red to the pencil in her bun. And then, before she could stop it, it lit up all at once, the whole of it, the way his own face must have lit on a kitchen floor in 1982 with a silver dollar coming out of his ear.

She shut it off almost at once. But he'd seen it.

He laid the card face down on the plank between them. She put her hand flat on it, and he put his on top of hers, and up on the scaffold in the smell of paint and lobster and wet wool, Archie Fairweather said the words his grandfather had said to him on the boards beneath their feet when he was nine years old, and his daughter said them back, every one, without a mistake.

"Yes," he said, when she'd finished. "Yes. Tonight. Lenny's got the practice trap in the workshop."

"Tonight," June agreed. She put the jack of clubs in her cardigan pocket and climbed down without another word, and he watched her cross the floor to Mattie and say something that made Mattie shriek and get paint on Deb Coombs.

---

By eight o'clock the hall was half painted. The north wall was done and the south wall nearly; the tin ceiling would be another day and another argument. People were finding their coats and disputing whose crockpot was whose. In the light of the work lamps the painted drape at the back of the stage glowed — gulls and schooners and a sky far too blue for Maine — and the tall bare windows were full of the night harbor.

Hollis stood at the center of the stage with her clipboard under her arm and her bare hands pushed into the pockets of her corduroys. She looked out at the half-painted hall, and at Archie coming down off the scaffold with cream paint to his elbows, and gave her short bark of a laugh.

"Your grandfather would have loved this," she said. "Every bucket of it."
