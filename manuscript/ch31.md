# Chapter 31

## Dress Rehearsal

Pru Haskell was in Bangor for her daughter's birthday, and she was the only person alive with the combination to the safe in the Spindrift Inn's office. She'd be back at nine. She had told him so twice on the phone that morning, kindly, and a third time at noon, less kindly.

By six o'clock on Thursday evening Archie had looked at his grandfather's watch eleven times.

The opera house didn't care. It was full of light and noise and fresh paint, the radiators knocking under the black windows. Ozzie and three of the Pinkham cousins had set out the folding chairs in rows that afternoon. The painted harbor drape was flown out, and the stage was cluttered with everything the show needed and some things it didn't: Lenny's cabinet, a rack of costumes, a stepladder, a bentwood chair with a white sheet folded over its back, and Chief Dale Pinkham, in the center aisle, holding a flashlight and a list.

"Archie," the chief called. "When folks come in. Do I tear the tickets or just look at 'em?"

"There aren't any tickets, Dale. It's free."

The chief considered this. "Then what do I tear?"

"Nothing. You smile and you point at chairs."

"Ayuh." Dale wrote something on the list. "Smile. Point."

They were running Act One in order, which meant they were on the Sparrows, which meant Lenny had flatly refused to release the doves. "They don't rehearse," he'd said. "They know it." So Archie stood alone in the gold wash at center stage producing four imaginary birds from an empty silk, and on the fourth he lifted his empty hands and said, "And then the stage goes blue and—"

The stage went blue. It went blue about two seconds after he'd stopped needing it to, and he stood there in it with his arms up, looking foolish.

"You said *blue* in the middle of a sentence," Hollis's voice came down from the booth at the back of the balcony. "Cue lines go at the *end* of the sentence, Archie. Like a period. I taught English."

"You taught *drama*."

"I taught it to people who could finish a sentence."

At the stage-right end of the second row, Shannon Mayo was folding programs on her knee from a stack the height of a lobster crate. She didn't laugh. Beside her, Liam did, loudly, and was elbowed.

---

At twenty past six the hinge on Lenny's cabinet began to scream. Lenny oiled it and swung the door, and it screamed. He got down on his knees with a flashlight, said, "Different hinge," and disappeared behind the cabinet.

In the hold, Archie sat down on the lip of the stage with his legs over the edge, and Liam Mayo came straight out of the second row.

"Do the coin," said Liam. "The walking one. Mattie says you do a walking one."

Archie took a half-dollar out of his waistcoat pocket and let it walk across his knuckles, forefinger to little finger and back by the thumb, without looking at it. Liam stood with his chin on the edge of the boards and watched it go.

"You're not even looking," Liam said.

"That's the whole trick. If I look, you look."

Liam dug a nickel out of his jeans and tried. It went over his first knuckle, and sideways, and dropped through a gap between two stage boards with a small, final *tink*.

From somewhere behind the cabinet, Lenny said, "That's gone."

Liam looked at the gap. Then he looked at his mother. Shannon looked at Archie for the first time that evening, a long, level look with nothing in it he could name.

"Liam," she said. "Leave Mr. Fairweather alone."

But she didn't come and get him, and Archie gave Liam the half-dollar, and Liam spent the rest of the hold dropping it on his own knee.

---

After that things went wrong in the ordinary way, which Archie had always loved about rehearsals. At a quarter to seven Dale came up the side steps holding a sheet of paper at arm's length.

"Fire marshal's fella was in at noon," he said, to the stage generally and the booth in particular. "That door at the end of the balcony. The fire escape. Says it's got to be unlocked during the show. Code. Second way out of the balcony."

"That door," Hollis called down, "lets in the entire North Atlantic."

"I just read 'em, Hollis."

"I know you do, Dale." There was a great sigh from the booth, a clatter of keys, and Archie watched her come out along the balcony in her cardigan to the iron door at the far end, unlock it, push it open on a wedge of cold black air, and jam it a few inches ajar with a folded program. "There. Unlocked."

She went back into the booth. A moment later her voice came down again, pitched for teaching. "Now, Mattie. Look here. The cue book lives in this drawer. *This* drawer, not that one, that one's full of my cough drops. Every cue's in pencil. If I get the flu, you open the drawer, you read the pencil, you push the slider when it says GO. That's all theater is."

"What if you get the flu in the *middle*?" said Mattie Pinkham, loudly and with great interest.

"Then you'll be very popular," said Hollis.

---

They did the Chair at seven, out of order, because Vivienne said that if she had to sit in a crawlspace in sequins she'd do it while her knees still worked.

Lenny took her down through the stage-right stairs. Archie heard them go along under the boards — his mother's voice saying *Leonard, if there is a mouse down here, I will know* and Lenny not answering — and then the sound of Lenny settling at the trap. Out in the house Hollis brought everything down but a single hard spot on the bentwood chair, standing on its leaf of the trap at center stage.

June came out into the light in her school cardigan, because the costume wasn't finished, and Archie came out beside her and stood by the chair and put the sheet on it. The hoop caught. The shape sat there.

He didn't look at it. He looked at the hall — Dale in the aisle with his flashlight off, Shannon with a program half-folded on her knee, Liam with the half-dollar in his fist — and listened to his daughter.

She started with Gus's line and then left it behind, and told the house a little about the chair, and about waiting. She looked at them the whole time. He could feel her not looking at the sheet; it was a kind of heat. The count went long and slow and nobody in the hall noticed it was long. Under his hand on the corner of the sheet, nothing moved.

"Three," said June, and pulled.

Vivienne Fairweather sat in the bentwood chair in the black sequined gown she had last worn on this stage in 1988, with her hands folded in her lap and her chin up, and looked out at the house with the expression of a woman who had been sitting there all evening and couldn't think what the fuss was about.

Liam stood straight up on his chair and shouted, "*HOW.*" In the aisle, Dale Pinkham sat down on the floor. Shannon's program slid off her knee. And from the booth, high at the back, came Hollis's short, unmistakable bark of a laugh, and then the spot went soft and wide and warm, exactly on time.

June didn't look at her father. She looked at the floor, with her mouth pressed tight, and he could see every bit of what it cost her not to grin.

---

At the break he went up to the booth with a question about that last cue. He wanted the spot to hold hard a half-second longer after the sheet came off, so the room could see Vivienne before it saw anything else.

The booth sat at the back of the balcony up three steep wooden steps. He was on the second step when he heard Rosie.

"Mumma says Daddy said it's warm," Rosie said, to nobody in particular. "Where he is. In the water. He said it's warm now."

Hollis's voice came back low.

"Oh, sweetheart. The water's never warm here." A rustle; somebody being shifted on a lap. "That's why your daddy had those big orange gloves. Now. Which one's the blue button? Show me."

Through the doorway he could see the back of Hollis's white head bent over the board, and a small hand on a slider, and Mattie at her elbow with the cue book. The cue would keep. Archie went back down the steps as quietly as the old treads allowed.

His mother was in the stage-left wing in her coat, with the sequins showing at the hem, checking her lipstick in a compact.

"You were magnificent," he said.

"I know, darling. Thank you." She snapped the compact shut. "Don't wait supper."

"Where are you going?"

"Out."

"Out where?"

"Archibald, I am seventy-three years old, and out is a complete sentence." She kissed the air beside his cheek and went out by the stage door, and he stood in the wing and heard Brannock in the rain again, *two feet from the horn*, and told himself it was nothing.

---

He asked about the cue at a quarter past eight, from the doorway of the booth, after Shannon had carried Rosie out asleep on her shoulder.

"Cue," said Hollis, without turning around. "Which one, and what's wrong with it."

"The Chair. After the sheet. Can you hold the spot a half-second longer before you warm it?"

"I can hold it till Christmas." She took a pencil from behind her ear and wrote in the cue book. "Half a second. There. Anything else?"

"That's all."

She went on writing. He left her to it.

---

At twenty minutes to nine he was sitting in the hearse in the dark lot behind the Spindrift Inn with the engine off and the release folded on the dashboard, watching the back door, and at four minutes past nine a pair of headlights swung in off Water Street, and Pru Haskell's little blue Subaru pulled into the space by the kitchen and stopped.
