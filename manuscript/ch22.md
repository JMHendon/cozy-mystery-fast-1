# Chapter 22

## Evidence

The select board room had been freezing on Monday. On Tuesday afternoon it was freezing everywhere except one corner, where the space heater Archie had ordered from Portland sat under Tess's end of the long table, ticking and glowing orange, with Tess's boots as close to it as the law allowed.

It was ten past two. Lieutenant Neil Brannock had driven down from Augusta at noon, and in the forty minutes since he'd walked in he had watched Kev Duchesne's footage seven times on Tess's laptop, said about a dozen words, and drunk most of an extra-large Dunkin' coffee. Kev sat at the far end of the table in his hoodie with his hands between his knees. At eleven a trooper had taken the little camera from him and an evidence tech had copied its memory card and given him a receipt, which Kev had folded into a very small square and kept turning over and over in his fingers. Archie sat across from him, because nobody had told him where to sit and nobody had told him to leave. On the wall above them hung framed photographs of every chair of the Merrow select board since 1899. The one nearest the door was a stern man in a bow tie who could only have been Warren's grandfather. Archie had been not looking at it for half an hour.

Brannock took off his reading glasses.

"Mr. Duchesne. When did you start the camera?"

"When she said hands, everybody. I pushed the button through my pocket."

"Did you edit the file?"

"No, sir."

"Trim it, filter it, brighten it, convert it, run it through any app at all?"

"I put it on my laptop," Kev said. "And then on my phone. And then I watched it about a hundred times. And then I didn't sleep."

"And on Sunday, in this room, Sergeant Calloway asked whether you'd recorded any part of the séance, and you said—"

"Not on my phone." Kev's face crumpled. "I know. I signed a thing that said they'd sue me for a million dollars if I brought a device in. And then she died, and I thought if I told, everybody would think I — and the lawyers — and I've got six hundred thousand—"

"Mr. Duchesne." Brannock didn't raise his voice. "I don't care about your contract."

Kev stopped.

"I care that a woman is dead and you sat on the best piece of evidence in this case for three days. I care that it reached me out of a crawlspace by way of my person of interest." He didn't look at Archie. He didn't have to. "And I care that I'm going to have to explain to the Attorney General's office how a file from a thirty-nine-dollar camera spent seventy-two hours on a YouTuber's laptop before it got anywhere near an evidence locker. It goes to the lab tonight for verification. If anybody has touched one frame of it, they'll tell me."

"Nobody touched it," said Kev. "I swear on—" He stopped, apparently unable to think of anything left that he could swear on.

"A trooper next door is going to take your formal statement. All of it. Friday night in the prop room too. Don't post anything. Don't post that you can't post anything."

Kev got up. At the door he turned and looked at Archie, and Archie gave him a small nod, and he went.

Brannock put his glasses on and pressed play an eighth time.

Archie watched it with him: the gray-green picture, the edge of the table, the white bands of the horn lying on its side in the dark. 20:00. 20:05. The tinny voices. Then 20:17:04, and the hand coming in at the top of the frame, rings on three fingers, the stones bright as new dimes, closing round the narrow end.

"That's her left hand," Archie said. "She was at the head of the table. It goes up to her left. That's the hand Ivy says she held."

"I can see what it is, Mr. Fairweather."

Brannock's phone buzzed on the table. He looked at the screen and picked it up. "Brannock." He listened. "Say again." He listened some more, and took a pen out of his shirt pocket and wrote something on the legal pad in small square capitals and underlined it. "Inside. Not the outer surface. Okay. Who ran it? — Okay. I want it in writing in an hour." He put the phone down and sat for a moment looking at what he'd written.

Then he turned the pad so that Tess could read it, and after a second, so that Archie could too.

ACONITINE — POS — INSIDE SMALL END (SEAM).

"The lab," Brannock said. "Inside the mouthpiece of the trumpet. In the seam where the metal's rolled over. The outside had been wiped. The inside wasn't, or not well enough." He took his glasses off again and pressed the bridge of his nose between finger and thumb. "So. You were right about the horn, Mr. Fairweather."

Each word sounded expensive.

"Holy Houdini," said Archie.

It came out of him the way the laugh had on the wharf, without asking. He was on his feet. He didn't remember standing. He'd been right. It wasn't the tea and it wasn't Bev's thermos and it wasn't the man who'd poured it; it was the horn, it had always been the horn — page one hundred and twelve, *lips to the small end*, half of Merrow could have told them on Sunday morning. He wanted to hug somebody. He looked at Tess, who took her boots off the heater and moved her chair six inches back, and he looked at Brannock, and decided against it.

"So it was on the horn," he said. "Then it's simple. Who got near the horn? In the pictures, it's always the stagehand."

"This isn't a picture," said Tess.

"No. In a picture they'd have cast it better."

"Sit down, Mr. Fairweather," said Brannock.

Archie sat. He was still grinning. He couldn't stop.

Brannock let him grin. He finished his coffee, set the empty cup down very precisely on the corner of the legal pad, and folded his hands on the table.

"Here's what your fan's footage does for me," he said. "Two things. One. It shows the victim's left hand free in the dark at 8:17, which means the daughter lied when she said she held it. You'd told us as much. Now I can show it to a jury." He turned the laptop so that the frozen frame faced Archie: the horn on its side on the table, three white bands. "Two. It shows that horn lying on that table, in the dark, untouched, from the moment the lights went out until the moment the victim picked it up. Nobody reached for it. Nobody brushed it. Seventeen minutes. Which means whatever was in that mouthpiece was in there before the lights went out."

Archie stopped grinning.

"Saturday night, at the hospital in Rockport," said Brannock, "you told Sergeant Calloway that when the lights came back up you were on the floor with the victim, and you saw Ivy Crane pick up the trumpet and wipe the small end with her scarf. Twice. Then put the scarf in her coat pocket." He waited. "Is that still your statement?"

Archie saw it then. He saw it all at once, the way you see a trick from the wrong side of the table, when the volunteer's back is turned and the whole method is lying in your palm in plain sight.

The daughter who poured everything her mother drank. Who sat at her left in the dark and said, twice, on tape, that she'd held her hand. Who'd been told she would be done when her mother said she was done. Who had stood in the full glare of the house lights while her mother was dying on the boards and picked up the horn and wiped the small end hard, twice, and put the scarf away in her pocket, and then wouldn't give it up.

He'd told Tess it was a reset. He still believed it. He believed it with his whole body; he had done it himself ten thousand times.

It didn't matter what he believed. It mattered what he'd seen.

He looked at Tess. She looked back at him with no help in her face at all, which was right. Out in the hall the radiator knocked. Under the table the heater ticked.

He could say he'd been on the floor with a dying woman's head in his arm. He could say it had happened very fast, and he couldn't swear to it now. He was a wonderful liar. He had lied to stadiums. He had lied to Lorelei Crane on the floor of his own stage, and she had smiled at him.

"Yes," he said. "That's what I saw."

Brannock nodded once and wrote it down.

"And I want something put in with it," Archie said. "I want it in the statement that a performer does that. After a show you wipe the props. You put them back. It's a reflex — it's how you protect the method. She's been doing it since she was twelve years old. It's called a reset."

"Put it in," said Brannock. "A jury likes a reason." He drew a folder from under the legal pad and opened it. "Here's what else I have. The remote you pulled out of the umbrella stand on Sunday kills the infrared feed on both of Marchand's cameras at the push of a button. We tested it. The prints on it that aren't yours belong to Ivy Crane." He turned a page. "The scarf. We took it from her room at the Spindrift last night, on a warrant. It's at the lab." He closed the folder. "And she inherits."

Archie said nothing. There was nothing left in him that would have helped.

"Sergeant," said Brannock, "get me an arrest warrant for Ivy Crane. Tampering with physical evidence, falsifying physical evidence. I'll call the AG's office and tell them what's coming."

Tess stood. For a fraction of a second she didn't move at all. Then she picked up her laptop and her coat.

"Yes, sir," she said, and went out, and didn't look at Archie on the way.

Then it was the two of them, and the heater, and Warren's grandfather on the wall.

"Trooper Lavoie is going to take your formal statement," said Brannock. "On tape, from the beginning. Thursday on. You're here voluntarily and you can leave whenever you want." He stood and picked up his empty cup. "I'd rather you didn't."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"Good."

Trooper Lavoie turned out to be a large, mild young man with a head cold and a small digital recorder, and he took Archie through it from the straitjacket on Thursday morning to the waiting room on Saturday night, and then took him back through the last two minutes of the séance four different ways. Archie answered the same way four times. He talked too much, the way he always did when he was frightened, and Lavoie let him and blew his nose and wrote things down. Brannock came and went with his phone at his ear. The tall windows went from white to gold to a deep cold blue, and someone brought the lieutenant another coffee, and nobody brought anything for Archie.

At 5:26 Lavoie said, "I think that's everything, sir," and turned off the recorder.

At 5:30 Archie came out through the town hall's front doors onto the granite steps.

The sun was behind the hill. Main Street was going dark from the bottom up — the Congregational church across the way still white at the steeple and gray below — and the streetlights were coming on one at a time along the sidewalk with a faint buzz. It was very cold. His coat was upstairs on the back of a dead woman's chair, and he couldn't face the stairs to get it.

A State Police cruiser came out of the lot beside the town hall, without lights, without hurry. Tess's gray Interceptor came after it. At the bottom of the hill, where Main Street met the harbor, both of them slowed, and turned left.

Left was Water Street. On Water Street was the Spindrift Inn.
