# Chapter 14

## Five-Card Draw

Sunday-night poker at Fairweather House had been Vivienne's idea, in September, and like most of Vivienne's ideas it had become a law of nature within a week.

They played in the dining room at Gus's long mahogany table, with a fire in the grate and a green felt cloth over the wood. The chips were Gus's own — heavy old clay ones in a walnut case, which clicked in a way that made Archie happy every single time. Nickel, dime, quarter. Bev had announced the stakes the first night, and nobody had questioned them since.

By eight o'clock Archie was down four dollars and seventy cents.

"I'll take three," he said.

Ozzie Lund dealt him three, one at a time. Ozzie was seventy, square and weathered, with a white fringe of beard and enormous wrists, and he dealt cards with the deliberation of a man setting a mooring. Beside him Vivienne, in a kaftan the color of a ripe plum, was eating cashews out of a silver dish and holding her cards very close to her pearls.

Archie picked up his three cards. A queen. A nine. Another queen.

Queens over nines. A full house.

He arranged his face into nothing, carefully. He had stood in front of thirty thousand people without letting them see a thing he didn't want seen; he could manage a dining room in Maine. He laid both hands flat on the felt and waited.

"Fold," said Bev, without even looking at her own cards.

"Fold," said Dale Pinkham.

"Fold," said Lenny, who had been folding every hand for an hour on the grounds that it was the only way to be sure.

Vivienne laid her cards face down on the felt and patted them, as if they were a dog that had disappointed her. "Darling, you're humming."

"I'm not humming."

"You're humming 'Hello, Dolly.'"

Ozzie folded too. Archie raked in a pot of eighty-five cents, all of it his own ante money, and looked around the table in genuine grievance.

"How," he said. "*How*. I didn't move. I didn't move a muscle."

"You stopped breathing," said Bev. "Then you started humming. Then you looked at the ceiling like the Lord had come down to see you personally." She shuffled, fast and without love. "You do it every time."

"You could learn to cheat," Dale Pinkham offered. He said it shyly. "I bet you could. I bet you could deal yourself anything you wanted. I've seen the specials."

"Dale. A magician who cheats at cards is just a crook with nice hands. It's against the code."

"What code?"

"*The* code."

"There's no code," said Lenny.

"There is a code, Lenny, and you've seen me walk out of a game in Reno over it." Archie anted a nickel. "I would rather lose to Bev every Sunday for the rest of my life than win once dishonestly."

"Good," said Bev, and dealt.

"You still want to go hauling?" Dale asked him, sorting his cards. "Me and Mattie are going out in the morning, before the weather turns. Five-thirty at the float. Bring that lobster of yours."

Archie had been angling for an invitation onto a lobster boat since August. He put down his cards. "Dale. *Yes.*"

"Don't be late. Mattie leaves people."

He lost that hand to Bev too, bluffing on a pair of fours that she called without blinking. It took him down to six dollars and five cents. She stacked his chips in front of her in a neat red column, and while she stacked she said, to the table generally:

"Pru Haskell says the State Police booked two rooms at the Spindrift from tomorrow. For a week. A lieutenant, from Augusta."

There was a little pause around the table. The fire popped.

Dale Pinkham looked very hard at his cards. "Brannock," he said, at last. "Neil Brannock. Major Crimes. He's—" He searched for the word. "Thorough."

"Is that good?" said Vivienne.

"It's thorough," said Dale, and discarded two.

Archie didn't take a card. He sat back in Gus's chair at the end of the table with his hands flat on the felt, and the idea arrived the way they always did, all at once and fully dressed, with a hat on.

"Hear me out," he said.

"No," said Lenny.

"Teddy Marchand." Archie leaned forward. "Teddy's show is about to end — she told me herself, she was quitting. Teddy also has an insurance policy on her through the end of the season; I heard him say so through a door. Now. What's the biggest television event in history? A medium who dies on camera, in the dark, talking to the dead, in the most haunted theater in New England. That's not a special. That's a *franchise*. And what does Lorelei want? A house somewhere warm, with no phone, and a stack of mysteries." He spread his hands. "Everybody wins."

"She did die," said Bev.

"*Did* she?"

"Archie," said Vivienne.

"A hospital where nobody'd ever seen her except on a bus. A doctor I'd never met. And then she's gone, into a room I couldn't go into." He was going faster now; he could feel it carrying him. "I've made a woman go gray onstage, Mother. Greasepaint and glycerin, twice a night in Vegas. Right now Lorelei Crane is on a beach in Baja with a paperback, and Teddy Marchand is on the phone to the network with tears in his voice, and the ratings—"

"There'd have to be two ambulances," said Dale Pinkham, with dawning wonder. "One for the — and then a second one, see, at the hospital, round the back—"

"*Exactly*, Chief—"

"She's dead," said Lenny.

"Lenny—"

"Last night you said poison. You told the police poison."

"Last night I was tired."

June's voice came from the hall, where she was sitting halfway up the front stairs with a library book and a blanket, in a position from which she could see the whole table and pretend she couldn't. "Dad."

"I'm just *saying*—"

"Dad. You were holding her."

He stopped.

The fire popped again. Ozzie dealt him two cards, very slowly, and Archie looked at them without seeing them.

Bev cleared her throat. She did not look at him. She picked up her coffee cup, found it empty, set it down.

"You brought my thermos back, anyhow," she said. "Took you long enough. I washed it. Cups and all." She took a card. "I wash everything that comes back. You'd be amazed what comes back."

Archie looked up.

It took a second to land. Then it landed, and he felt it go all the way down, cold. The thermos. The two cups that screwed onto the top. The cup he'd poured for Lorelei, and handed to her across the table, on camera, and which she'd drunk half of. The one thing in the world that might have proved the tea was only tea.

"She'd wash the Pope," said Dale comfortably, "if he sat still long enough."

"She washed my hat," said Ozzie. "Nineteen ninety-four."

"It needed it," said Bev.

Vivienne laughed. Lenny didn't. He was watching Archie across the table, and so, Archie realized, was his mother — the laugh had been for the room, not for him.

He didn't say anything funny. He couldn't find anything.

"Archie?" said Dale.

"Dad," said June from the stairs, in a different voice. "There's somebody in the driveway."

Headlights had swung across the dining-room windows and stopped. A car door shut. Boots on the porch. Lenny, who was nearest, got up and opened the front door before anyone knocked, and there was Tess Calloway on the porch in her charcoal coat with the cold coming in around her.

She looked past Lenny at the table — the fire, the felt, the chief of the Merrow Police Department holding a pair of jacks — and her eyebrows went up a sixteenth of an inch.

"Evening, Dale," she said.

"Sergeant." Dale put his cards face down, very fast.

"I've just come from Augusta." She didn't come in. She spoke to Archie, across the room, past Lenny's shoulder. "The autopsy's done. The toxicology's gone to the lab. I'll have it when I have it."

"Archie thinks she's in Baja," said Dale helpfully.

"I was in the room this afternoon, Dale." Tess said it quietly. "She's not in Baja."

Nobody at the table said anything. Archie looked down at the felt.

"You were at the Spindrift with Ivy Crane at one o'clock," Tess went on. "That I know about, because I was there. At four you were at Shannon Mayo's house."

"How do you—"

"It's Merrow." She took off one glove. "Stop. That's what I drove down here to say. Stop visiting my witnesses. Stop finding things in umbrella stands. Stop."

"Sergeant," said Vivienne. She had risen from her chair in her plum kaftan, and she was holding out her hand. "You look absolutely frozen, and you've driven an hour and a half to say one word. Come in and sit down. Ozzie, pull out a chair. One hand."

"I'm on duty, Mrs. Fairweather."

"Then you'll play carefully." Vivienne kept her hand out. "One hand. It would be rude to go straight back out into that."

Tess looked at her for a long moment. Then she stepped in, and somehow she was sitting at the end of the table opposite Archie, with her coat over the back of the chair and a stack of Vivienne's chips in front of her.

Ozzie dealt. Five each.

Archie picked up his cards. Nothing. Ten high, two hearts, two clubs, a spade. He took four, slowly, watching her across the table, and got nothing back. Ten high still.

Bev could read his face; fine. Tonight he made it a wall. When the bet came round he pushed in his whole stack, six dollars and five cents in red and white clay, and sat back and looked at Tess with no expression at all, while his fingers riffled three chips over and under, over and under.

Bev folded. Dale folded. Vivienne folded with a sigh. Lenny had never been in.

Tess looked at her cards. She looked at him.

"Call," she said, and pushed her chips in, and turned over a pair of sixes.

He turned over ten high.

"How," he said. It wasn't a performance this time. "My face didn't move. I *know* my face didn't move."

"It didn't." Tess began, without hurry, to stack his chips. "It's your hands. When you've got something, they stop." She nodded at his right hand, which was still riffling three red chips. "You haven't stopped doing that since I sat down. You had nothing." She stacked another column. "You did it on the stage this morning, too, before you went down to the umbrella stand. Your hands stopped. That's when I knew you'd thought of something."

Archie sat very still at the end of his grandfather's table.

His hands, he realized, had stopped.

Nobody watched his hands. That was the whole point of the face. For thirty years, in every room he'd been in, he had been the one doing the watching — and here was a woman in a charcoal coat, stacking his chips into neat red towers, who had apparently been reading him since the afternoon she walked into the common room at Sea View, and had kept it to herself until it was worth six dollars and five cents.

"Thank you, Mrs. Fairweather," said Tess, standing. She left the chips where they were and picked up her coat. "Walk me out, Mr. Fairweather."

---

The porch was cold and black, and the stars were out thick over the harbor. Out on the point, Merrow Light swept its green flash across the water and was gone, and came back. Her gray car was parked beside the hearse, so precisely parallel to it that it looked measured.

"You could have called," Archie said. "About the autopsy. You didn't have to drive all the way down here."

"No."

"So why did you?"

Tess pulled on her gloves. "I wanted to see your face when I told you to stop."

"And?"

She looked at him in the light from the dining-room windows for a moment. Then she went down the steps.

"And you're not going to," she said, over her shoulder, and got into her car.

He stood on the porch until her taillights had gone down Shore Road, and then went back in. Bev was dealing. June had gone back to her book on the stairs and was pretending she'd never looked up from it.

Vivienne was gathering the chips Tess had left. She stacked them neatly, red on red, and slid the whole pile back across the felt to Archie's place, and didn't look up.

"I like her," she said. "She doesn't need you to be wonderful."
