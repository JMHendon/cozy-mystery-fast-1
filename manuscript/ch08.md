# Chapter 8

## The Other Side

The dark in the Merrow Opera House was not like other darks.

Archie had sat in a lot of them. Dark was his business. He had been sealed in boxes and safes and coffins and once, memorably, a hollowed-out Steinway, and he knew that most darkness had a kind of grain to it — a little gray seeping under a door, a phone glowing in somebody's pocket three rows back, the ghost of an exit sign. Darkness was usually just light that hadn't been invited in.

This was the real thing. Marchand Media's drapes had swallowed the windows, and Hollis had killed every light in the hall, and for a moment after the house lights went the only thing left in the world was the small red bulb on its stand by the table, glowing like a coal. It lit nothing. It made a little red smear in the air and gave the dark something to press against.

He could hear everything. The radiators, knocking. The old building creaking in the wind off the harbor. Kev Duchesne breathing through his mouth beside him, fast and shallow. His mother's rings against his knuckles. Somewhere out in the hall, very faint, the electric hum of the cameras.

"Let us join our hearts," said Lorelei Crane.

Her voice came out of the dark across the table, low and warm and rich as a cello, and Archie felt the whole circle lean toward it the way plants lean toward a window.

She prayed. It wasn't a prayer to anything in particular — it was to *those who have gone before*, and *the loving ones on the other side*, and *anyone who has a message for those gathered here tonight*, and it went on long enough that Archie's eyes stopped straining for light and gave up and let the dark in. That was the purpose of it. He knew that. He'd written a whole segment once on the opening prayer: two minutes to let the sitters' eyes adjust to nothing, and their hearts adjust to hope.

Then the red bulb went out, and Lorelei's voice changed.

It dropped half an octave and slowed down and grew a faint, unplaceable accent — Welsh, maybe, or Bible. "*Good evening, my children.*"

Kev's hand jumped in his.

"Brother Amos is with us," Lorelei said, in her own voice again, softly delighted. "He's my guide. He'll be helping tonight. Brother Amos, there's a lot of love in this room."

"*There is a great deal of love,*" agreed Brother Amos, "*and a great deal of doubt.*"

That got a nervous laugh from somebody — Warren, Archie thought. He didn't laugh. He was counting. *Opening prayer. Control spirit. Now the raps.*

The raps came on cue: three sharp knocks, seemingly from inside the table itself, then three more. *Knee,* thought Archie. *Or toe against the table leg. Or a little clicker in her shoe.* He listened for the direction and couldn't pin it down, and was pleased with her for that, professionally.

Then, beside him, Vivienne gasped.

The hand bell had begun to ring.

It rang softly at first, a single silver note, and then again, and then it was moving — he could hear it travel across the tabletop, ringing as it went, and rise, ringing, into the air above them, and circle there.

*Somebody has a hand free,* he thought. *Two hands, probably. One of them is hers.*

He could feel the circle all the way around through his own two hands — his mother's dry, cool fingers gripping hard on his left; Kev's hot, damp ones on his right. He couldn't feel past them. Nobody could. That was the beauty of the circle. Everybody was absolutely certain of the two hands they held, and nobody knew a thing about anybody else's.

The bell dropped to the table with a clank. Something cold and wet and soft brushed across Archie's forehead and was gone.

Across the table, Warren Gilley gave a high, short yelp, like a dog stepped on.

Vivienne laughed — a startled, delighted peal of laughter in the dark, the laugh of a woman who had held the flashlight for her father at eleven séances and had just been touched on the cheek by a wet glove on a stick for the first time in fifty years.

"*There's no need to be frightened,*" said Brother Amos reprovingly.

"I'm not frightened," said Vivienne. "I'm nostalgic."

Kev made a strangled sound. From the stage-right wing, very faintly, came Teddy Marchand's murmur to somebody on his headset — *static, static, we've got the static, love it, love it* — and Kev's damp fingers squeezed Archie's so hard the knuckles ground together.

"It's the Crane Static," Kev breathed. "That's the — the spirits interfere with the cameras. It happens every time she gets close."

*Every time,* thought Archie. *On cue. Like a man flipping a switch.*

He didn't say it. He'd promised.

And then, in the dark above the table, a light began to rise.

It was three thin bands of pale green, glowing faintly, one above another. The trumpet. It lifted off the table slowly and hung there, and then began to float — up and away to the left, turning as it went, the bands tilting and swimming against the absolute black like something seen at the bottom of the sea.

It was beautiful. Archie had seen it done fifty times and explained it on television twenty, and it was still beautiful. *It works,* Gus had written, *because nobody in the room wants it not to.*

The trumpet stopped, high over the place where Shannon was sitting. It tilted toward her.

"*Shan,*" it said.

The voice came out of the air. It was a man's voice — or a woman's voice pitched low and roughened, coming down through two feet of aluminum cone — and it was hoarse and young and tired, and it had a Maine flatness in the vowels that Archie hadn't heard Lorelei use once in two days.

Shannon Mayo made a sound Archie had never heard a grown woman make.

"*Shan. It's me.*"

"Tommy?"

"*It's warm here, Shan. It's warm. The water's warm. I'm not cold anymore.*"

"Oh, God. Oh, Tommy—"

"*Tell the kids. Tell Liam I see him on the wharf. Tell Rosie—*"

Archie sat in the dark holding his mother's hand and a stranger's and felt it rise in him, the thing he had felt at nineteen in a church basement in Somerville, watching a medium tell an old man his dead wife forgave him and take forty dollars for it. He wanted to stand up. He wanted to drop Kev's hand and shout for the lights and walk around the table in the glare and lift Lorelei Crane's free hand up off the table and hold it up for the cameras and the crew and Merrow, Maine, to see.

He had promised Shannon he wouldn't humiliate her.

The voice in the trumpet was telling her about the kids. She was crying so hard she could barely breathe. If he did it now — if he flooded this room with light at this exact moment — the whole town would remember forever that Shannon Mayo had been sobbing to a tin horn when the lights came up.

He held on to the hands. He stayed in the dark.

"*I love you, Shan. I'll be waiting.*"

The green bands drifted away from Shannon, wavering. They hung for a moment in the middle of the air over the table. Then the voice changed again.

It was older now. Deeper. It had a crack in it and a laugh at the bottom of it, and it said:

"*Viv.*"

His mother's hand went rigid in his.

"*Vivi. Is that you, honey?*"

Vivienne didn't answer. Archie could feel her breathing, short and hard.

"*And Sparrow,*" said the voice. "*Sparrow's here.*"

Archie stopped breathing.

In the space where his breath should have been there was a memory he hadn't known he still owned: a kitchen, summer, a screen door banging, a big man with silver hair crouched down in front of a very small boy with a silver dollar between two fingers. *Watch closely, Sparrow. Watch my hands.* The smell of pipe tobacco and Brylcreem. The dollar gone. The boy's whole body lighting up.

*Sparrow.* Nobody had called him that in thirty-seven years. He hadn't remembered it himself until this second.

He knew how she'd done it, in the same instant — some old newspaper, some interview with a grieving town in 1988; a researcher with a library card. He *knew.*

And for one second, sitting in the black dark with the green bands floating over him, he didn't care. For one second he would have given her the two million dollars and the house and the hearse and everything else he owned if she had just kept talking.

"*The trunk, Sparrow,*" said Gus Fairweather's voice. "*You're looking in the wrong place. The answer's in the — the answer's in—*"

The voice caught.

It thickened and slurred. "*The answer'sh — in—*" It coughed. It coughed again, wetly, and the green bands lurched in the air.

"He's fading," said Kev, in a whisper of pure joy. "He's going—"

The trumpet fell.

It hit the edge of the table with a hollow aluminum *bonk* and rolled, and Archie heard it drop off the edge and clatter across the stage boards, and in the same instant a chair scraped hard across the floor, and Lorelei Crane said, in her own voice — a high, frightened, ordinary voice that sounded nothing like a cello —

"My mouth. My — I can't feel my mouth—"

Archie let go of both hands.

"*Lights!*" He was on his feet. "Hollis! *Lights!* Lights, now!"

The house lights came up all at once, white and enormous, and the whole circle flinched.

Lorelei Crane was slumped sideways in her chair at the head of the table. Her face had gone a terrible gray under the makeup, and it was shining with sweat, and her dark red lipstick was smeared at one corner as if she'd wiped her mouth with the back of her hand. Her eyes were wide open. Ivy was holding her left hand. Shannon, white as paper, still had her right.

Archie went straight across the table. He didn't go around it. He put one knee on the oak and went over, scattering the slate and the tambourine, and got to her as she started to slide out of the chair, and caught her, and went down with her to the stage boards, her head in the crook of his arm.

"Lorelei. Lorelei, look at me."

She looked at him. Her pupils were huge. Her breath was coming short and fast, and he could feel, through the velvet, her heart going in his arm like a bird thrown against a window — too fast, then a stumble, then too fast again.

"Somebody call—" he started.

"*Ambulance is coming!*" Hollis's voice, from up in the booth, high and carrying. "*I've got them on the phone! Keep her still!*"

Chief Pinkham was running up the aisle, his radio already at his mouth. Vivienne was on her feet with both hands over her face. Kev was standing behind his chair saying *oh my God, oh my God* in a small voice, over and over. Warren hadn't moved.

And Ivy Crane, at the corner of the stage just past her mother's chair, bent down and picked up the trumpet off the floor.

Archie saw it from where he knelt — down low, at the level of the boards, with Lorelei's head in his arm. He saw Ivy turn the narrow end toward herself. He saw her pull the silk scarf off her throat in one quick motion and wipe the mouthpiece with it, twice, hard, twisting — the way you'd wipe a spoon — and then push the scarf deep into the pocket of the coat on the back of her chair and set the trumpet down on the table.

It took perhaps three seconds. Nobody else was looking at her. Everybody else was looking at her mother.

Lorelei's hand found Archie's sleeve and closed on it. Her rings were cold.

"Honey," she whispered. Her lips were barely moving. "Did you catch me?"

He looked down at her — at the smeared dark mouth and the huge eyes and the white streak in her hair spread out over his arm — and he knew exactly what she was asking, and he did the only thing he could think of.

He lied to her.

"No," Archie said. "You were perfect."

Her mouth moved. It might have been a smile. Her fingers tightened on his sleeve.

Down the stairs, the doors banged, and boots came pounding up, and voices, and the rattle of a stretcher. Lorelei Crane's eyes stayed on his the whole time, wide and dark and full of something he couldn't read — as if, even now, she was waiting to see how the trick came out.
