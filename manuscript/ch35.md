# Chapter 35

## Pudding Lane

He had not been able to ask her all morning, because all morning she had been surrounded.

By eight o'clock on Halloween the opera house was full of people: three of the Pinkham cousins on ladders hanging the new lights, Deb Coombs hemming a curtain, the liars' table in a row of folding chairs at the back offering opinions about the ladders. And in the middle of it, with her clipboard and her ring of keys and her reading glasses swinging on their beaded chain, Hollis Tibbetts, calling cues up to the booth and sending Corey from the Galley to the hardware store for gaffer tape. Archie stood on a ladder beside her for an hour, handing up cable.

"Your tails need pressing," she told him, from the rung below, "and your hair needs cutting. I'll do the first. I wouldn't touch the second for a hundred dollars." She held up her hand for the next coil. "Elwin, if Gus owed your father three dollars, take it up with Gus."

Every time she laughed, it went into him like a splinter.

At noon he went up to Sea View, because it was Friday and he always went on Friday, and did a trick for Carol Gilley, and sat with Walt Calloway through the story about Gus and the police cruiser, and laughed in the right place. At half past two Hollis went home to finish June's costume.

At three o'clock he parked the hearse on Pudding Lane.

---

She was in the garden.

The house was what it always was: a weathered gray Cape with a blue door and a picket fence, books in every window, a dress form wearing half a Victorian bodice behind the glass of the front room. The long border along the fence, where the tall blue flowers had stood in September, had been dug over its whole length to bare brown earth. Only a row of dahlias was left at the end of it, their stalks blackened by frost. Hollis was on her knees beside them on a folded feed sack, in her men's cardigan and an old felt hat, lifting the tubers with a garden fork one clump at a time and laying them on newspaper to dry.

Malvolio sat on the roof of the potting shed in the last of the sun, watching her do it.

She looked up when the gate creaked, and smiled.

"You're early. June's jacket's on the dress form; I've only the hem to finish." She sat back on her heels. "I put the kettle on when I saw that hearse. Did you want tea?"

"No. Thank you."

She looked at him. Then she went back to the dahlias. The fork went in under the next clump, and the tubers came up out of the cold ground in a fat muddy knot, and she shook the dirt off them and laid them on the paper.

"You have to get them up before the ground freezes," she said, "or they rot. Everybody thinks dahlias are tough. They're the most delicate things in the garden." She reached for the next one. "Peter used to say I fussed over them more than over him."

Archie stood at the edge of the dug bed with his hands in the pockets of his buffalo-check coat. Driving over, he had worked out a dozen ways to do this. He could come at it sideways — a little warmth, a little talk, a question she wouldn't see the point of until it had already landed. He'd been doing it to strangers for thirty years.

He didn't want to do it to her.

"Hollis," he said. "What was your husband's name?"

The fork stopped in the ground.

She didn't look up right away. She looked at the dahlias, and at her own gloved hands on the handle, and then she sat back on the feed sack and looked at him the way she might have looked at a student who had finally, at the end of the year, read the book.

"Peter," she said.

"Peter Langley."

"Peter Langley." She nodded. "I was Hollis Langley for twenty years, everywhere but at the school. People forget that."

"Did Peter go to Rockland on Thursday nights, Hollis?"

She didn't answer that.

She pulled the fork out of the ground and laid it across the newspaper so its tines wouldn't touch the tubers. She took off her garden gloves and laid them on the fork. Then she got up off the feed sack, which took her two tries, and Archie didn't help her because she wouldn't have wanted him to.

"How did you know?" she said. She sounded curious.

"The library card. In Gus's book. *H. Langley, December 2003.*"

"Deb and her cards." Hollis shook her head. "Forty years she's been told to throw those out." She looked at the house. "Wait here."

She went in. The blue door closed behind her. Archie stood in the garden with the bare bed and the black dahlia stalks and the cat watching him from the shed roof, and the sun went behind the roofs across the lane, and the cold came up out of the ground all at once.

She came back out with a long white envelope under her arm and a mug of tea in each hand.

"The kettle was on anyway," she said, and held one out to him.

It was chamomile. He could smell it from where he stood: hay and honey, steam going up off it into the cold. The mug said MERROW PLAYERS — *TWELFTH NIGHT* 1997 and had a chip in the rim. Thirty feet behind her, on the roof of the potting shed, Malvolio yawned.

He knew what she was. He had known it at five o'clock that morning on a stool in the Galley. He knew what she had done with her hands, and how little time it had taken her, and that nobody had seen.

He took the mug and drank.

It was too hot, and too sweet. Hollis watched him over the rim of her own. He saw her see it — that he knew exactly what she was, and had taken a cup from her hand anyway — and her mouth moved once at the corner, and was still.

Neither of them said a word about it.

She set her own mug on the fence post and held out the envelope. It was sealed. Across the front, in a teacher's upright, unmistakable hand, it said *Det. Sgt. Theresa Calloway — by hand*.

"I wrote it last night," she said. "When they charged that girl with murder. It's all in there. What I did and how, and where the rest of it is, and that Ivy Crane didn't know a thing about it. I'd have brought it to Tess myself tonight, after." She looked at it. "I'd have liked to tell Vivienne first. I don't suppose that's possible now."

Archie didn't take it.

"Why?" he said. "Hollis. *Why?*"

"It's in there."

"I don't want to read it in a letter."

"No. I don't suppose you do." She was quiet a moment. "You'll get it, Archie. All of it. I'll tell you myself — not like this, in a garden, with the dahlias half up. Afterward."

"After what?"

"After the show."

He stared at her.

"Gus's show. Tonight. His night." Her voice was perfectly steady. "I've run the lights in that building for thirty years. I ran them for him at the end, when old Mr. Thibodeau's hands got too bad for the board — I was twenty-nine, and he let me run the board for the Empty Chair. I've never once missed a show. Not when Danny—" She stopped. "Not ever."

"Hollis—"

"I'm not going anywhere. Where would I go? I'm sixty-six years old and I've got a cat." She was still holding out the envelope. "Take it. If I don't come down out of that booth tonight, it goes straight to Tess. And if I do, it goes to her anyway, and I'll go with it. Either way. Tonight. You have my word."

He thought about Bev at five in the morning, saying *Archie* in that voice. He thought about Ivy Crane in Knox County Jail, who couldn't get out on a Friday night whatever anybody did, and who would walk out tomorrow morning if this envelope said what Hollis said it did. He thought about three hundred people, his grandfather's name on the poster, and two hundred and eighty light cues that lived in this woman's head and in a coffee-stained cue book in a drawer.

And in nine weeks she had never once lied to him. He hated himself for counting that.

He took the envelope.

"Thank you," said Hollis.

"I'm going to tell Tess," he said. "That I know. I'm not going to hide it from her."

"No. You shouldn't." Her face softened. "You're a terrible liar, you know. To people you're fond of. Vivienne says you always have been. It's the only thing about you that isn't a trick."

She went back into the house, and when she came out this time she was carrying a jacket over her arm: dark blue velvet, cut down small, with silver frogging across the front and a high collar. It had been one of Gus's. Archie recognized it from a photograph in the turret.

"For June," said Hollis. "The hem's done. I finished it while you were standing there working out how to ask me." She laid it in his arms on top of the envelope. "She'll look like a queen in it. Tell her to stand up straight."

The jacket was heavy, and warm from being indoors, and smelled faintly of cedar and of Hollis's house.

She picked up her fork and her gloves and looked down at the last of the dahlias still in the ground, and left them.

"Go on," she said. "You've got a show to do, and so have I." She touched his arm once, lightly, and went toward the house with the fork over her shoulder.

At the blue door she looked back.

"Break a leg, Archie," said Hollis Tibbetts, and went inside.

---

He sat in the hearse at the curb with the velvet jacket on the passenger seat and the envelope in the inside pocket of his coat, where he could feel its edge every time he breathed.

He wrote *Tess, I know who* and deleted it. He wrote *Tess, please come to the show* and deleted that too. Across the fence, Malvolio stood up on the shed roof, stretched, and jumped down out of sight.

He typed two words and sent them.

Then he started the hearse and drove to the opera house, where the lights were already on.
