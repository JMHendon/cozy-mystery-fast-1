# Chapter 35

## Pudding Lane

He had not been able to ask her all morning, because all morning she had been surrounded.

By eight o'clock on Halloween the opera house had been full of people — Mattie Pinkham and three of her cousins on ladders hanging the new lights, Deb Coombs hemming a curtain, the liars' table in a row of folding chairs in the back, offering opinions about the ladders. And in the middle of it, everywhere at once, with her clipboard and her ring of keys and her reading glasses swinging on their beaded chain, Hollis Tibbetts: calling cues up to the booth, sending Corey from the Galley to the hardware store for gaffer tape, telling Archie his tails needed pressing and his hair needed cutting and that she'd do the first and wouldn't touch the second for a hundred dollars. She'd been cheerful. She'd been brisk and funny and completely herself. He had stood on a ladder beside her for an hour, handing up cable, and every time she laughed it went into him like a splinter.

He couldn't ask her there. Not in front of Mattie on a ladder, and Deb with her pins, and Elwin Coombs telling everybody he'd known Gus Fairweather personally and Gus had owed his father three dollars.

At noon he'd gone up to Sea View, because it was Friday and he always went on Friday, and because he could not think of one other thing to do with his hands. He'd done a trick for Carol Gilley. He'd sat with Walt Calloway and listened to the story about Gus and the police cruiser again, and laughed in the right place.

At half past two Hollis had gone home to finish June's costume.

At three o'clock he parked the hearse on Pudding Lane.

---

She was in the garden.

The front of the house was what it always was: a weathered gray Cape with a blue door and a picket fence, books in every window, a dress form wearing half a Victorian bodice visible through the glass of the front room. The big front border had been cut to the ground and turned over for the winter and was bare brown earth now, except for a row of dahlias along the fence, their stalks blackened by frost. Hollis was on her knees beside them on a folded feed sack, in her men's cardigan and an old felt hat, with a garden fork in her hands, lifting the tubers out of the cold ground one clump at a time and laying them on newspaper to dry.

Malvolio, enormous and gray, was sitting on the roof of the potting shed in the last of the sun, watching her do it.

She looked up when the gate creaked. She smiled.

"You're early," she said. "June's jacket's on the dress form. I've only the hem to finish. Did you want tea?"

"No. Thank you."

Something in his voice made her look at him a second longer. Then she went back to the dahlias. She worked the fork in under the next clump and levered it up, and the tubers came out of the earth in a fat knot, brown and pale and muddy, and she shook the dirt off them and laid them on the paper.

"You have to get them up before the ground freezes," she said, "or they rot. Everybody thinks dahlias are so tough. They're not. They're the most delicate things in the garden." She reached for the next one. "Peter used to say I fussed over them more than over him."

The fork went in. The tubers came up.

Archie stood at the edge of the bare brown bed, where all those tall blue flowers had been in September, with his hands in the pockets of his buffalo-check coat. He had thought, driving over, about all the clever ways he could do this. He knew a hundred. He could come at it sideways, the way you come at an audience volunteer — a little talk, a little warmth, a question she wouldn't see the point of until it had already landed. He'd been doing it to people for thirty years.

He didn't want to do it to her.

"Hollis," he said. "What was your husband's name?"

She stopped with the fork in the ground.

She didn't look up right away. She looked at the dahlias, and at her own gloved hands on the handle of the fork, and then she sat back on her heels on the feed sack and looked at him for a long time, the way she might have looked at a student who'd finally, at the end of the year, read the book.

"Peter," she said.

"Peter Langley."

"Peter Langley." She nodded slightly. "I was Hollis Langley for twenty years, everywhere but at the school. People forget that. It's been a long time."

"Did Peter go to Rockland on Thursday nights, Hollis?"

She didn't answer that.

She pulled the fork out of the ground and laid it across the newspaper, carefully, so its tines wouldn't touch the tubers. She took off her garden gloves and laid them on top of the fork. Then she got up off the feed sack, which took her two tries, and Archie didn't help her because she wouldn't have wanted him to. She brushed the dirt from the knees of her trousers.

"How did you know?" she said. Not as if she were denying anything. As if she were curious.

"The library card. In Gus's book. *H. Langley, December 2003.*"

For a second something almost like a laugh went across her face.

"Deb and her cards," said Hollis. "Forty years she's been told to throw those out." She shook her head. "Wait here."

She went into the house. The blue door closed behind her. Archie stood in the garden with the bare bed and the black dahlia stalks and the cat watching him from the shed roof, and the sun went behind the roofs across the lane, and the cold came up out of the ground all at once, the way it does in Maine at the end of October, as if somebody had opened a door.

She came back out with an envelope.

It was a long white business envelope, sealed. Across the front, in a teacher's upright, unmistakable hand, it said *Det. Sgt. Theresa Calloway — by hand*.

"I wrote it last night," Hollis said. "When they charged that girl with murder." She held it out to him. "It's all in there. Everything Tess needs. What I did and how, and where the rest of it is, and that Ivy Crane didn't know a thing about it. I'd have brought it to her myself tonight, after." She looked at the envelope in her hand. "I'd have liked to tell Vivienne first. I don't suppose that's possible now."

Archie didn't take it.

"Why?" he said. "Hollis. *Why?*"

"It's in there."

"I don't want to read it in a letter."

"No," she agreed. "I don't suppose you do." She was quiet a moment. "You'll get it, Archie. All of it. I'll tell you myself, I promise you that — not like this, in a garden, with the dahlias half up. Afterward."

"After what?"

"After the show."

He stared at her.

"Gus's show." Hollis's voice was perfectly steady. "Tonight. His night. I've run the lights in that building for thirty years, Archie. I ran them for him, at the end, when old Mr. Thibodeau's hands got too bad for the board — I was twenty-nine years old and he let me run the board for the Empty Chair. I've never once missed a show. Not one. Not when Danny—" She stopped. "Not ever."

"Hollis—"

"I'm not going anywhere. Where would I go? I'm sixty-six years old and I've got a cat." She was still holding out the envelope. "Take it. If I don't come down out of that booth tonight, it goes straight to Tess. And if I do, it goes to her anyway, and I'll go with it. Either way. Tonight. You have my word."

He thought about Tess. He thought about Bev at five in the morning in the empty Galley, saying *Archie* in that voice. He thought about Ivy Crane in Knox County Jail, who couldn't get out on a Friday night whatever anybody did, and who would walk out tomorrow morning either way if this envelope said what Hollis said it did. He thought about the opera house tonight, three hundred people, his grandfather's name on the poster, two hundred and eighty light cues that lived in this woman's head and in a coffee-stained cue book in a drawer.

He thought — and hated himself for thinking it — that she had never once in nine weeks lied to him. Not about anything.

He took the envelope.

Hollis let out a breath. It was the only sign she'd given, the whole time, that she hadn't been sure he would.

"Thank you," she said.

"I'm going to tell her," Archie said. "Tess. That I know. I'm not going to—" He stopped. "I'm not going to hide it from her."

"No. You shouldn't." She looked at him, and her face softened. "You're a terrible liar, you know. To people you're fond of. Vivienne says you always have been. It's the only thing about you that isn't a trick."

She went back into the house again, and this time when she came out she was carrying a jacket over her arm — dark blue velvet, cut down small, with silver braid frogging across the front and a high collar, the kind of jacket a magician's assistant would have worn on the stage of the Merrow Opera House in 1960. It had been one of Gus's. Archie recognized it from a photograph in the turret.

"For June," said Hollis. "The hem's done. I finished it while you were standing there trying to work out how to ask me." She put it into his arms, on top of the envelope. "She'll look like a queen in it. Tell her to stand up straight."

He held the jacket. It was heavy, and warm from being indoors, and smelled faintly of cedar and of Hollis's house.

Hollis bent and picked up her garden fork and her gloves, and looked down at the last of the dahlias still in the ground, and decided, visibly, to leave them.

"Go on," she said. "You've got a show to do, and so have I." She touched his arm once, lightly, the way she'd touched it on the ladder that morning, and then she turned and went toward the house with the fork over her shoulder.

At the blue door she looked back.

"Break a leg, Archie," said Hollis Tibbetts, and went inside.

---

He sat in the hearse at the curb with the velvet jacket on the passenger seat and the envelope in the inside pocket of his coat, over his heart, where he could feel its edge every time he breathed.

He took out his phone.

He wrote *Tess, I know who* and deleted it. He wrote *Tess, please come to the show* and deleted that too. He sat looking at the empty screen. On the roof of the potting shed across the fence, Malvolio stood up, stretched, and jumped down out of sight.

Archie typed two words, and looked at them for a long moment, and sent them before he could take them back.

Then he started the hearse and drove to the opera house, where the lights were already on.
