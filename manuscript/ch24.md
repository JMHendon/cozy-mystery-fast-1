# Chapter 24

## Family Meeting

At ten o'clock on Tuesday night the kitchen at Fairweather House smelled of soup that nobody had eaten, and Archie was drawing a table.

He'd sat in the lobby of the Knox County Jail for an hour, on a bench bolted to the wall, until a steel door buzzed at nine-forty and Margaret Okafor came through it in a camel coat. Her client, she said, was eating a sandwich. Her client did not wish to see him. His part in this was the paying part, and he could do that from home.

So he was home, at the scarred farm table, with a yellow legal pad. He had drawn a circle for the table and seven small circles around it and labeled them in capitals — LORELEI at the top, then IVY, WARREN, MOTHER, ME, KEV, SHANNON — and a long skinny cone in the middle for the trumpet. Down the margin he had written *7:35 — TOOT*, and at the bottom of the page, *7:59 — KEV'S CAMERA.* Between the two there was a great deal of empty yellow paper.

A half-dollar walked across the knuckles of his left hand, over and under, first finger to little finger, and back. He didn't watch it. His hands had been doing that since he was eleven whenever the rest of him was busy.

Vivienne set a bowl of soup at his elbow. She was in a green kaftan with a man's gray cardigan over it.

"Eat that."

"I will."

"You've said 'I will' to three bowls," said his mother, and sat down across from him. "I counted."

June was at the far end of the table with her laptop open and her chemistry book shut beside it, which was the wrong way around for a Tuesday. When he'd come in she had looked up and said "Dad," in the voice that meant *I saw it on the news*, and he'd said, "I know," and that had been the whole conversation so far.

The back door opened on cold air. Lenny came in from the carriage house in all four sweaters, looked at the soup, looked at the legal pad, and pulled out a chair and sat down at the table — which he did not do, in this kitchen or any other, unless he meant to stay. Archie felt it like a hand on his shoulder.

Lenny turned the legal pad around with one finger. He brought his reading glasses down from his forehead and studied the drawing.

"Who touched it," he said, "after you?"

The half-dollar stopped.

It was the right question. Archie had spent four hours asking *why did she wipe it* and *what is she eating in there*, and Lenny, who in thirty years had never asked about anything but hardware, had walked in out of the cold and asked about the hardware.

"I put it down," Archie said. "Dead center. Where it was."

"Then."

"Then Teddy called cut, and Lorelei and Ivy went down to the green room, and I went to the back and talked riverboats with Dale." He made himself go through it properly. "Ivy must have come back up at some point. I looked under that table at seven-thirty and there was nothing under it but table, and in the dark something touched me on the forehead with a wet glove on the end of a stick. So she put the stick there afterward. I didn't see her do it. And Hollis. Teddy called from the wing for her to slide the horn over, it was fighting the candle, and she slid it a few inches and went off with her tray. That's all I saw. I was watching from a long way off."

Vivienne laughed.

It was her real laugh, the one from the bottom of her chest, and it was the first time he'd heard it since Saturday. "Hollis," she said. "Darling, Hollis Tibbetts taught half the State Police in this county where to put a comma. She isn't poisoning anybody. She hasn't the time. She's doing my hems."

At the end of the table June snorted into her sleeve, surprised out of it, and after a moment Archie heard himself laugh too. It was the first funny thing anybody had said in his kitchen in days.

Lenny didn't laugh. Lenny had not laughed since 1994. He put one blunt finger on the cone in the middle of the drawing.

"Then it's the girl," he said, "or it's nobody."

"It's not nobody." Archie took the pen back. "Somebody put something on that horn in twenty-four minutes, and I was at the back of the room being adored by a police chief." He tapped the empty margin. "The tripod cameras weren't recording. Teddy cut them — I heard him. *Cut controls, fifteen minutes.* And on Monday Brannock showed me every frame Marchand Media had given him. Me with the horn at my mouth. Me pouring the tea. The same two angles, dead level, wide. Tripods." He stopped. "Then the dark, from Kev."

Lenny waited.

"There was a third camera." The coin was moving again. "On a man's shoulder. Drifting around that stage the whole time I was at the back — the candles, the window, the empty chairs. If Teddy had handed that over, Brannock would have been dying to show it to me. Me, from a new angle, near the horn." Archie put the coin down flat on the pad. "Teddy didn't give it to them."

"Who's the cameraman?" said June.

"The big fella. Black watch cap. Gloves." Archie had met the man three times. He'd stood beside him on Saturday afternoon discussing the light off the harbor window. "His name is—"

"Marco," said Lenny.

"How do you know that?"

"He borrowed my gaffer tape. Thursday." Lenny pushed his glasses back up onto his forehead. "He brought it back." From Lenny, that was a recommendation for a medal. "You never look at the crew."

"I look at the crew."

"Thirty years you've had crews." Lenny folded his arms. "Who ran your flies in Las Vegas? Nine years. Every show."

Archie opened his mouth. A face arrived: young, sunburned, a goatee, a Dodgers cap. Nothing arrived with it.

"Dwayne," he said.

"Luis."

"Luis. Of course. Luis." He felt that land somewhere he didn't like, and didn't look at it.

June's laptop was already turned toward him. "Camera operators all have reels online," she said. "If he's Marco and he works for Marchand, he'll be on a crew list with his last name, and then he'll have an Instagram, and it'll say where he is. Give me twenty minutes."

"No."

"Dad, I found the posters in about an hour—"

"I know you did." He heard how it came out, and tried again, gentler, and it came out the same. "June. There's a woman in a cell in Rockland tonight because of something I said. I'm not putting your name anywhere near this. Not this one."

"It's not your name. It's mine." She said it very evenly. "I get to decide where it goes."

"Not tonight."

June looked at him down the length of the table, and he watched her decide not to argue. He'd seen her do it at a debate podium, when she'd judged a round was lost and her time was better spent elsewhere. She closed the laptop.

"Okay," she said.

She picked up her chemistry book and set the laptop on top of it and went out and up the back stairs. He heard her door close. June had never slammed a door in her life. She closed them precisely, all the way, until the latch clicked, which was much worse.

Vivienne didn't move. She sat with her hands folded on the table and looked at him.

"Archibald."

"Don't."

"That child is not a volunteer from the audience," his mother said. "You don't get to call her up onstage when it suits the act and then thank her very much and send her back to her seat." She stood, took the cold soup, and poured it back into the pot. "Stop. For one night. Sit in this kitchen with the people who live in it. It will all still be wrong in the morning."

"I can't, Mother."

"I know you can't," said Vivienne. "That's why I asked."

---

The turret was cold, and he didn't turn on the lamp. Every six seconds Merrow Light swept green across the windows and showed him the trunk under the north window with its faded gold stars.

He sat down on the floor in front of it. He hadn't been up here in three nights. He didn't take out the notebook.

*The trunk, Sparrow.*

Seven letters. He had known since Saturday night, in the dark, with the green bands floating over the table.

He put his fingers on the first dial and turned it to S.

The brass was cold. The dial clicked softly into place. He turned the second to P, and the third to A, and his fingers went to the fourth.

Lorelei Crane had found that word somewhere — an old *Packet*, some neighbor quoted in November 1988. Her daughter had found it with a library card and a laptop, and Lorelei had put it into a tin horn and used it to make his mother go rigid in the dark, on camera, for money.

He took his fingers off the dial.

He was not going to let a dead woman and her research open Gus's trunk. Not with that word. He spun the three dials back to nonsense and sat with his hands on his knees.

When he looked up, Vivienne was in the doorway with a fresh bowl of soup, and she had been there long enough. She didn't say anything about the dials. She came in and set the bowl on the rolltop desk, steaming, and touched his hair on her way past, and went back down the stairs.

His phone buzzed.

*Teddy Marchand posted a picture of his boarding pass an hour ago,* June had written. *Cape Air, Owls Head to Boston, 9:05 tomorrow morning. I'm not volunteering. I'm just telling you.*
