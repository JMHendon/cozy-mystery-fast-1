# Chapter 17

## Malvolio

Pudding Lane climbed away from Water Street for six houses, then lost interest and turned into a footpath through somebody's blueberry barren. Hollis Tibbetts lived in the fourth house: a small weathered Cape with a picket fence, a potting shed whose roof had been sagging since the Carter administration, and a blue front door. At six o'clock on Monday evening it was full dark, and every window she owned was lit.

Archie parked the hearse along her fence, which used up most of the fence, and sat for a minute listening to the engine tick.

He had not gone home. A news van from Portland had been parked at the bottom of Shore Road since four o'clock, and at a quarter past four his mother had texted him: *Don't come in the front, darling. There is a young man with a microphone in the rhododendrons and Lenny has gone out to speak to him.* At four-twenty: *Lenny is back. The young man has gone. Don't ask.* So Archie had driven around the peninsula instead, twice, in a black 1958 Cadillac hearse with his grandfather's name on the side in gold, which was not how a person of interest kept his head down. At five Hollis had called. She'd pulled some costumes for his show, she said, and there was a fish chowder on the stove, and he could come and take away either or both, and she had a fair idea which one he needed.

He hadn't argued. It was the first conversation he'd had since two o'clock that didn't have a lieutenant in it.

She opened the door before he reached the step — oatmeal cardigan, fingerless gloves, glasses on their beaded chain, and an apron that said MERROW PLAYERS · *THE MUSIC MAN* · 1994. She looked him over. Thirty-five years of sophomores had been looked over like that, on mornings they'd turned up without the book.

"You've been in that select board room," she said.

"Two hours and a quarter."

"It shows. Shoes off. Mind the cat."

The cat was on the third stair. He was large and gray and flat-faced, with yellow eyes, and he sat with his front feet together and considered Archie as if Archie had come to the wrong party in the wrong clothes and was not on the list.

"That's Malvolio," said Hollis. "He bit the Methodist minister at Easter, so you're in good company."

"Why Malvolio?"

"He's pompous, he has no sense of humor, and he believes the lady of the house is secretly in love with him."

"Is she?"

"Kitchen," said Hollis.

He followed her through the front room, which had stopped being a front room a long time ago. The Merrow Players lived there now, or their wardrobe did: thirty years of shows standing about on dress forms in the lamplight like guests nobody had told to sit down. A widow in black bombazine. A Union cavalryman. A Puss in Boots with one boot. A Lady Bracknell under a hat the size of a birdbath. A sewing machine on a card table by the window still had a hem caught under its foot, and there were books on every flat surface, books stacked up the side of the stairs, books propping one end of the sofa where a leg had given out. A woodstove ticked in the corner. Against the far wall an upright piano stood under a fringed shawl, its top crowded with photographs in mismatched frames.

The kitchen was low and warm and smelled of onions and salt pork and hot milk. Hollis ladled two bowls from a dented pot, set out oyster crackers and the heel of a loaf and a saucer of butter, and sat down across the oilcloth from him.

"Eat first," she said. "Then you can tell me how dreadful everything is."

He ate. It was haddock and potatoes and a great deal of black pepper, and it was the best thing that had happened to him since Saturday night. He finished the bowl. She took it and filled it again without asking.

Halfway through the second bowl, something heavy landed in his lap.

Malvolio turned around twice, kneaded Archie's thighs with great thoroughness, and lay down. He was very heavy and very warm.

Hollis stared at him. "Well," she said. "I'll be."

"Animals love me."

"They don't. That one doesn't. He's never sat on anybody in his life." She leaned over and looked at the cat with real concern. "He must be coming down with something."

Archie couldn't reach the bowl now without disturbing him, so he gave up on the bowl. He took a quarter out of his pocket instead and walked it across the knuckles of his right hand above the cat's head — over the forefinger, under the middle, over the ring finger and back. The yellow eyes followed it. A gray paw came up off Archie's knee and hung in the air, considering.

"Don't let him," said Hollis. "He'll have your finger off at the joint."

The paw went back down. Malvolio shut his eyes. He had decided the quarter was beneath him.

"So," said Hollis. "The show."

"I don't know that there's going to be a show."

"There is going to be a show. It's on the posters. Deb Coombs put one up in the library over the new fiction, which she has never done for anybody, including the governor." She buttered a piece of bread and handed it to him. "Go on, then. Say it."

He said it. He hadn't meant to say all of it, but the kitchen was warm and the cat was heavy and nobody here was taking notes. He told her that he kept seeing Lorelei on the stage boards with her head in the crook of his arm. That she'd asked him if he'd caught her, and he'd told her no. That he'd sat across a fire from her on Friday night and liked her better than anybody he'd met in years, and now she was in the medical examiner's office in Augusta, and he couldn't stop thinking that if he'd shouted for the lights five minutes sooner—

"Stop that," said Hollis.

"I'm only—"

"I know what you're only. Stop it." She put down her spoon. "I'm sorry she's dead. I'd be sorry if a bus had hit her. But that woman was a vulture, Archie, and she came to this town to pick it over, and half of Merrow will tell you so to your face and the other half will tell you after church. She took a thousand dollars off a girl with two babies and a husband on the bottom off Monhegan. Over the *telephone*." Her voice didn't rise. It went flatter, which in Hollis was worse. "I'm Rosie's godmother. I've had Shannon at this table more nights since February than I could count. Don't you sit in my kitchen and mourn Lorelei Crane like she was your sister."

"She was funny," Archie said.

"Plenty of vultures are funny. Have you ever watched one walk?" Hollis picked her spoon back up. "Eat your chowder."

---

When he'd finished, she made him put the cat on the floor, which Malvolio took as a betrayal of the first order, and marched him into the front room.

She had pulled three things for him and hung them on the back of the door. He tried on eleven.

He tried on a tailcoat from a 1971 *Pygmalion* whose sleeves stopped halfway down his forearms. He tried on a scarlet cape and a pair of felt horns from a *Faust* nobody in Merrow would admit to having seen. He tried on a fez. He tried on Puss in Boots's one boot, because it was there, and Hollis barked — the short, sharp, single note that was her laugh — and didn't look up from the seam she'd started letting out. He was standing in front of the long mirror by the stairs in a black-and-white striped convict suit from some melodrama, holding the number board under his chin, when he stopped clowning and looked at himself.

"The lieutenant would like me in this," he said.

"Then take it off. Don't give him ideas." Hollis was sitting on the arm of the sofa with her glasses on and the tailcoat across her knees. "What did he do to you?"

"Nothing. He was perfectly fair. That's what's so awful about him." Archie started on the buttons. "He's got film of me pouring her tea. He's got film of me with my mouth on the horn. And he said I fool cameras for a living."

"You do."

"He made it sound like a crime."

"Well, it isn't one, or they'd have to put a fence around Las Vegas." She bit off a thread. "That man sat in my booth this morning for forty minutes with a Dunkin' cup the size of a fire extinguisher and asked me to explain the dimmer board to him slowly. So I explained it slowly. Then I explained it again, more slowly, and he thanked me, and I said you're welcome, Lieutenant, there'll be a quiz on Friday." She held the tailcoat up to the lamp and squinted at the seam. "Archie Fairweather, I spent thirty-five years in a classroom watching people decide what they were going to be. I can spot a fool across a gymnasium with the lights off. You're not one. You're a show-off, which is a different thing entirely, and it isn't fatal."

He stood there in the convict trousers with his mouth open and found that he was grinning, for the first time all day.

"Is that your professional assessment?"

"It's going on your permanent record. Try the cape."

The cape was the last thing on the door: black wool, floor-length, with a high collar. It wasn't Gus's. Gus's lay folded on the trunk at home, lining out, shoulders in. But it was near enough, and when he put it on and turned to the mirror, the lamp behind him did something to the shape of it, and he stood still.

Hollis set the tailcoat down.

"I was in the balcony that night," she said. "Did you know that?"

He didn't.

"Twenty-nine. My second year teaching. Front row of the balcony with my coat on, because the radiators were worse then, if you can believe it." She was looking at him in the mirror. "From up there you can see past the edge of the proscenium into the stage-left wing. Nobody down in the house can. Before the Chair, he stood in that wing with his hand on the curtain for a full minute. The stage manager was beside himself — you could see him flapping. Gus didn't move. He was looking out at the second row." She got up and came over and lifted the cape off Archie's shoulders, briskly, and folded it over her arm. "I always supposed he was looking at you."

Archie didn't say anything for a moment. He hadn't known there had been a minute. He'd been ten years old in a clip-on velvet bow tie, waiting for the curtain to move, and the minute had simply been part of the waiting.

"I didn't know that," he said.

"Well. Now you do." She was already turning away. "Sit down, I want to see you in boots."

He sat on the piano bench and pulled on a pair of cavalier boots from somebody's *Three Musketeers*, and Malvolio came in from the kitchen, looked at him with loathing, and climbed back into his lap. The photographs on the piano were just above Archie's shoulder: a cast photo from some long-ago *Our Town*, a black dog in a snowbank, a man in oilskins squinting into the sun, and a boy of about twelve on a dock, sunburned to the ears, holding up a mackerel in both hands as if it had put up a tremendous fight.

"Who's the big-game fisherman?"

"Danny. My son." Hollis handed him a waistcoat over the cat's head. "Try this."

It was plum velvet, with a shawl collar and jet buttons, cut long in the body. He stood up, dislodging Malvolio a second time, and put it on, and it fit — across the shoulders, down the long back, everywhere a waistcoat had never once fit him off a rack.

"Made for a Scrooge in 2009," said Hollis. "He came down with shingles the week we opened, and the understudy was a forty-six. Nobody's ever worn it." She stood back with her head on one side, and nodded. "That one. And the tailcoat, once I've let it out. And there's a box of white gloves for your ushers, if you can find any ushers. Now go home. You've got a daughter."

She loaded him at the door: the waistcoat in a dry cleaner's bag over one arm, the tailcoat over the other, the box of gloves on top, and a foil-covered dish of chowder for his mother wedged under his chin. Malvolio watched from the stairs. The porch light was on, yellow on the step and the gate. Under it, the long border along her fence had been cut down to the ground and the bed turned over for the winter, and Archie went down the path past it with his arms full of velvet and got everything into the hearse without spilling the chowder, which he counted as the first unqualified success of his week.

The news van was gone from the bottom of Shore Road. He drove home with the window cracked and the heater roaring and salt and woodsmoke coming in on the cold, and he was halfway up the drive before he noticed he'd been humming.

The kitchen light was on. June was sitting at the end of the farm table in pajama pants and her school cardigan, with a pencil through her bun and her laptop open in front of her. It was half past nine. She had clearly been waiting up, and she had clearly decided not to look as if she had.

"Put that down, Dad," she said. "I need to show you something. Upstairs."
