# Chapter 32

## The Hatbox

The hatbox had come from a milliner on Newbury Street in Boston, a long time ago. It was round and deep, covered in green-striped paper gone soft at the edges, with a cord handle and a lid that fit too tightly, and at half past nine on Thursday night it sat in the middle of the kitchen table at Fairweather House where the monkshood had been, with Archie in front of it and nobody else awake.

His mother had gone up at nine with a hot-water bottle and the satisfied silence of a woman who had brought the house down in sequins. Lenny's light was out in the carriage house. The fog had come in after supper, and every so often, from out past the point, the bell buoy said something low and irregular about the swell.

Archie took the lid off.

Inside, standing on end like files, were small notebooks: thirty of them, the cheap drugstore kind with cardboard covers, in faded reds and blues and blacks, each with a rubber band around it. Most of the rubber bands had died and stuck. He picked one at random and peeled it open.

The pages were full of tiny, cramped, slanting marks in blue ballpoint. Not words. Clusters of consonants, numbers, dashes, small circles, a little wavy line, a sort of T with a bar through it, check marks. Every page, top to bottom, both sides.

He opened two more, and sat back.

Thirty years of a woman's working life, in a language nobody could read but her.

He was still sitting there at ten when he heard June on the back stairs. She came into the kitchen in her pajama pants and his old Harvard Square sweatshirt, with her hair down, and stopped.

"I couldn't sleep," she said. "The count keeps going in my head." She looked at the hatbox. "Is that it?"

He thought about telling her to go to bed. She had school in the morning and a show at night.

He pulled out a chair for her instead.

---

"It's code," June said, after a minute with one of the books. "Isn't it."

"It's shorthand. Her own." Archie had the third book open in front of him, and he was looking at a cluster of letters near the top of a page — *Lwstn* — and something at the back of his mind had gone very quiet and attentive. "Lewiston," he said slowly. "That's Lewiston. No vowels."

He turned a page. A small circle beside a number. A wavy line. The T with the bar.

And then he knew where he'd seen it, and it was so obvious that he laughed out loud.

"It's Gus's," he said.

"What?"

"Chapter nine of *The Dead Don't Whisper*. 'The Billet and the Blue Book.' The old mediums used to pass notes on their sitters from town to town — who'd died, how, what the widow wanted to hear — in a shorthand the Spiritualist camps had been using since the eighteen-nineties. Gus printed the whole table so people would recognize it if they ever saw it in a medium's notebook. Consonants only. Numbers for relations. A circle means passed. The wavy line is water. The T is the trumpet. A check means it landed." He could see the page in his head, the fold-out table on the cheap paper, his own nine-year-old finger moving down the columns on the turret floor. "She told me herself, by the fire at the inn. Every medium of her generation learned the trade out of his book."

"You memorized a table out of a book when you were *nine*?"

"I memorized everything in that book when I was nine. I thought there'd be a test." He pulled the nearest notebook toward him. "Find me 2003."

"Which one's 2003?"

He looked at the covers. There were no years on them. He opened one and looked at the top of the first page — *Th 3/9* — and opened another — *Su 11/2* — and understood.

"She didn't write the year," June said, reading his face. "Just the day and the date."

"Then we guess."

"We don't *guess*." She was already pulling the hatbox toward her side of the table. "If she wrote the day of the week, the date only lands on that day in certain years. March ninth is a Thursday in—" she had her phone out, scrolling a calendar "—1995. 2000. 2006. 2017. 2023. And the towns change, and the prices go up. You read me what she wrote. I'll tell you when she wrote it."

So that was how they did it. June laid the notebooks across the farm table in a long row and worked down it with her phone, a pencil and the back of an envelope. Archie read her the top of each book — first date, first town, first price — and she wrote a year on a yellow sticky note and stuck it on the cover. She wouldn't let him skip ahead, and twice she was right not to.

At eleven she stopped with her pencil in the air.

"Read that one again."

"*Mrs. Albert P., Biddeford. Four, passed. Wants to know did he suffer.*"

"Four is what?"

"Wife."

"Mrs. Albert Pomeroy's wife died."

"Apparently."

"And the man in Saco two pages back — you said his husband."

He looked. He turned back. He turned forward again, and read three more, and sat with his finger on the page.

"She moved everything over one," June said. "Every relation. One place."

"Of course she did." The grin came up out of him before he could do anything about it. "Gus published the table. Any sitter who'd read the book could have read her notes over her shoulder. So she shifted it." He looked at his daughter across a table covered in a dead woman's notebooks. "That's very good, June."

"It's arithmetic," said June, and wrote on the next sticky note.

---

After that he could read it, and he read it to her.

He'd expected a ledger, and they were a ledger, but Lorelei had written down more than the money. *Mrs. Albert P., Biddeford. Husband passed. Wants to know did he suffer. Told her no. Cried — check. Forty dollars.* *Old man, Saco. Wife passed. Wants to know is she still mad about the dog. Told him she loves the dog. Twenty-five dollars. Gave him back ten. He paid in quarters.* *Man, Bangor. Mother passed. Wants the will. Mother says look in the freezer. (It's always the freezer.)*

June laughed at that one, and then looked as though she wished she hadn't.

On a page in what she'd marked 2000, between two sittings in Augusta, there was a single short line by itself.

"*I., four today,*" Archie read. "*Pancakes. No sittings.*"

June didn't say anything. She took the notebook from him and looked at the line for a while, and gave it back.

By half past twelve the row of notebooks had sticky notes from 1995 to last year, and June had moved one stack apart from the rest. Three books. The towns in those three changed less than in any of the others.

"Rockland," she said. "Thursdays. Over and over, for two years, starting in the fall of 2001. Always the same initials first." She counted, touching each entry with the pencil. It took her a while. "Forty-one times."

Archie took the first of the three books and found the first Thursday.

*Th 10/4. Rcklnd, Pk St. — P.L., Mrrw. Lbstr. 6○ D. 15. ~ qry, 8/01. W. H. dsnt kno — NO calls hse. $350.*

He read it twice in his head before he read it aloud.

"P.L. Merrow."

June's pencil stopped.

"Lobsterman. Son passed. D. Fifteen. Water — the quarry. August of 2001." He kept his voice level. "Wife, H. Doesn't know. Don't call the house. Three hundred and fifty dollars."

Neither of them said anything for a moment. The refrigerator hummed. Out past the point the bell buoy rang, twice, and stopped.

"There's a quarry," June said. "Two miles inland. Mattie says everybody swims there in August."

"Keep going," said Archie, and turned the page.

He read her the two years. He tried to read them quickly, and couldn't. Thursday after Thursday, the same initials. The first time the T with the bar appeared, in December: *Son spoke through T. "Dad." Cried — check.* The price going up in the spring to something she called *special sittings*, two thousand five hundred dollars each. June added it all on the back of the envelope without being asked, and turned the envelope around so he could see the number, and he looked at it and turned it face down.

The lines that worked had check marks.

*"It's warm here" — check.*

*"I'm not cold anymore" — check.*

In October of 2003, near the bottom of a page, in the same tiny hand: *"Come see me on the water" — check.*

Archie stopped reading aloud.

June leaned over and read it herself, and read the mark beside it, and he watched her remember what the mark meant.

He turned the page. There were two more Thursdays, and then a gap. Then, in late November, one entry, without a day of the week.

*11/20. P.L. lost at sea. Saw it in the paper.*

And under that — not in shorthand, not in any code at all, in plain, ordinary, slanting longhand, the only ordinary words in thirty years of notebooks:

*God forgive me.*

And under that, back in the code: *No more T for privates.*

---

The kitchen was very quiet.

"Lost at sea," June said finally. "In November 2003."

"Peter Langley," said Archie.

She looked up.

"It's on the memorial. The granite at the end of the wharf." He could see it the way he saw everything he'd ever read once — the cold pink morning, Shannon in his coat, the rows of names. "*Arthur Coombs, 1978. Robert and James Haskell, 1991. Peter Langley, 2003.*"

June's face went still. Then it went white, starting at the mouth.

"Langley," she said. "H. Langley."

"June—"

"On the card. In Gus's book. The sign-out card." She was looking at nothing. "W. Calloway, eleven, eighty-eight. M. Pinkham. And then H. Langley, twelve, oh-three. I've signed under it three times." Her voice was very small. "December 2003. A month after. Somebody named H. Langley went to the library and took out Gus's book about how mediums fool people."

The wife who didn't know. Who found out.

Archie didn't answer. He was somewhere else: in a small warm house on Pudding Lane on Monday night, with a fat gray cat on his knees and fish chowder in his stomach, holding a photograph in a frame that he'd picked up off a piano. A boy on a dock, squinting into the sun. *Danny,* Hollis had said, already turning away to the costume rack. *My son.* And she had handed him a velvet waistcoat and talked about buttons.

He didn't say it.

June was watching his face. He had never in his life been able to hide anything from her that mattered, and he watched her see it, and he watched her refuse it.

"You're thinking about Mrs. Tibbetts," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You didn't have to." She put the pencil down. "She's Tibbetts. Not Langley. There must be fifty women in this town whose name starts with H. Helen. Heather. Half of them are probably Coombses. And lots of people are named Danny, Dad." She was talking fast now, in her debate voice, building it. "Lots. It's a coincidence. It's a coincidence until it isn't, and you check it. The *Packet*'s on microfilm at the library. Deb opens at nine. There'll be an obituary. There'll be a — something. We check it."

He looked at his daughter across the table: sixteen, white to the lips, holding a dead woman's notebook in both hands.

"Lots of people are named Danny," he said.

"Right." She let out a breath. "Right."

"Go to bed, Junebug. You've got school. You've got a show."

She didn't move for a moment. Then she squared the three notebooks with a sharp tap against the table, the way she squared a deck, and set them by his elbow, and stood.

At the foot of the back stairs she stopped.

"We'll check it," she said. "In the morning. Both of us."

"Goodnight, June."

She looked at him a second longer than she needed to. Then she went up, and he heard her door close, and the house was quiet.

---

He didn't go up.

He sat at the farm table with the hatbox and the long row of notebooks with their yellow years, and the one book open at November. After a while he took off his grandfather's watch and wound it, slowly, all the way, the little crown clicking under his thumb, and put it back on. It was two o'clock. Then it was three.

The fog pressed white against the windows. Every six seconds, through it, Merrow Light went green and faded, green and faded, and the bell buoy off Shag Ledge kept ringing, low and uneven.

He kept his hand flat on the page, over the only plain words in thirty years, and sat there until it was time for the Galley to open.
