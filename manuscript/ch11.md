# Chapter 11

## Walkthrough

There was yellow tape across the doors at the top of the opera house stairs, and Chief Dale Pinkham lifted it for Archie with the solemn joy of a man opening a velvet rope.

"Sign in, please," he said, holding out a clipboard. "Name and time. Sergeant's rules."

*A. Fairweather, 9:58.* The top name on the sheet was *T. Calloway, 7:15*.

The fog had burned off, and the one bare window at the stage-left end let in hard white Sunday light that made the hall look shabby: black drapes sagging on their rods, cables taped across the floor. On the stage the round oak table stood where it had stood last night, its seven chairs pushed back at seven angles. The candles, the glasses, the bell, the slate and the trumpet were gone. In their places sat small yellow plastic tents with numbers on them.

Tess Calloway was on the stage beside a man in blue nitrile gloves who was photographing the floor. She was in the navy fleece today, MAINE STATE POLICE over the heart, and she had slept about as much as he had. She looked at him, and then at his coat.

It was Gus's pea coat from the hall closet. It smelled of cedar, and the sleeves stopped two inches above his wrists.

"Where's your coat?"

"Ivy Crane has it."

She filed that without comment. "This is Gagnon, from the crime lab. He bagged the table last night." The man in gloves lifted a hand without looking up. "Officer Bissonnette."

Kayla Bissonnette was twenty-four, compact and very upright in a Merrow PD jacket, with her dark hair in a braid down her back that Archie would have bet hadn't been a braid in August.

"Here's what I want," Tess said. "Tell me what a woman like Lorelei Crane brings into a room to do what she did, so I know what I'm looking for. Tell me from where I put you. Don't touch anything."

"Ladies and gentlemen," said Archie, "the medium's toolkit. A telescoping rod, black, like a car antenna, for the bell and the tambourine. A damp glove on the end of it, for the touches. And—" He nodded at the two cameras, still on their tripods with evidence tags on their legs. "Something that kills the picture on cue. Every time she reached a good part, the feed went to snow. Kev Duchesne thinks that's the spirits. It's a button."

"Could be a button."

"It's a button."

"Could be. I don't have a button. I have a woman who died in a hospital and seven people who were in the dark." She turned. "Mrs. Tibbetts. The prop room, please."

Hollis was at the top of the stage-left steps with her ring of keys in both hands, going through it. She had been going through it, Archie gathered, for some time.

"It's a short brass one," she said. "It used to have red nail polish on the bow. Somebody took the nail polish off."

Archie went down into the stage-left wing to the plain wooden door with its hundred-year-old warded lock. He'd been opening locks like it since he was eleven.

"Officer Bissonnette. May I borrow a bobby pin?"

Kayla's hand went to her braid. She looked at Tess. Tess looked at the ceiling. Kayla handed one over, and Archie bent it straight, put a crook in the end with his thumbnail, slid it into the keyhole, found the ward, lifted and turned.

The bolt went back. Four seconds.

Behind him, Hollis held up a short brass key. "Here it is."

"Mr. Fairweather," said Tess, very evenly. "Would you like to be arrested now, or after the tour?"

"I didn't touch anything. I touched a lock."

"A lock is a thing."

"After the tour," Archie decided, and returned the bobby pin, which was no longer any shape a bobby pin could use. Kayla put it in her pocket with care.

The prop room was narrow and cold and smelled of old paint and mice: a chipped sink, a headless dress-form wearing half a pirate, and shelves to the ceiling crammed with the Merrow Players' history — a papier-mâché moose head, a crate of tin swords, a pyramid of hats. On a middle shelf was an empty space about three feet long with a clean rectangle in the dust.

"That's where their things were," said Hollis. "Thursday night till yesterday afternoon. Their crew brought it all up for the show."

Archie wasn't looking at the clean rectangle. He was looking at the shelves. They were built of old crate boards, gray and splintery, and on the end of one, half under a hat, was a word stenciled in faded black: ST. PIERRE.

"Sergeant," he said. "Hear me out."

"No."

"Saint-Pierre. French island off Newfoundland. In Prohibition it was the biggest liquor depot in the North Atlantic. Every rum-runner between here and Boston loaded there. These are Saint-Pierre crates, in a town hall on a harbor, with a trapdoor in the stage and a hatch to the alley. This building was a drop, Sergeant. Nineteen twenty-four. And somebody unlocked this door on Friday night looking for something that's been hidden in here for a hundred years, and Lorelei—"

"Mrs. Tibbetts," said Tess, without turning her head, "is anything missing from this room?"

"Not that I can see."

"Then nobody stole the rum." She stepped back into the wing. "Gagnon, the shelf, please. Mr. Fairweather, stand in the light where I can see you."

He went and stood in the light.

---

"Show me," said Tess.

They were back at the table. She'd listened with her arms folded while he explained the hand release, and then she'd said those two words, and Archie felt the old thing go through him: an audience that wanted to see.

"Two volunteers. Chief, you're Ivy."

Dale Pinkham looked at the chair at the medium's left, and then at Tess, with naked longing.

"Sit," said Tess.

He sat.

"Officer Bissonnette, you're Lorelei. I'll be Shannon." Archie took the chair at the medium's right. "Shannon held this hand from start to finish. I believe her. So whatever came loose came loose on the other side." He put the chief's right hand into Kayla's left. "Chief, you're the plant. You're in on it. Close your eyes. Now I need a trumpet."

"The trumpet's in a bag in Augusta," said Tess.

"Something like it. Conical. About two feet."

Kayla was already up. They heard her boots on the stairs, the street door, a car trunk, and then she came back up two at a time with an orange traffic cone under one arm and set it on the table at her place.

"Officer," said Archie, "you're going to go far."

She went pink, sat, and took the chief's hand again. Archie took a teaspoon off the tray of Hollis's tea things in the wing, cold as the room, and pressed it under the table into Kayla's free hand.

"It's very dark," he said. "Everybody in this circle could swear on a Bible to the two hands they've got. The medium says the spirits are restless. The plant squeezes once—"

The chief squeezed. His knuckles went white.

"—and lets go."

Dale Pinkham, eyes screwed shut, let go and put his hand flat on the table.

"Now the medium's got a hand free, and so has the plant, and nobody else at the table knows. Officer, pick it up and say something."

Kayla raised the traffic cone by its narrow end, bright orange against the black drapes, put the small end to her lips and said into it, in a voice as flat as a parking ticket, "*Shan. It's me.*"

It came out of the wide end enormous and rang off the pressed-tin ceiling.

"And while everybody's looking up at that," said Archie softly, "the medium does this."

Kayla had been told nothing about the spoon. She worked it out anyway. She reached across with her free right hand and laid the cold steel bowl flat against the back of the chief's neck.

Dale Pinkham went straight up off his chair — the same four inches he'd gone off the stool at the Galley on Friday. His eyes flew open. He looked at the spoon and the cone, and then at Archie, wounded to the core.

"I was *in* on it," he said. "I was the *accomplice*."

Somebody laughed.

It was short and startled and got out before anyone could stop it. Archie turned his head. Tess Calloway stood at the edge of the stage with one hand pressed over her mouth and her shoulders moving, once, and her eyes on the floor.

She took her hand away. Her face was perfectly composed.

"Go on," she said.

He didn't say a word about it. He would think later that it was the most disciplined moment of his career. He wanted very badly to make her do it again.

"That's the trick," he said instead. "And whatever was on the small end of that trumpet went into the one mouth at the table that was meant to touch it."

"Or on something else, or in something else," said Tess. "Could." She came up the steps. "Mrs. Tibbetts, a couple of things for the record. Who's had the key to that prop room this week?"

Hollis came out of the wing with the ring clanking at her hip. "Me. It lives on my ring. And Warren Gilley — I lent it to him Friday afternoon for his inventory, the posters and fixtures for the closing. He gave it back at the Galley that evening. Saturday morning the door was unlocked. He says he locked it."

"And the props on stage last night. Who handled them?"

"Everybody. Mr. Fairweather inspected the lot on camera and put his mouth on one. I lit the candles, the permit's in my name, and I set the water glasses. Mr. Marchand asked me to slide the horn over, it was fighting the candle for his camera, so I slid it. Ivy came and went. That young man in the sweatshirt photographed everything until they made him stop." She shrugged. "It was a stage, Theresa. People touch things on a stage."

Tess wrote. Archie had stopped listening.

*Warren.* Warren, who'd gone white as milk when Lorelei leaned over and murmured in his ear. Warren, who'd left the stage at seven-forty holding his stomach. Warren, with a key to the room where the trumpet had spent two nights.

"Warren Gilley," he said.

"No," said Tess, still writing.

"I didn't say anything."

"You said Warren Gilley in a voice."

Hollis looked at Archie over her glasses as if he'd read the first chapter and announced how the book ended, and went back into the wing.

Archie walked to the lip of the stage and stood with his back to them. He had Gus's old gold watch on, and he found he was winding it in small turns between finger and thumb while he ran the end of the séance again.

Lights up. Ivy wiping the horn, pushing the scarf into a pocket. The medics, the stretcher, Ivy following her mother down the stairs with no coat and the handbag in her hands. And somewhere on her, all through the dark, a thing with a button.

You didn't walk out past a police chief with the method in your pocket. You reset. You put it down somewhere ordinary on the way out, where nobody would look.

He was moving before he'd finished thinking it — up the aisle, under the tape ("*Sign out*," said the chief, faintly), down to the landing where the stairs turned beneath the poster of THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER DEFIES THE ASYLUM. In the corner stood a cast-iron umbrella stand holding four dead umbrellas. It had been there so long nobody saw it.

He reached down among the umbrellas and closed his fingers on something small and hard.

It was a black plastic remote the size of a matchbox, with a single gray rubber button and a stub of antenna. He held it up into the light from the stairwell window.

"Mr. Fairweather."

Tess was on the stairs above him. Gagnon was behind her, pulling an evidence bag out of his jacket.

"Put it on the step," Tess said. "Now. Take your hand away."

He put it on the step.

"You've just put your fingerprints on that." She didn't raise her voice. "On top of whoever else's are on it. If it's anything at all, you've made it a third less useful to me and a great deal more interesting to my lieutenant."

"It was in the umbrella stand."

"I can see where it was. I'd have liked Gagnon to see where it was."

She watched the tech lift the remote into the bag with two gloved fingers and seal it and write on the seal. Then she turned her back on Archie and made a call.

"Calloway. Is the Crane autopsy still this afternoon? — Good. Before anybody cleans her up, I want her lips swabbed. The lipstick, the skin, the inside of the mouth. Separate swabs. — Because I'm asking. Thank you."

She put the phone away and looked down at him on the landing, in his grandfather's coat with the sleeves too short.

"Autopsy's at two, in Augusta. I'm going up for it. I've asked them to look for your wolfsbane."

"Aconite," said Archie. "Wolfsbane's the garden name."

She came down two steps, so that they were nearly level.

"If they find it," she said quietly, "you understand where that leaves you."

He'd understood it last night by the vending machine, and again at breakfast with June counting on her fingers. He looked at the little black box in Gagnon's bag with his own prints on it now, and found that what he mostly felt, God help him, was an itch to know whose were underneath.

"Yes, Sergeant," he said.
