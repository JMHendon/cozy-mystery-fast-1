# Chapter 26

## Forty Seconds

At eleven on Wednesday morning the opera house was still dressed for a funeral.

Marchand Media's blackout drapes still hung over every tall window; Hollis had volunteers coming at four to take them down. The cables and tripods were gone, and the police had released the hall on Tuesday morning and taken the props to the lab. What was left stood center stage under two work lights on stands: the round oak table and its seven chairs, exactly where the crew had left them, with little torn corners of the evidence tech's tape still stuck to the boards around the legs. Rain ticked on the windows behind the drapes.

Lenny had brought a laptop up from the workshop, and a card reader, and fifty feet of orange extension cord, which he'd run from the outlet by the prop room in the firm belief that electricity was out to get him. He set the laptop on the table in front of Lorelei's chair and held out his hand.

Archie put the little plastic case in it.

Lenny opened it, looked at the card, and didn't plug it in.

"What did it cost?"

There was no point lying to Lenny. There never had been. "The Glass Box," Archie said.

Lenny brought his reading glasses down off his forehead, looked at him through them, and pushed them back up.

"You told him."

"On a napkin. He signed an agreement."

"He's a television producer."

"It was a very good napkin, Lenny."

"Eleven months," said Lenny. "In a garage. In Henderson. In August."

"I know."

"A hundred and nineteen degrees. The resin wouldn't set."

"I know, Lenny."

Lenny slid the card into the reader and plugged the reader into the laptop with more force than any connection has ever needed, and after that he said nothing at all, which for Lenny was the same as shouting the building down.

There were footsteps on the stairs. June came up into the hall at ten past eleven in her Doc Martens and a brown thrift-store cardigan, with her laptop bag on one shoulder and her face set. It was a teacher-workshop day at Penobscot Regional, which she regarded as a personal insult, and she had evidently heard the hearse leave the house and worked out where it was going. She stopped at the top of the center aisle among the stacked chairs and stood there, ready to be sent home.

Archie looked at her down the length of the hall. Then he went to the table and pulled out a chair — the one at the right of Lorelei's place — and stood behind it.

"That's Shannon's," he said. "You can be Shannon."

June came down the aisle and up the steps and sat in it. She took the pencil out of her bun and a legal pad out of her bag and laid them on the table, squared to the edge.

"Okay," she said. It wasn't last night's okay. He'd take it.

"What did you give Teddy?" she said. "For the card."

Lenny, at the laptop, didn't look up.

"The Glass Box," said Archie.

June looked at him for a long moment. Then she wrote the date at the top of her legal pad and underlined it twice.

The footage came up.

It was a low angle from somewhere near the stage-left wing, handheld but steady, the picture warm and grainy under the work light, with a timecode running in the corner: 19:36:02. The first thing on the screen was Archie's own elbow.

He watched himself at the table in his black shirt with the sleeves pushed up. He watched himself unscrew the cups from Bev's thermos and pour, and hold one out across the table to a woman in black velvet, who laughed and moved her daughter's hand aside and took it. There was no sound for the toast; Marco had been too far away. Lorelei drank half and set the cup down. Archie watched her set it down, and for a second he couldn't watch anything else.

"Seven forty-one," said June, writing. "Cut."

On the screen Lorelei rose and went out of the frame toward the stage-right stairs, and Ivy rose and followed with the water bottle and the lozenges. The work light went down. The camera stayed on the table. On the audio, faint, came Teddy's voice from the wing: *Marco, stay on the table for me. Give me the table breathing. That's the cold open.*

"That's why he never stops," said June.

The table breathed. Empty chairs; the bell, the tambourine, the slate; the trumpet in the center, its three pale bands catching what light there was.

At 19:43:50, Ivy Crane came back up the stairs alone.

She came in from the right and went straight to her own chair, the one at her mother's left. She didn't sit. She bent, half turned from the camera, and her hands went under the overhang of the tablecloth at her own place and slid something in — long and thin and black — and smoothed the cloth down over it. It took about ten seconds. Then she straightened and went back out of the frame the way she'd come.

The trumpet sat in the middle of the table the whole time. Her hands never came nearer to it than the table's edge.

"That's the rod," said Archie. "That's how Warren got kissed on the ear in the dark."

Lenny took a tape measure out of one of his sweaters and ran it from the edge of the real table, at Ivy's place, to the center.

"Twenty-three inches."

"Run it back," said June. Lenny ran it back. Ivy's hands went under the cloth and came out empty and went away. "She never reaches," June said, and wrote it down.

Something was rising in Archie that he didn't trust yet. He sat on it.

Seven forty-six: Kev Duchesne leaned into the frame with his phone held out in both hands, photographing the table like a man photographing a celebrity's lunch, and Teddy's voice came sharp off camera — *Phones away, please, sir* — and Kev jumped and backed off toward the radiators on the stage-left wall, where he stayed, a dark hoodie at the edge of the picture. Seven forty-seven: Warren Gilley crossed behind the chairs with one hand on his stomach and was gone. Seven forty-eight: Hollis in a pair of black stage gloves, bending over the table with a long match. She lit the tall pillar candle in the middle, a hand's width from the trumpet, and then the small ones around the edge. She went off and came back with a tray and went around the circle setting a glass of water at every place. Vivienne came into the frame and fussed with a black shawl over the back of her chair until it hung exactly so. Shannon came up the steps and sat down at her place and put her hands in her lap.

"Does he ever point it somewhere else?" said June. "Ever?"

"Find out," said Archie.

She took the trackpad and did it the way she did everything, with a method: forward ten seconds at a time, writing down what was in the frame at each jump — *table, table, table* — all the way down the page. Lenny watched her with his arms folded. Archie watched the candles.

At 19:51:10 the picture went white.

It bloomed out from the center — the tall flame and its reflection in the polished curve of the trumpet right beside it, flaring together in the lens until the whole frame was a smear of gold — and the camera swung away fast, to the right and up, and settled on the one bare window at the stage-left end of the hall. Out in the dark harbor the riding lights of the moorings were coming on, one by one.

On the audio, Teddy: *Hollis — sorry — can you slide the horn left, it's fighting the candle.*

The harbor. The moorings. A little bob as Marco adjusted something. The harbor.

At 19:51:50 the camera swung back to the table.

The trumpet sat a few inches to the left of where it had been, out of the candle's way. Hollis was walking off toward the stage-right wing with her empty tray under her arm. Behind her, a young man from the crew in black gloves knelt to tape down a cable, and at the very edge of the frame Marco's own black-gloved hand came up and did something to the lens and went away again.

"Forty seconds," said June.

She ran it again, and a third time. Then she went through the rest of the card, ten seconds at a time, to the end, and came back, and put her pencil down.

"That's the only time," she said. "From thirty-six to fifty-nine he never leaves the table, except for those forty seconds."

"So who's there?" said Archie. "In the forty seconds."

June read it off her list, flat, the way she'd read out a citation. "Mrs. Tibbetts, with the tray. Grandma, at her chair. Shannon, at her place. Kev, by the radiator." She looked up. "And the crew going back and forth. You can see two of them at least. Everybody back there's in black gloves."

Archie got up and went around the table to the chair beside his own, the one with the shawl in the footage, and stood behind it. He put his hand out over the tabletop. The center was right there. Anyone at that table could have touched it without leaning. That was what a round table was for.

His mother had been standing exactly here.

He took his hand back.

"Walk it," he said.

They walked it. June sat in Shannon's place with her phone's stopwatch running and reached for the center: three seconds. Lenny stood at the radiator for Kev, walked to the table, touched the middle of it, and walked back: twelve seconds. Archie stood where Hollis had stood and slid an invisible horn; then he stood in Vivienne's place and reached. Every one of them fit, with time left over to stop and say good evening.

"And anybody from the crew could have walked through," said June.

Lenny looked at the radiator as if it had personally let him down.

At 19:55 on the laptop, Archie walked into the frame himself. He watched himself cross to his mother's chair and sit beside her and put his hand on the table, and turn it over when she laid hers on top. On the screen, in the candlelight, she looked very small.

"You're not in the forty seconds, Dad," said June. "You're not on camera at all until fifty-five."

"I was at the back with Dale."

"Dale was with him," said Lenny.

"I know," said June. "I'm saying what the lieutenant's going to say."

Archie barely heard her. He was looking at the frozen frame on the laptop — the little flames, the empty chairs, the horn moved a few inches to the left — and the thing he'd been sitting on since seven forty-four came up through him anyway, all at once, without permission.

"She never touched it," he said. "Ivy. Not after I put it down. She was at her place for ten seconds with her hands under the cloth, twenty-three inches away, and then she was downstairs until seven fifty-eight, and then she sat down, and then Kev's camera has it lying there in the dark until Lorelei picks it up herself." He was on his feet. He didn't remember standing. "The rod's a fraud, fine. The scarf's a reset. The whole act's a fraud. But she never laid a finger on that horn after I had it at my mouth."

"Then it's one of four people," said June. "Or the crew."

"Ivy didn't do it," said Archie. "That's what matters."

June looked at him and opened her mouth, and then shut it again and wrote something on her legal pad that he didn't read.

Lenny ejected the card, put it back in its little case, and held it out.

"Where are you taking it?"

"Tess," said Archie. He took it, and he was halfway down the aisle before Lenny had finished winding up the cord.
