# Chapter 21

## Spectral Kev

By ten o'clock the fog had burned off, and Main Street was all hard bright light and long blue shadows, the kind of late-October morning that makes white clapboard hurt to look at. The yellow tape was gone from the town hall's side door. Officer Bissonnette had taken it down at nine, rolled it up and put it in her cruiser, and Archie knew this because Kev Duchesne had narrated the whole thing, live, to six hundred thousand people.

Kev was on the town hall steps. He had his phone on a little motorized handle that swiveled to keep his face in the middle of the screen, a black beanie, and the SPECTRAL KEV hoodie under an unzipped puffer jacket, and he was talking to the phone in a hushed, thrilled voice.

"—so the State Police have *released the scene*, guys, which means technically, *technically*, it's just a building again, and I'm going to see if anybody will let me—" He saw Archie coming up the sidewalk. His face went through delight, terror and delight again. "Oh my God. Guys. Guys, it's—"

Archie put one finger over the lens.

"Hi, Kev."

"I'm live," whispered Kev.

"Not anymore."

Kev looked at the phone, and at Archie's finger, and turned it off. Up close he hadn't slept since Saturday. There were gray smudges under his eyes, and he smelled of energy drinks.

"I want to go in," he said. "I've wanted to go in since — I mean, it's the Great Fairweather's *theater*. It's the site. Do you know how many people want to see that room right now? Six hundred and four thousand." He said the number the way other people said the name of a saint. "On Friday I had eleven thousand. I got a check, Mr. Fairweather. From the actual platform. Nobody's ever sent me a check. A mattress company wants me to say their name at the top of every video. I don't even like the mattress."

"I have keys," said Archie.

Kev stopped breathing.

"One hour. In the dark, the way it was. Just you and me. No camera."

"Audio?"

"No."

"Thermal? Thermal's not technically a camera, it's more of a—"

"Kev."

"Okay. Okay." Kev was nodding hard. "What do you want?"

Archie had thought about how to put it all the way up from the wharf, and had decided on plainly.

"Whatever you're not telling Sergeant Calloway," he said.

Kev went the color of the clapboards.

---

Marchand Media's black drapes still hung over every tall window in the hall, and with the doors shut at the top of the stairs it was as dark as it had been on Saturday night. Darker; there was no red lamp. By flashlight, Kev unpacked: two EMF meters, a digital recorder, a box he called a spirit box that hissed and gabbled through radio stations, a thermometer on a lanyard, a roll of glow-in-the-dark tape, three flashlights besides the one in his teeth, and a gallon freezer bag of spare batteries. He laid it all out on the stage boards in a careful row.

The round oak table was still there, its seven chairs pushed back at odd angles where people had left them. The props were gone. The State Police had the trumpet and the bell and the slate and the tambourine in bags in Augusta.

Kev switched on the orange EMF meter — the one Archie had signed — and walked toward the stage-left wall, holding it out in front of him like a dowsing rod.

It shrieked. Every light went red.

"Oh my God," Kev breathed.

"That's the new wiring," said Archie. "Eleven thousand dollars of it, from a man in Thomaston. It runs under there to the new dimmer rack. I can show you the invoice."

Kev moved the meter to the radiator. It shrieked again.

"Thermostat relay."

He moved it over the table. It said nothing at all.

"Huh," said Kev, sounding betrayed.

"Sit. Your own chair." Archie pulled out the one across from the head of the table and sat, and switched off his flashlight, and after a moment Kev did the same, and there was nothing.

Then, from somewhere inside the table, three sharp knocks.

Kev made a small sound.

"Toe," said Archie in the dark. "Against the leg. Or a knee up under the top — I think that was hers; she had a very good knee. Again?" Three more. "You can't place it, can you? Sound in the dark doesn't come from anywhere. You let the room tell you where it came from, and the room lies."

"Okay," said Kev, faintly. "Okay, but—"

Something cold and wet and soft touched the back of his neck.

Kev screamed. It was a high, pure, operatic note. It went up into the dark of the pressed-tin ceiling and came back off the balcony, and somewhere in the middle of it Archie distinctly heard the word *Amos*.

He switched on his flashlight and pointed it at himself. He was sitting in his own chair with Kev's selfie handle, fully extended, in his right hand. Clipped where the phone should have been was Archie's left glove, which he'd dunked in Kev's water bottle.

"You left it on the floor by your chair," he said. "You didn't feel me take it."

Kev looked at the glove for a long time.

"That's the cold touch," he said. "That's — Warren yelped. Your *mom* laughed. That was a *stick*?"

"A rod. Hers was nicer — black, telescoping, it'd go up a sweater sleeve." Archie unclipped the glove. "Now the static. Give me your phone."

"I don't — I'm not—"

"The phone, Kev."

Kev handed it over. Archie held it up, filming the dark, so that Kev could see the screen. Then he moved his thumb, and the screen went black.

"Crane Static," he said. "In the dark you can't see a hand press a button in a pocket. All you see is the picture go."

Kev didn't say anything.

"Apports," said Archie. He put the phone down and held his empty right hand up into the beam of the flashlight, turned it front and back, closed it slowly on nothing, and opened it on a playing card — the four of spades, standing up between his first two fingers. "Mediums did it with roses. Sometimes a live pigeon, if the room was big enough. Very good with widows."

"Do it again," said Kev, and then, "No. Don't."

Archie took the glow tape and a rolled-up program from the box of old ones in the wing, and wound three bands of tape around the paper cone where Kev could watch him do it. Then he switched off the light. The three pale green rings hung in the black. They rose. They tilted, and drifted, and swam up over the table, up and to the left, exactly the way they had on Saturday night, and stopped high over Kev's head and turned toward him.

"*Kevin*," said the paper horn, in a slow, deep, faintly Welsh voice.

"Please stop," said Kev.

Archie stopped. He brought the program down and switched the flashlight back on. Kev was sitting in his chair with both hands over his face.

"I did forty minutes on the Crane Static," he said through his fingers. "Forty minutes. With graphics. A guy in Brazil did subtitles. I told six hundred thousand people Brother Amos was in the room with me." He took his hands away. "I *felt* him."

"You felt my glove."

"Before that. Saturday. I felt *something*." He looked at the empty table. "Didn't I?"

Archie let that one go by. He pulled his chair around to face him.

"Kev. Who's paying you?"

"Nobody! The platform! The mattress!"

"And the prop room?"

Kev's mouth opened and nothing came out of it.

It took him a while to get going, and then it came out all at once, the way water comes out of a hose somebody's been standing on. Friday night. Half past nine. Marchand's crew still up here rigging the drapes, so the street door was propped open; he'd taken a coil of cable off the van and carried it up the stairs, "because nobody stops a guy carrying cable, that's like a rule." The prop-room door wasn't locked. He'd just turned the handle. "I swear to God. I didn't pick anything. I don't know how to pick anything." The trumpet had been on a middle shelf in an open case lined with red velvet, with the bell and the slate.

"I just wanted to film it. Ninety seconds. I put on gloves first! Nitrile! I'm not an *animal*. I held it up to the light like you did, I looked down it." His voice dropped. "And I took one selfie. With it. Not touching my face. Near my face."

"And after Saturday?"

"I deleted the selfie." Kev swallowed. "Then I deleted it out of Recently Deleted. Then I googled *can the police recover deleted photos*. Then I deleted my search history. Then I googled *can the police recover search history*." He looked up, stricken. "Then I deleted that."

Archie didn't laugh. It cost him something.

"Kev," he said. "At 7:35 on Saturday night I put my mouth on that horn and blew a raspberry through it in front of two cameras and the entire crew of *The Other Side*. Whatever you did to it on Friday, I'm sitting here."

Kev looked at him. Several things happened in his face, one after another.

"Oh," he said. "Oh. You're the — you're like the control sample."

"I'm exactly the control sample."

Kev let out a breath and put his head down on his knees for a moment. When he came up, he looked five years younger and about as tired.

"I just wanted to see it," he said. "It's *the* horn. It's been on all eight seasons. Before that she used it in the church halls — there are photos from a Grange in Vermont in '98 with the same dent in the bell. Except for the Trumpet Hiatus."

"The what?"

"Oh-three to oh-six. Sister Lori stops using the horn in private sittings. Three years, no horn, nothing. Then season one, it's back like nothing happened." Kev waved a hand. "There's a forty-page thread. Most people think Brother Amos told her to rest it. One guy in Ohio thinks it was her rotator cuff."

"There's a *thread*."

"There are *eras*, Mr. Fairweather. The Church Hall Era. The Hiatus. The Cable Years."

"Do I have eras?"

Kev looked at him with pity. "Pre-Fenway and Post-Fenway," he said. "Obviously."

Archie decided he could live with that.

"Is it true about the trapdoor?" Kev asked. "Gus's? That's in, like, every article."

It was a few feet downstage of the table: a square of boards cut into the floor with a flush iron ring at one edge, which Archie and Lenny had spent a Saturday in September un-sticking. Archie should have said no. He knew it as he crouched and got two fingers into the ring. He pulled the trap up and laid it back on the boards, and cold air came up out of the black square smelling of earth and mice and a hundred years. A short iron ladder went down.

He shrugged out of his coat — it would be filthy down there — and dropped it over the back of Lorelei's chair, and went down first, in his velvet jacket, and Kev came after him with two flashlights and the thermometer.

The crawlspace ran the whole length of the stage, three and a half feet high, on brick piers, with a packed-dirt floor and the underside of the stage boards close overhead. Old programs and a rusted paint can and a single child's shoe lay in the dust. Far off toward the back of the building the beams made a narrowing tunnel.

"It's eleven degrees colder down here," Kev reported.

"It's a cellar, Kev."

They sat on the dirt with their backs against a pier, knees up. Kev turned off one flashlight to save the batteries, which seemed to Archie the most reasonable thing he'd done all morning. Overhead, a long way up, the old building creaked in the wind off the harbor.

"Just tell me one thing," Kev said.

He said it quietly, to the dirt between his sneakers.

"You've been in more dark rooms than anybody alive. You've sat with everybody. There must have been one time. One. Something you couldn't explain. You don't have to tell me what. Just tell me there was one."

Archie looked at the beam of the flashlight lying across the dust.

He thought of a church basement in Somerville when he was nineteen. A woman in a hot tub in Reno. Four hundred rooms, give or take, and the same hands in all of them, and him in every one with his eyes wide open, wanting.

"I've been looking since I was seven years old," he said. "I've never found one, Kev. Not one."

"Then why do you—"

"I haven't stopped looking."

Kev didn't say anything. After a while he nodded.

A shaft of white light came down through the open trap and swept the crawlspace and found them, two grown men sitting in the dirt under a stage with their knees up around their ears.

"The clerk downstairs," said Tess Calloway's voice from above, "says there's been somebody screaming up here for twenty minutes."

She was crouched at the edge of the trap, one hand on the floorboards and the flashlight in the other. She took them both in: the dirt, the thermometer, Archie's wet glove sticking out of his jacket pocket.

"Mr. Duchesne," she said. "Mr. Fairweather."

Kev looked up into the light. Then he looked at Archie. Then he got his phone out of the puffer jacket with a hand that wasn't steady and scrambled on his knees to the bottom of the ladder.

"I have to show you something," he said. "Both of you. I should have showed you Sunday. I'm sorry. I'm really sorry."

Tess came down two rungs so that she could see. Archie crowded in beside Kev.

"I had another camera," Kev said. "In my hoodie pocket. It's tiny — it's infrared, it was thirty-nine dollars — I cut a hole for the lens. It said in the contract no devices, so I didn't — and then she — and Mr. Marchand's lawyers—" He gave up and pressed play. "I started it when she said hands, everybody. It never got the static. Not once. It just kept going."

The picture was gray-green and grainy, shot low, from across the table: the edge of the tabletop, a candle stub, and lying on its side in the middle, the trumpet, its three painted bands glowing hard white in the infrared. The clock in the corner said 19:59. Then the faint wash of the house lights went, and the picture didn't change. Tinny little voices came out of the phone — the prayer, Brother Amos, three raps. Kev dragged his thumb along the bottom of the screen. 20:06. 20:10. A peal of laughter that was Vivienne's. The horn lay where it was. Nobody touched it. Nothing moved near it at all.

At 20:17:04 a hand came into the top of the frame.

It was a woman's hand, and there were rings on three of its fingers, big stones that blazed pale in the infrared. It closed around the narrow end of the trumpet and lifted it out of the picture, and the bands went up and away to the left, and were gone.

Nobody said anything. Down in the dirt, very small, the phone said, *Shan.*

Kev stopped it.

"How long," said Tess, "have you had this?"

"Since Saturday."

"On Sunday you sat across a table from me and said you hadn't recorded anything."

"I said I hadn't recorded anything on my *phone*. Which was technically—" Kev saw her face and closed his mouth.

Tess looked at the frozen screen for a long moment. Then she climbed back up the two rungs and stood at the edge of the trap with the light behind her.

"Upstairs," she said. "Both of you. Now."

Kev climbed. Halfway up the ladder he turned and looked down at Archie with his whole round, exhausted face.

"They're going to arrest me," he whispered.

"You?" Archie put a hand on the bottom rung. "You're getting a thank-you, Kev. The arrest is for somebody else."
