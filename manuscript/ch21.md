# Chapter 21

## Spectral Kev

By ten o'clock the fog had burned off, and Main Street was all hard bright light and long blue shadows. Officer Bissonnette had taken the yellow tape off the town hall's side door at nine, and Archie knew this because Kev Duchesne had narrated the whole thing, live, to six hundred thousand people.

Kev was on the town hall steps in a black beanie, with the SPECTRAL KEV hoodie under an unzipped puffer jacket and his phone on a little motorized handle that swiveled to keep his face in the middle of the screen.

"—so the State Police have *released the scene*, guys, which means technically, *technically*, it's just a building again, and I'm going to see if anybody will let me—" He saw Archie coming up the sidewalk. His face went through delight, terror and delight again. "Oh my God. Guys. Guys, it's—"

Archie put one finger over the lens.

"Hi, Kev."

"I'm live," whispered Kev.

"Not anymore."

Kev turned it off. Up close he hadn't slept since Saturday. He smelled of energy drinks.

"I want to go in," he said. "It's the Great Fairweather's *theater*. It's the site. Do you know how many people want to see that room right now? Six hundred and four thousand." He said the number like the name of a saint. "On Friday I had eleven thousand. I got a check, Mr. Fairweather. Nobody's ever sent me a check. A mattress company wants me to say their name at the top of every video. I don't even like the mattress."

"I have keys," said Archie.

Kev stared at him.

"One hour. In the dark, the way it was. Just you and me. No camera."

"Audio?"

"No."

"Okay. Okay." Kev was nodding hard. "What do you want?"

"Whatever you're not telling Sergeant Calloway."

Kev swallowed.

---

Marchand Media's black drapes still hung over every tall window in the hall, and with the doors shut it was as dark as it had been on Saturday night. Darker; there was no red lamp. By flashlight, Kev unpacked on the stage boards: two EMF meters, a spirit box, a thermometer on a lanyard, a roll of glow-in-the-dark tape and a gallon freezer bag of batteries. The round oak table was still there, its seven chairs pushed back where people had left them. The props were in evidence bags in Augusta.

Kev switched on the orange EMF meter — the one Archie had signed — and walked toward the stage-left wall, holding it out in front of him like a dowsing rod.

It shrieked. Every light went red.

"Oh my God," Kev breathed.

"That's the new wiring," said Archie. "Eleven thousand dollars of it, from a man in Thomaston. I can show you the invoice."

Kev moved the meter over the table. It said nothing.

"Huh," said Kev, sounding betrayed.

"Sit. Your own chair. And lend me that." Archie nodded at the selfie handle, and Kev handed it over without a thought. Archie pulled out the chair across from the head of the table and sat, and switched off his flashlight, and after a moment Kev did the same, and there was nothing.

Then, from somewhere inside the table, three sharp knocks.

Kev made a small sound.

"Toe," said Archie in the dark. "Against the table leg. Again?" Three more. "You can't place it, can you? In the dark the room tells you where a sound came from, and the room lies."

"Okay," said Kev, faintly. "Okay, but—"

Something cold and wet and soft touched the back of his neck.

Kev screamed. It was a high, pure, operatic note. It went up into the dark of the pressed-tin ceiling and came back off the balcony, and somewhere in the middle of it Archie distinctly heard the word *Amos*.

He switched on his flashlight and pointed it at himself. He was holding Kev's selfie handle, fully extended, and clipped where the phone should have been was Archie's own glove, dunked in Kev's water bottle.

Kev looked at the glove for a long time.

"That's the cold touch," he said. "That's — Warren yelped. Your *mom* laughed. That was a *stick*?"

"A rod. Hers was nicer — black, telescoping, it'd go up a sweater sleeve." Archie unclipped the glove.

He took the glow tape and a rolled-up program from the box of old ones in the wing, and wound three bands of tape around the paper cone where Kev could watch him do it. Then he switched off the light. The three pale green rings hung in the black. They rose. They tilted, and drifted, and swam up over the table, up and to the left, as they had on Saturday night, and stopped high over Kev's head and turned toward him.

"*Kevin*," said the paper horn, in a slow, deep, faintly Welsh voice.

"Please stop," said Kev.

Archie stopped. He brought the program down and switched the flashlight back on. Kev was sitting in his chair with both hands over his face.

"I did forty minutes on the Crane Static," he said through his fingers. "Forty minutes. With graphics. A guy in Brazil did subtitles. I told six hundred thousand people Brother Amos was in the room with me." He took his hands away. "I *felt* him."

"You felt my glove."

"Before that. Saturday. I felt *something*." He looked at the empty table. "Didn't I?"

Archie let that one go by.

"Kev. Who's paying you?"

"Nobody! The platform! The mattress!"

"And the prop room?"

Kev's mouth opened and nothing came out of it.

Then it came out all at once. Friday night, half past nine, Marchand's crew still up here rigging the drapes and the street door propped open. He'd carried a coil of cable off the van and up the stairs, "because nobody stops a guy carrying cable, that's like a rule." The prop-room door wasn't locked. He'd just turned the handle. "I swear. I don't even know how to pick anything." The trumpet had been on a middle shelf in an open case lined with red velvet.

"I just wanted to film it. Ninety seconds. I put on gloves first! Nitrile! I'm not an *animal*. I held it up to the light like you did, I looked down it." His voice dropped. "And I took one selfie. With it. Not touching my face. Near my face."

"And after Saturday?"

"I deleted the selfie." Kev swallowed. "Then I deleted it out of Recently Deleted. Then I googled *can the police recover deleted photos*. Then I deleted my search history. Then I googled *can the police recover search history*." He looked up, stricken. "Then I deleted that."

Archie didn't laugh. It cost him something.

"Kev. At 7:35 on Saturday night I blew a raspberry through that horn on two cameras. Whatever you did to it on Friday, I'm sitting here."

"Oh," said Kev. "Oh. You're the — you're like the control sample."

"I'm exactly the control sample."

Kev put his head down on his knees for a moment. When he came up he looked five years younger.

"I just wanted to see it," he said. "It's *the* horn. All eight seasons, and the church halls before that — there are photos from a Grange in Vermont with the same dent. Except for the Trumpet Hiatus."

"The what?"

"Oh-three or oh-four, depending who you ask, to oh-six. Sister Lori stops using the horn in private sittings. Then it's back like nothing happened." Kev waved a hand. "There's a forty-page thread. Half of it's people fighting about when it starts. Most people think Brother Amos told her to rest it. One guy in Ohio thinks it was her rotator cuff."

"There's a *thread*."

"There are *eras*, Mr. Fairweather. The Church Hall Era. The Hiatus. The Cable Years."

"Do I have eras?"

Kev looked at him with pity. "Pre-Fenway and Post-Fenway," he said. "Obviously."

Archie decided he could live with that.

"Is it true about Gus's trapdoor?" Kev asked.

It was a few feet downstage of the table, two leaves cut into the boards with a flush iron ring at one edge. Archie should have said no. He knew it as he got two fingers into the ring and pulled, and cold air came up out of the black opening smelling of earth and mice and a hundred years.

He shrugged out of his coat — it would be filthy down there — dropped it over the back of Lorelei's chair, and went down the iron ladder first in his velvet jacket. The crawlspace ran the length of the stage, three and a half feet high, on brick piers, with a packed-dirt floor. Old programs and a rusted paint can lay in the dust.

"It's eleven degrees colder down here," Kev reported.

"It's a cellar, Kev."

They sat with their backs against a pier, knees up. Overhead the old building creaked in the wind off the harbor.

"Just tell me one thing," Kev said, quietly, to the dirt between his sneakers. "You've been in more dark rooms than anybody alive. I had your Challenge poster over my bed all through high school. *Nobody fools Archie Fairweather.* There must have been one time. One. Something you couldn't explain. You don't have to tell me what. Just tell me there was one."

Archie looked at the beam of the flashlight lying across the dust.

He thought of a church basement in Somerville when he was nineteen. A woman in a hot tub in Reno. Four hundred rooms, give or take, and the same hands in all of them, and him in every one with his eyes wide open, wanting.

"I've been looking since I was seven years old," he said. "I've never found one, Kev. Not one."

"Then why do you—"

"I haven't stopped looking."

Kev didn't say anything. After a while he nodded.

A shaft of white light came down through the open trap and swept the crawlspace and found them, two grown men sitting in the dirt under a stage with their knees up around their ears.

"The clerk downstairs," said Tess Calloway's voice from above, "says there's been somebody screaming up here for twenty minutes."

She was crouched at the edge of the trap. She took them both in: the dirt, the thermometer, the wet glove sticking out of Archie's pocket.

"Mr. Duchesne," she said. "Mr. Fairweather."

Kev looked up into the light, and then at Archie. Then he got his phone out with a hand that wasn't steady and scrambled on his knees to the foot of the ladder.

"I have to show you something," he said. "Both of you. I should have on Sunday. I'm sorry."

Tess came down two rungs. Archie crowded in beside Kev.

"I had another camera," Kev said. "In my hoodie pocket. Infrared, thirty-nine dollars, I cut a hole for the lens. The contract said no devices, and then she — and the lawyers—" He gave up and pressed play. "I started it when she said hands, everybody. It never got the static. Not once."

The picture was gray-green and grainy, shot low across the table: the edge of the tabletop, a candle stub, and lying on its side in the middle, the trumpet, its three bands glowing hard white in the infrared. The clock in the corner said 19:59. The house lights went, and the picture didn't change. Tinny voices came out of the phone — the prayer, Brother Amos, three raps. Kev dragged his thumb along the bottom of the screen. 20:06. 20:10. A peal of laughter that was Vivienne's. The horn lay where it was. Nothing moved near it.

At 20:17:04 a hand came into the top of the frame.

It was a woman's hand, and there were rings on three of its fingers, big stones that blazed pale in the infrared. It closed around the narrow end of the trumpet and lifted it out of the picture, and the bands went up and away to the left, and were gone.

Nobody said anything. Down in the dirt, very small, the phone said, *Shan.*

Kev stopped it.

"How long," said Tess, "have you had this?"

"Since Saturday."

"On Sunday you sat across a table from me and said you hadn't recorded anything."

"I said I hadn't recorded anything on my *phone*. Which was technically—" Kev saw her face and closed his mouth.

Tess looked at the frozen screen a while longer. Then she climbed back up.

"Upstairs," she said. "Both of you. Now."

Kev climbed. Halfway up he turned and looked down at Archie.

"They're going to arrest me," he whispered.

"You?" Archie put a hand on the bottom rung. "You're getting a thank-you, Kev. The arrest is for somebody else."
