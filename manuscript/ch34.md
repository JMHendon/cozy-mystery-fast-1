# Chapter 34

## The Trumpet Woman

Her father had kept everything on paper.

Tess Calloway had known that her whole life. When she was nine and her mother left, Walt Calloway had come home from the station that night and sat at the kitchen table and written it down — not in a diary, he didn't keep a diary, but on a yellow legal pad, in his square block capitals, the way he wrote up a fender-bender: DATE. TIME. WHAT WAS SAID. Then he'd torn off the sheet and folded it in thirds and put it in the drawer by the stove with the takeout menus and the warranty for the refrigerator, and as far as Tess knew it was still there, in the house on Pound Hill that she was renovating one weekend at a time, badly.

He'd run the Merrow Police Department the same way for twenty-nine years. Every call, every complaint, every lost dog and every drunk and every summer kid who'd taken a lobster boat for a joyride had gone onto paper, and the paper had gone into the green steel filing cabinets in the basement of the town hall, one drawer per year, filed alphabetically by the name of whoever had walked in the door to complain.

Not by the name of whoever they'd complained about. By complainant. That was the thing nobody at the State Police had known, and the thing it had taken her until three o'clock Thursday afternoon to think of.

"Two thousand three," said Dale Pinkham, and hauled the drawer open with a shriek of rusted runners.

The basement smelled of damp stone and old paper and the oil furnace that roared on the other side of the wall. A single bulb hung over the cabinets. Dale had brought down two folding chairs from the select board room and a box of doughnuts from the Galley, and he'd eaten two of the doughnuts on the stairs.

"You want to tell me what we're looking for?"

"I'll know it when I see it."

"Ayuh. That's what your dad used to say." He handed her the first fat handful of manila folders. "He'd have been at this an hour ago."

She'd been uneasy since Wednesday. Uneasy wasn't evidence, and she didn't take uneasy to her lieutenant, but she couldn't make it go away. She'd watched the footage of the controls segment forty times — the magician with the trumpet at his mouth, his cheeks puffing, that obscene sound — and every time she watched it she believed him, and every time she believed him she told herself that was precisely what he was paid to make people do. She'd read the lab report on the scarf: aconitine and lipstick, together, on a single fold, in a smear consistent with wiping. Consistent with wiping something off. Not putting something on. She had stood in the rain in the town hall parking lot on Wednesday and watched Archie Fairweather's face when Brannock said *your mother*, and it had not been the face of a man protecting himself.

And then on Thursday morning at the jail, in the corridor outside the attorney visiting room, she had passed him coming out with a sheet of paper in his hand, Ivy Crane's signature on it and Margaret Okafor's under that.

Okafor had been happy to tell her what it was. Okafor wanted a third party more than she wanted lunch. *A man in Maine, Sergeant. Two thousand three. My client's mother stopped using the trumpet in private sittings after him and didn't start again for years. Find him, and you'll find someone with a much better reason than my client had.*

A man in Maine. Two thousand three. Tess had driven back to Merrow from Rockland thinking about a drawer in her father's kitchen, and had stopped the car on the side of Route 1 and sat there for a minute with the engine running.

"Abbott," read Dale, from the folder in his hand. "Dog bite. Ayuh, that's the Abbotts' shepherd, that dog bit everybody." He set it on the pile. "Ames. Burglary. Ames — that's the summer place out on the point, they lost a television." Another folder. "Bickford. Noise complaint." He looked up. "Your dad wrote down *noise complaints*?"

"He wrote down everything."

"Ayuh, I can see that."

Dale had come to Merrow in 2010, from the sheriff's office in Rockland, the year her father retired. He knew the town's people now, but he didn't know its paper. He read every name aloud, and said who they were, and was right about most of them, and Tess let him, because it helped to have someone talking.

It was twenty minutes past noon when she found it.

It was a thin folder, near the back of the drawer, with a strip of masking tape on the tab and two words in her father's hand: LANGLEY, H.

She knew before she opened it. She knew the way you know a voice on the phone before it says its name. Hollis Langley. That was what her mother had called her, and the women at church, and the woman at the post office — *Hollis Langley, Peter's wife* — back when Tess was small and Mrs. Tibbetts was Mrs. Tibbetts only at the high school, where she'd taught Tess to diagram a sentence and told her, in the eleventh grade, that she wrote like a person giving testimony and that it wasn't a compliment.

And Danny. Danny Langley, a grade ahead of her at Penobscot Regional, with his mother's laugh and his father's ears, who had played Puck in the Merrow Players' summer show the year Tess was twelve and had swung down out of the flies on a rope and gotten stuck, and laughed so hard hanging there that the whole audience had laughed with him. Danny Langley, who drowned at the quarry the summer Tess turned fourteen.

She opened the folder.

There were three sheets of yellow legal paper, written on both sides, and a photocopy. Her father's square capitals marched down the pages, pressed so hard into the paper she could feel them with her fingertips from the back.

*DATE: 11/26/03. COMPLAINANT: HOLLIS LANGLEY, 14 PUDDING LANE. RE: HUSBAND PETER LANGLEY (DECEASED 11/18/03, LOST AT SEA, SHAG LEDGE).*

*COMPLAINANT STATES SHE HAS FOUND HUSBAND'S NOTEBOOK AND RECEIPTS SHOWING HE ATTENDED 41 PRIVATE SESSIONS 10/01–11/03 WITH A "SPIRITUALIST MEDIUM" CALLING HERSELF SISTER LORI CRANMER, PARK ST., ROCKLAND (OVER THE LAUNDROMAT). TOTAL PAID $61,500. SECOND MORTGAGE TAKEN ON HOUSE W/O COMPLAINANT'S KNOWLEDGE.*

*NOTEBOOK RECORDS "MESSAGES" FROM SON DANIEL (DECEASED 8/01) GIVEN THROUGH A "TRUMPET." MESSAGES INCLUDE: "DAD IT'S WARM HERE," "THE WATER'S WARM," "I'M NOT COLD ANYMORE," AND IN FINAL ENTRIES "COME SEE ME ON THE WATER."*

*COMPLAINANT BELIEVES THESE MESSAGES CAUSED HUSBAND TO TAKE BOAT OUT IN GALE.*

There was more. A call to the Rockland police: the room over the laundromat already empty, the landlord paid through December in cash. A call to an assistant attorney general in Augusta, whose name her father had written down and underlined twice. And at the bottom of the last page, in the same capitals, pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through:

*NO STATUTE APPLIES PER AAG. SHE NEVER TOLD HIM TO GO OUT IN A STORM. NOT IN SO MANY WORDS. TOLD H.L. I AM SORRY. NOT FILED W/ STATE.*

The photocopy was of a flyer. It had been pinned to a corkboard once — there were holes in the corners — and copied badly, so the gray had gone to black in places. MESSAGES FROM LOVED ONES, it said across the top, in a font that was trying to look like handwriting. SISTER LORI CRANMER, SPIRITUALIST. PRIVATE SITTINGS. Underneath was a photograph of a woman in her thirties with dark hair and a dark mouth and steady, amused eyes.

There was no white streak yet. It didn't matter. Tess had looked at that face on the autopsy table on Sunday afternoon.

"Tess?" said Dale. He'd stopped reading names. "You all right?"

"Who's Hollis Langley," she said. It came out wrong — not a question.

"Couldn't tell you. Before my time."

"It's Hollis Tibbetts, Dale."

He looked at her. She watched him work it out, the way you'd watch a man realize the ice under his boots was thinner than he'd thought. His big face went slowly slack under the walrus mustache, and he set the folder in his hands down on the pile very carefully, as if it might break.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, no. Oh, Hollis."

She needed one more thing, and she needed it before she called Augusta, because Brannock was going to ask her how she knew the woman in a twenty-two-year-old photocopy was the woman in the morgue, and *I looked at her* was not an answer a lieutenant accepted.

Sea View was four minutes up the hill.

Marisol was at the nurses' station, and smiled when she saw her. "You just missed him," she said. "The magician. He was here at noon, same as always."

"Was he." Tess kept her voice even.

"He did a trick for Carol." Marisol nodded toward the common room. "She hasn't let go of it."

Carol Gilley was sitting in her armchair by the window in a cardigan that was, for once, buttoned right, holding a single playing card in both hands. It was the queen of hearts. Across the face of it, in black marker, in someone else's handwriting, was the word WARREN. Carol was looking at it and smiling, and every so often she turned it over to check the back, and then turned it face up again, as if to make sure the name was still there.

Tess stood in the doorway and looked at that for longer than she meant to.

Her father was by the piano in his wheelchair, humming. He was in a good way today; she could see it from across the room — the clear blue eyes, the big hands quiet on the arms of the chair. He smiled when he saw her. "Tessie."

"Hi, Dad." She pulled the footstool over and sat down by him and took the photocopy out of her coat, folded so only the photograph showed. "Dad, I need you to look at something for me. Do you know this woman?"

He took it. His hands shook and the paper shook with them. He held it a long way from his face, the way he did now, and looked at it for so long that she started to think he was gone again, somewhere in 1988, and wouldn't be coming back today.

"That's the trumpet woman," said Walt Calloway.

Tess didn't move.

"Hollis's woman." He tapped the photograph with one big finger. "Hollis came to see me about her. Sat in my office and cried. Hollis Langley never cried in her life, Tessie, not even at the boy's funeral. I couldn't do a thing." His face clouded. "Nothing to charge. I told her I was sorry. I told her—"

"I know, Dad. You wrote it down."

"I wrote it down." He nodded slowly, satisfied, and handed the paper back to her. Then he looked at her — properly, the lighthouse look — and smiled, and she saw him leave 2003 and go somewhere else, somewhere softer.

"You made me mail that letter," he said.

"What letter?"

"To the magic fella. On the TV. You were twelve. You wrote him how he did the lighthouse trick and you made me put two stamps on it, so it'd get there faster." He chuckled, deep in his chest. "Two stamps. I told you that's not how stamps work."

"Dad."

"He wrote you back, too. You remember? You kept it in your—"

"Dad," said Tess. "I remember."

She kissed the top of his head, and stood, and went out onto the long front porch of Sea View, where the rocking chairs sat empty in the cold and the whole bay lay spread out below in the hard bright afternoon light. She stood at the rail for a moment until her face was her own again. Then she called her lieutenant.

Brannock listened to all of it without interrupting, which was how she knew he was taking it seriously.

"A twenty-two-year-old complaint," he said when she'd finished, "a flyer, and an Alzheimer's patient."

"And the forty seconds, Lieutenant. She slid it. At the producer's request, in gloves, in the one gap in the footage. We've had her on our own list since Sunday. We just didn't have a reason."

There was a pause on the line. She could hear him breathing through his nose.

"Write me the affidavit," he said. "House and outbuildings. I'll walk it to the judge myself. You don't have probable cause to arrest her on this and you know it, so nobody goes near her until we've been through that shed. Where will she be tonight?"

"The opera house. She's running the lights for the show."

"Of course she is." Another pause. "Bissonnette in plain clothes in the hall from five. A car on Main Street. Quietly, Sergeant. If the search gives us anything, we take her at intermission, out of sight of three hundred people and a television crew. Not before." Then, in a different voice: "And Calloway. Your magician."

She didn't say anything.

"He's been to see the daughter. He had her sign something. Does he know?"

"I don't know what he knows."

"Find out," said Brannock, and hung up.

She drove down the hill at a quarter past three with the affidavit already half-written in her head. At the bottom she could have gone left, on Elm, straight to the town hall. She went right instead, onto Pudding Lane, without quite deciding to.

It was a short street of small old houses with picket fences. Halfway down it, in front of a weathered Cape with a blue door and a big front border cut down to the ground for the winter, a long black 1958 Cadillac hearse was parked at the curb, with THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER — MARVELS & MYSTERIES in gold letters on its side.

Tess didn't slow down. She didn't look at the house. She drove to the end of Pudding Lane and turned onto Water Street and pulled over by the inn and sat there with both hands on the wheel.

He knew.

Of course he knew. He'd had Ivy Crane's release in his hand on Thursday morning, and Archie Fairweather had never in his life been able to leave a locked thing locked. He knew, and he'd gone to her, alone, because that was what he was — a man who couldn't walk past a closed door without trying the handle, and couldn't walk past a friend in trouble without trying to save her, and didn't seem to understand that sometimes those were the same mistake.

He hadn't called.

She sat there for a long time with the engine running and the heater on. Out on the harbor the afternoon light was going long and gold, and the bell buoy off Shag Ledge was ringing, faint and irregular, the way it did when there was a swell coming in.

At twenty minutes to four, her phone buzzed on the seat beside her.

It was a text, from a number she'd never saved and knew anyway. Two words.

*Intermission. Please.*
