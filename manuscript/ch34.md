# Chapter 34

## The Trumpet Woman

Her father had kept everything on paper. The night her mother left, when Tess was nine, Walt Calloway had come home from the station and sat at the kitchen table and written it up on a yellow legal pad in his square block capitals — DATE. TIME. WHAT WAS SAID. — and folded it in thirds and put it in the drawer by the stove with the takeout menus. He had run the Merrow Police Department the same way for twenty-nine years, and every call and complaint had gone into the green steel cabinets in the basement of the town hall, one drawer per year, filed by the name of whoever had walked in the door to complain.

Not by the name of whoever they'd complained about. By complainant. Nobody at the State Police knew that. It had taken Tess until Thursday afternoon, driving back from the jail, to remember it herself, and Dale until Friday morning to find the key to the basement.

"Two thousand three," said Dale Pinkham at eleven o'clock on Friday morning, and hauled the drawer open with a shriek of rusted runners.

The basement smelled of damp stone and old paper and the oil furnace roaring on the other side of the wall. A single bulb hung over the cabinets. Dale had brought down two folding chairs from the select board room and a box of doughnuts from the Galley, and he'd eaten two of the doughnuts on the stairs.

"You want to tell me what we're looking for?"

"I'll know it when I see it."

"Ayuh. That's what your dad used to say." He handed her the first fat handful of manila folders. "He'd have been at this an hour ago."

She had believed the magician since Wednesday. She'd told him so on the phone that night, against orders, in a call she would never log. Believing him wasn't evidence. What she had was the lab report on the scarf — aconitine and lipstick together on a single fold, in a smear consistent with wiping something off, not putting something on — and what Margaret Okafor had told her on Thursday in the jail corridor, after Tess had passed Archie coming out with Ivy Crane's signature in his hand. Okafor wanted a third party more than she wanted lunch. *A man in Maine, Sergeant. Two thousand three. My client's mother put the trumpet away after him for years. Find him.*

A man in Maine. Two thousand three. Tess had pulled over on Route 1 with the engine running and thought about a drawer by a stove.

"Abbott," read Dale. "Dog bite. Ayuh, that's the Abbotts' shepherd, that dog bit everybody." He set it on the pile. "Ames. Burglary. Summer place out on the point, they lost a television." Another folder. "Bickford. Noise complaint." He looked up. "Your dad wrote down *noise complaints*?"

"He wrote down everything."

Dale had been a Knox County deputy for more than twenty years before he came to Merrow in 2010, the year her father retired. He knew the town's people now, but not its paper. He read every name aloud and said who they were, and was right about most of them, and Tess let him, because it helped to have someone talking.

At twenty past noon she found it.

It was a thin folder near the back of the drawer, with a strip of masking tape on the tab and two words in her father's hand: LANGLEY, H.

She sat with it closed on her knee. *Hollis Langley, Peter's wife.* That was what her mother had called her, and the women at church, back when Tess was small and Mrs. Tibbetts was Mrs. Tibbetts only at the high school, where she'd taught Tess to diagram a sentence and told her, in the eleventh grade, that she wrote like a person giving testimony and that it wasn't a compliment. And there had been a boy. Danny Langley, a grade ahead of her, who had played Puck in the Players' summer show the year Tess was twelve, and who drowned at the quarry the summer she turned fourteen.

She opened the folder.

Three sheets of yellow legal paper, written on both sides, and a photocopy. Her father's capitals were pressed so hard into the paper she could feel them from the back.

*DATE: 11/26/03. COMPLAINANT: HOLLIS LANGLEY, 14 PUDDING LANE. RE: HUSBAND PETER LANGLEY (DECEASED 11/18/03, LOST AT SEA, SHAG LEDGE).*

*COMPLAINANT STATES SHE HAS FOUND HUSBAND'S NOTEBOOK AND RECEIPTS SHOWING HE ATTENDED 41 PRIVATE SESSIONS 10/01–11/03 WITH A "SPIRITUALIST MEDIUM" CALLING HERSELF SISTER LORI CRANMER, PARK ST., ROCKLAND. TOTAL PAID $61,500. SECOND MORTGAGE TAKEN W/O COMPLAINANT'S KNOWLEDGE.*

*NOTEBOOK RECORDS "MESSAGES" FROM SON DANIEL (DECEASED 8/01) GIVEN THROUGH A "TRUMPET." INCLUDING: "DAD IT'S WARM HERE," "I'M NOT COLD ANYMORE," AND IN FINAL ENTRIES "COME SEE ME ON THE WATER."*

There was more. A call to the Rockland police: the room over the laundromat already empty, the landlord paid through December in cash. A call to an assistant attorney general in Augusta, whose name her father had underlined twice. And at the bottom of the last page, pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through:

*NO STATUTE APPLIES PER AAG. SHE NEVER TOLD HIM TO GO OUT IN A STORM. NOT IN SO MANY WORDS. TOLD H.L. I AM SORRY. NOT FILED W/ STATE.*

The photocopy was of a flyer that had been pinned to a corkboard once; there were holes in the corners. MESSAGES FROM LOVED ONES, it said across the top, in a font trying to look like handwriting. SISTER LORI CRANMER, SPIRITUALIST. PRIVATE SITTINGS. Underneath was a woman in her thirties with dark hair and a dark mouth and steady, amused eyes.

There was no white streak yet. It didn't matter. Tess had looked at that face on the autopsy table on Sunday afternoon.

"Tess?" Dale had stopped reading names. "You all right?"

"Peter Langley," she said.

"Langley." He frowned at the furnace wall. "Went onto Shag Ledge in a gale, oh-three. I was on the county then. We got the call when they found him." He shook his head. "Never knew the wife."

"Yes, you do. It's Hollis Tibbetts, Dale."

He looked at her. His big face went slowly slack under the walrus mustache, and he set the folders in his hands down on the pile, very carefully, and didn't pick up another.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, no. Oh, Hollis."

---

She needed one more thing before she called Augusta, because Brannock was going to ask how she knew the woman in a twenty-two-year-old photocopy was the woman in the morgue, and *I looked at her* was not an answer a lieutenant accepted.

Sea View was four minutes up the hill.

"You just missed him," said Marisol at the nurses' station. "The magician. He was here at noon, same as always. He did a trick for Carol. She hasn't let go of it."

Carol Gilley was in her armchair by the window in a cardigan that was, for once, buttoned right, holding a single playing card in both hands. It was the queen of hearts. Across its face, in black marker, in someone else's handwriting, was the word WARREN. Every so often Carol turned it over to check the back, and then turned it face up again, as if to make sure the name was still there.

Tess stood in the doorway and watched that for longer than she meant to.

Her father was by the piano in his wheelchair, humming. He was in a good way today — the clear blue eyes, the big hands quiet on the arms of the chair. "Tessie."

"Hi, Dad." She pulled the footstool over and took the photocopy out of her coat, folded so only the photograph showed. "Do you know this woman?"

He took it. His hands shook and the paper shook with them. He held it a long way from his face and looked at it until she started to think he'd gone back to 1988 and wouldn't be coming out today.

"That's the trumpet woman," said Walt Calloway.

Tess didn't move.

"Hollis's woman." He tapped the photograph with one big finger. "Hollis came to see me about her. Sat in my office and cried. Hollis Langley never cried in her life, Tessie, not even at the boy's funeral. I couldn't do a thing." His face clouded. "Nothing to charge. I told her I was sorry. I told her—"

"I know, Dad. You wrote it down."

"I wrote it down." He nodded, satisfied, and handed the paper back. Then he looked at her properly, and smiled, and she saw him leave 2003 for somewhere softer.

"You made me mail that letter," he said.

"What letter?"

"To the magic fella. On the TV. You were twelve. You wrote him how he did the lighthouse trick and you made me put two stamps on it, so it'd get there faster." He laughed, deep in his chest. "Two stamps. I told you that's not how stamps work."

"Dad."

"He wrote you back, too. You remember? You kept it in your—"

"I remember," said Tess.

She did. A glossy eight-by-ten of a skinny young man in a tuxedo, signed across the bottom, and on the back, in black felt-tip, in a hand that leaned forward as if it were in a hurry: *You're right. Don't tell anyone. — A.F.* It was in a shoebox on the top shelf of her closet on Pound Hill, under her diploma. She had never told anyone.

She kissed the top of his head and went out onto the long front porch, where the rocking chairs sat empty in the cold and the whole bay lay below in the hard afternoon light. She stood at the rail until her face was her own again. Then she called her lieutenant.

Brannock listened to all of it without interrupting, which was how she knew he was taking it seriously.

"A twenty-two-year-old complaint," he said, "a flyer, and an Alzheimer's patient."

"And the forty seconds. She slid it, at the producer's request, in gloves, in the one gap in the footage. She's been on our own list since Sunday. We just didn't have a reason."

She could hear him breathing through his nose.

"Write me the affidavit," he said. "House and outbuildings. I'll walk it to the judge myself. You don't have probable cause to arrest her on this and you know it, so nobody goes near her until we've been through that house. Where will she be tonight?"

"The opera house. She's running the lights for the show."

"Of course she is." A pause. "Bissonnette in plain clothes in the hall from five. A car on Main Street. If the search gives us anything, we take her at intermission, quietly, out of sight of three hundred people. Not before." Then, in a different voice: "And Calloway. Your magician. He had the daughter sign something. Does he know?"

"I don't know what he knows."

"Find out," said Brannock, and hung up.

She drove down the hill at a quarter past three with the affidavit already half-written in her head. At the bottom, Elm Street went left to the town hall. She went right, onto Pudding Lane.

It was a short street of small old houses with picket fences. Halfway down, in front of a weathered Cape with a blue door, a long black 1958 Cadillac hearse was parked at the curb with THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER — MARVELS & MYSTERIES in gold letters on its side.

Tess didn't slow down. She didn't look at the house. She drove to the end of Pudding Lane and turned onto Water Street and pulled over by the inn and sat there with both hands on the wheel.

He knew. He'd had Ivy Crane's release in his hand on Thursday morning, and he knew, and he'd gone to her alone.

He hadn't called.

She sat with the engine running and the heater on and the affidavit unwritten, watching the gulls work the pier, until twenty minutes to four, when her phone buzzed on the seat beside her.

It was a text from a number she'd never saved and knew anyway. Two words.

*Intermission. Please.*
