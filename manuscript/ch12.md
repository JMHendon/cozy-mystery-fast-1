# Chapter 12

## The Captain's Suite

Bev took the thermos back without a word.

Archie set it on the Galley counter a little after noon — dented steel, masking tape, BEV — RETURN IT — and she picked it up by the handle, looked at it, and put it in a gray bus tub under the counter with three others just like it. Then she looked at him.

"Chowder," he said. "A quart. For somebody who hasn't eaten since yesterday."

"Clam or fish?"

"Which is better?"

"Clam's Sunday." She ladled it into a white paper tub, pressed the lid down with the heel of her hand, and added two packets of oyster crackers without being asked. "Seven-fifty."

He gave her a twenty. She gave him twelve-fifty back, exactly, and went to pour coffee for the liars' table, who did not look at him. Sixty-one.

The Spindrift Inn was three streets away, and Pru Haskell met him in the front hall with her hands knotted in a dish towel and told him that Ivy Crane was upstairs with the State Police and wasn't to be disturbed.

"Which room?"

"The Captain's Suite. Top of the house. Mr. Fairweather, I really don't think—"

He was already on the stairs. They went up three flights, narrowing as they went, to a single door under the eaves.

Tess Calloway opened the suite door about eight inches and looked at him through the gap.

"No," she said.

He held up the chowder. "She hasn't eaten."

"You don't know that."

"She hadn't eaten at nine last night, and she hadn't eaten at eleven when I dropped her here, and I've known a lot of people who couldn't eat after a show. Clam. It's Sunday." He kept his voice low. "I'll put it down and go."

Tess looked at him for a moment. Then she looked back over her shoulder into the room. He couldn't see what she saw. Whatever it was, it made up her mind.

She opened the door.

The Captain's Suite took up the whole third floor under the roof — a sitting room with a sloped ceiling and dormer windows over the harbor, a bedroom through an open door, and in one corner a steep ladder-stair up to a hatch, which would be the widow's walk. It was warm, and it smelled of woodsmoke and menthol and something expensive. Lorelei's long velvet coat hung on the back of the bedroom door. On the windowsill a paperback mystery lay face down, open to the page where she'd stopped.

"Sit there," she said, and pointed at a straight-backed chair against the wall by the closet, well out of the way. "Don't talk."

Ivy Crane was in an armchair by the dormer window, in the same gray sweater, with her knees together and her hands in her lap. Archie's buffalo-check coat was folded over the arm of her chair. She looked at the chowder, and at him, and at nothing.

He put the tub on the low table beside her with a plastic spoon and both packets of crackers, and went and sat in the straight chair against the wall, and folded his hands, and was quiet.

Tess sat down again across from Ivy. There was a small digital recorder on the table between them, its red light on, and a notebook open on Tess's knee.

"For the recording," Tess said, "Mr. Fairweather has come in. He isn't part of this interview. Ms. Crane, you're still here voluntarily. You can stop whenever you like."

"I know."

"You were telling me about the work you did for your mother."

"I research the sitters," Ivy said. "Before every show. Everybody does it. Obituaries, local papers, social media, church bulletins, whatever there is. Names, dates, how they died, what they called each other." She said it the way you'd describe how to change a tire. "It's in the binder."

"May I have the binder?"

Ivy got up and went to the closet. It was a deep old closet with a shelf above the rail, and she reached up and took down a fat black three-ring binder from the shelf, next to a round green hatbox, and handed it to Tess without a word. Then she sat back down.

Tess opened it on her knee. From the wall, Archie could see colored tabs. MAYO. GILLEY. DUCHESNE. FAIRWEATHER, V. FAIRWEATHER, A.

There was his name, on a pink tab. Behind it, somewhere, was where *Sparrow* came from.

"Ivy," he said. "Where did you find—"

"Mr. Fairweather," said Tess, without looking up.

"I only want to know where—"

"It's all in there," said Ivy, to the window. "Everything I found is in there."

Tess closed the binder and set it on the floor beside her chair, on the side away from Archie, and put her boot next to it.

"Saturday night," she said. "In the dark. Your mother was seated at the head of the table. You were on her left. Were you holding her hand?"

"Yes."

"The whole time? From when the lights went out until they came back on?"

"She held my hand," said Ivy.

Tess wrote it down. Archie watched Ivy's fingers in her lap. They weren't moving now. She'd stopped doing that sometime in the night.

He had told himself on the stairs that he would sit in the chair and be quiet. He had meant it. But he knew what it was like, the first morning after, when you've got to hold the whole thing up yourself and nobody in the world knows how heavy it is, and he found he had leaned forward with his elbows on his knees.

"Ivy," he said gently. "I saw you wipe the trumpet. When the lights came up. I told the sergeant." He let that sit for a second, so she'd know he wasn't hiding it. "I understand why you did it. I'd have done the same thing. You reset. The prop's where the method lives, and you don't let anybody pick it up till it's clean. It's the first thing anybody teaches you. It doesn't mean anything except that you were raised in the business."

Ivy turned her head and looked at him. Her eyes were pale and very steady.

Then she turned back to Tess.

"I held my mother's hand," she said, clearly, to the notebook. "The whole time. Write that down."

Tess wrote it down.

Archie sat back. He'd thrown her a rope and she'd looked at it and stepped around it, and he could hear in his own head, as clearly as if she'd said it aloud, the sergeant's voice: *I told you not to talk.*

He tried once more anyway, because it was true, and because she should have it.

"She was going to stop, you know," he said. "Your mother. She told me on Friday night, at the fire downstairs. This was her last season. She was going to buy a house somewhere warm and read mysteries. She said she hadn't told anyone."

For a moment nothing happened.

Then Ivy Crane's face did something. It didn't crumple; it went the other way, very still and very white, as if every muscle in it had been pulled tight at once.

"She told *you*," she said.

"Ivy—"

"I asked her for a year." Her voice was flat and low and shaking very slightly at the bottom, the way it had in the parking lot. "I asked her every month for a year. *When are we done. When can I stop.* And she said *when I say*. And she told *you*. At the fire. With a drink." She looked down at her hands. "She never told me."

Archie had nothing. He had a whole career of things to say into a silence, and not one of them was any use. Tess didn't write anything. She sat very still with the pen resting on the page, and let it be quiet.

The door banged open without a knock.

"Ivy. Sweetheart. I'm so sorry, I know, I know, I just need two minutes." Teddy Marchand came in fast, a phone in each hand and mango vapor trailing off his fleece vest. He saw Tess, and Archie, and kept going anyway. "Sergeant, hi, two minutes. Ivy. I've been on with the network since six. They want to air it. The full special, the séance, everything up to the — everything. In memoriam. Tasteful. A card at the end with her dates. They're talking a tribute hour after." He crouched beside her chair, both phones held loosely between his knees. "She'd want it out there, honey. You know she would. She'd want the last word. I just need you to sign off as next of kin."

"No," said Ivy.

"Ivy, it's what she—"

"You don't get to tell me what she wanted." Ivy stood up, so suddenly that Teddy rocked back on his heels. "Did she tell *you*, Teddy? That she was quitting?" Her voice went up, for the first time. "Get out."

"Okay. Okay, I hear you, I hear that you're upset—"

"*Get out.*"

Archie was on his feet. He couldn't help it; it was a stage, and someone in the wrong place was holding it up. He crossed the room in three steps and put a friendly arm around Teddy's shoulders and lifted him gently off his heels and turned him toward the door, all in one movement, talking the whole time in the warm, easy voice he used for volunteers who'd stayed up too long.

"Teddy, let me walk you out. You've had a long night. We've all had a long night. Come on, there's coffee downstairs, Pru's got a—"

"Don't."

He stopped, his arm still around Teddy's shoulders. Ivy was standing in the middle of the room with her hands in fists at her sides.

"Don't help me," she said.

Archie took his arm away. Teddy looked from one of them to the other, put both phones in his vest pockets, and went out on his own, saying something about the network to nobody in particular. They heard his white sneakers squeak all the way down the stairs.

Ivy picked up the buffalo-check coat from the arm of her chair. She held it out to Archie across the room, at arm's length, folded.

"Take your coat," she said.

He took it. She sat back down in the armchair by the window and looked out at the harbor.

Tess clicked off the recorder. "Thank you, Ms. Crane. I'll be in touch about the binder. I'll give you a receipt for it." She picked it up, and wrote the receipt, and laid it on the table beside the chowder, which nobody had touched. Then she looked at Archie, and at the door.

---

The third-floor hall was narrow and low, with a runner of faded red carpet and a window at the end over the parking lot. Tess walked to the window before she stopped, and Archie followed, with his own coat over his arm, and they stood there with the binder between them.

"Well," said Archie.

"She said it twice," said Tess. "*She held my hand.* Both times before I'd finished the question." She looked out at the lot. "People who are telling the truth answer the question you asked. She answered the one she'd been waiting for."

"That's a cop's read."

"That's my read." She shifted the binder under her arm. "And now it's on tape, in her words, voluntary, twice. If it isn't true, it's going to matter."

"I got something you couldn't," Archie said.

"I know you did." She didn't say it kindly or unkindly. "She didn't know her mother was quitting. Her mother told a stranger with a drink and not her own daughter, and you're the one who handed her that." Tess looked at him. "Was that a kindness?"

He opened his mouth and found he didn't know.

"I let you in," Tess said, "because I wanted to see her face when she looked at the man who'd told me about the scarf. I saw it. That's what I came for." She turned toward the stairs. "Don't come to my interviews, Mr. Fairweather."

"You opened the door."

"Next time I'll keep it shut."

She went down. Archie stood at the end of the hall with his coat over his arm. It was warm from the room, and it smelled faintly of menthol, and of something expensive, and he stood there holding it longer than he needed to before he put it on.
