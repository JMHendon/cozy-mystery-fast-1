# Chapter 27

## Narrowed Down

By two o'clock it was raining the way it only rains on the coast of Maine at the end of October: sideways, off the harbor, cold enough to sting, and in no hurry at all.

Lieutenant Neil Brannock's car was a dark gray Explorer with state plates, parked nose-in at the back of the town hall lot under a bare maple. The driver's door stood open. Brannock sat sideways in the driver's seat with his feet out on the running board and a laptop on his knees — Tess's, with a State Police sticker on the lid — and his reading glasses on, which he wore with the resignation other men bring to a cast. Rain was coming in on his left shoulder. He didn't seem to have noticed. Beside the open door, in her charcoal coat with the hood up and her braid gone dark with wet, stood Tess Calloway, holding a clear evidence bag with the little black card case inside it. Archie stood beside her in his buffalo-check coat, which had begun to smell like a sheep.

He had called her at half past twelve from the hearse. She'd listened, and said nothing for so long that he took the phone away from his ear to check it, and then she'd said: *Parking lot. Two o'clock. He leaves for Rockland at ten past. You get ten minutes.* She had hung up before he could thank her. He understood that she'd taken it to Brannock herself, and what that had cost her to do, and he had been grateful about it for an hour and a half.

"Lieutenant," he'd begun, when he came across the lot, "before you say anything, I'd like you to watch something remarkable—"

"I've watched it," Brannock had said. "Three times. Sergeant Calloway insisted."

Now he watched seven forty-four once more — Ivy's hands under the tablecloth, twenty-three inches from the horn — and then sat back and took the glasses off.

"Where did you get this?"

"It was found in the cameraman's kit," said Archie. "By Mr. Marchand. Who gave it to me. As a witness."

"When?"

"This morning."

"Where?"

There was no point. "The airport in Owls Head. Around eight-thirty."

Tess turned her head and looked at him. He felt it on the side of his face like a draft from an open door.

Brannock didn't look at Tess. He folded the glasses and put them in his breast pocket. "On Sunday Mr. Marchand told me that card was exteriors and had gone to Los Angeles with his drives. I'll be having a conversation with Mr. Marchand's lawyers about the word *exteriors*." He looked at the laptop. "So the only footage that clears my suspect has been hand-delivered to me by a man who fools cameras for a living, who got it in an airport from a man who lied to me about it, in exchange for —" He stopped. "What did you give him?"

"An interview."

Brannock waited.

"And something else," said Archie. "It isn't relevant."

"Everything's relevant until it isn't." Brannock closed the laptop and handed it out to Tess, who tucked it under her coat. "This goes to our forensic video people in Augusta before it's worth anything to anybody. Metadata. Continuity. Whether the card is from the camera it says it's from. If it's clean, it's clean."

"It's clean."

"I hope so. I really do." He meant it; Archie could see that he meant it. "Now. You want me to call the jail and open the door."

"She never touched the horn after I put it down. You just watched it."

"I watched twenty minutes of a tablecloth. Here's what I have, Mr. Fairweather, and I'd like you to listen to all of it, because I'm only going to say it once, in the rain." Brannock held up a thumb. "Ms. Crane wiped the mouthpiece of the murder weapon with her scarf in front of you, and the scarf came back from the lab with aconitine on it and her mother's lipstick. She told my sergeant her mother held her hand the whole time, and she's on Mr. Duchesne's tape letting go of it. She ditched a remote with her prints on it in an umbrella stand. And she laid out every prop on that table at seven-twenty, alone, before you ever got near it, while the room was still taking its coats off. That's what put her in a cell. Nothing on this card changes one word of it."

"That's tampering. That's fraud. That isn't—"

"I know what it is. The tampering stands either way; she did it." Brannock looked out at the rain. "Now your card. Your card clears her of the murder on one condition, and the condition is seven thirty-five. Your little trumpet solo." He looked up at Archie. "Did your lips touch the metal, Mr. Fairweather? Or did you make a noise near it and let a roomful of people and two cameras believe they did?"

"I put my mouth on it."

"I'm sure you did. You're very convincing. It's the job."

"Then swab it. You'll find me."

"We did. It's a mixture. A small one — the victim, and whatever the scarf left behind, and possibly somebody else. The lab calls a third person inconclusive and is running six weeks behind." Brannock didn't sound pleased about it. "So I can't put you on it, and I can't keep you off it. All right. Say it was real. Say you tooted, honest as the day. Then the horn's clean at seven thirty-five, and I've got your forty seconds, and in your forty seconds I've got a retired schoolteacher who moved the thing at the producer's request, in gloves, with no connection to the victim we can find. I've got a fan by the radiator whose channel has made more money since Saturday than in the five years before it. I've got a widow who'd paid the victim a thousand and fifty dollars and was waiting to hear from her husband." He paused. "And I've got your mother."

"My mother."

"Your mother, who cut a vase of monkshood out of your garden last week for the kitchen table and told us so herself, very helpfully. Your mother, who's in that frame at her chair, two feet from the horn, with her hands on the back of it." He said it plainly, with no pleasure in it at all. "I don't think your mother poisoned anybody. I'm telling you what that tape says to a defense lawyer who's never met her. A mother and her son. He does the talking; she's at the table. That's a story a jury can follow."

Rain drummed on the roof of the Explorer. Archie found he had nothing to say, which happened to him perhaps twice a year.

"And if it wasn't real," said Brannock, "then I'm back where I was on Monday. Then I've got Ms. Crane at seven-twenty, alone with the props. Or I've got the one man in that building with a motive — two million of them — and poison growing in his own dooryard, and the best pair of hands in the country, putting the horn to his mouth on camera and fooling the room for the length of a raspberry." He let it sit. "Either way, your card doesn't open a door. It takes a door I'd nearly finished with and opens it back up onto you."

"Where would Ivy even get aconite? She lives in Los Angeles. You've been through her room and her car and her phone—"

"Half the dooryards on this peninsula grow it, including yours. She was here three days before the séance, and she knew to the inch what went into her mother's mouth." Brannock swung his feet in out of the rain. "I'm not telling you it's a lovely case. I'm telling you it's a case."

He put the key in the ignition. Then he looked up at Archie, and his face changed — not softer, exactly. Older.

"One more thing, and then I'm going to Rockland. Since Saturday you've been to see the victim's daughter, the widow, the producer, the fan, and the chair of the select board, and you confronted one of them in a diner in front of forty people. Every person on that tape is a witness in a homicide. If you go near any of them again about this case, I'll treat it as tampering with a witness, and I'll charge it. I won't enjoy it. I'll do it anyway." He looked at Tess for the first time. "Sergeant Calloway won't be in contact with you about this case from here on. Anything you have, you bring to me. In daylight. Through the front door."

"Yes, sir," said Tess, in a voice with nothing in it at all.

Brannock pulled the door shut. The Explorer backed out of its space, crossed the empty lot, signaled — at nobody — and turned onto Main Street toward Route 1, wipers going, and was gone.

Tess stood in the rain with the evidence bag in her hand and the laptop under her coat.

"You went to the airport," she said.

"Yes."

"At eight in the morning. Alone. To a witness who'd lied to us." She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the empty space where the Explorer had been. "And then you called me at half past twelve and let me carry it to him. And let him ask you where it came from with me standing here."

"I didn't think he'd—"

"He thinks I sent you." She turned. Her face was wet, and her gray eyes were very steady. "He's thought I was running you since the walkthrough on Sunday. He was nearly done thinking it. Now he isn't." She held the evidence bag up between them. "Do you know what I'd have done, if you'd called me at seven instead of twelve-thirty? I'd have driven. I'd have walked in with a badge. I'd have had Marchand sign for it in front of a witness, with a time on it. It'd be worth something."

"He'd never have handed it to a badge."

"Then we'd have gotten a warrant."

"And he'd have been in Boston, and that card would have been in a trash can at Logan."

"Maybe." She didn't give him an inch. "Why didn't you call me?"

He thought about lying to her, and knew he wouldn't manage it. He never had, with anyone he cared about.

"Because you'd have told me not to go," he said.

"Yes," said Tess. "I would have." The rain ran off the edge of her hood. "That's what calling me is for, Archie."

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she turned and walked away across the lot to the back door of the town hall, with the bag and the laptop held against her, not hurrying, through the rain. She didn't look back. The door shut behind her, and he stood in the empty lot in a wet coat that smelled like a sheep, with nothing left in his pockets but a napkin.
