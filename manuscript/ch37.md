# Chapter 37

## Intermission

The note was four lines in pencil, on the back of a page torn from the cue book, in the same upright hand as the envelope in his pocket.

*Archie —*
*Thank you for the lights. I'm sorry about the second half.*
*Ivy Crane didn't do it. You know that.*
*— H.*

He read it twice. Then he turned it over. On the other side, in Hollis's writing from some earlier year, faded, it said *Q.47 — blue to pin on 4th bird — GO*.

"The booth's empty," said Lenny. "Board's on the act-one preset. Her coat's gone. The fire-escape door at the end of the balcony's propped open." He paused. "The one the fire marshal made her unlock."

Archie looked up from the note.

The stage-left wing was a narrow dim space between the back wall and the edge of the curtain, crowded with Lenny's rope rail and a prop table and the Empty Chair itself — a plain bentwood chair with a white sheet folded over its back, waiting for the finale. Through the gap in the curtain he could hear the hall, loud and happy, three hundred people standing in the aisles with their cider. Somebody was laughing about Lionel.

"Archie." It was June, at his elbow, in the blue velvet jacket. Her eyes went from his face to the note in his hand. "What's wrong? What's going on?"

He didn't answer, and he saw her see that he didn't.

The side door to the balcony stairs banged open.

Tess Calloway came through it fast, with Kayla Bissonnette behind her, and she had a folded legal document in one hand and her phone in the other, and her face was the face from the hospital waiting room — the whole room in a second and a half — except that this time, when it reached Archie, it stopped.

"Where is she?"

"She's gone." He held out the note. "Out the fire escape. I don't know where."

Tess took the note and read it. Kayla, behind her, had gone white to the lips. "I was on the booth door, Sarge," Kayla said. "The whole time. She never came out the booth door, I swear to—"

"I know. It's not on you." Tess didn't look away from Archie. "The balcony fire door. It's supposed to be locked."

"Fire marshal made her unlock it on Thursday," said Lenny. "For the show. She grumbled."

Tess closed her eyes for exactly one second.

"We executed the warrant at six-forty," she said, very quietly. "Her house and her shed. There's a blue glass jar on the second shelf of that potting shed, Archie, with about four ounces of something in it, and a pair of black cotton gloves rolled up beside it like a pair of socks, and the evidence tech wouldn't let any of us in the door until he'd put his own gloves on." Her voice was perfectly level. "We were going to take her at intermission. Quietly. Out the back. Not in front of three hundred people." She looked at the note in her hand. "You knew. When you sent me that text, you knew."

"Yes."

"*Intermission. Please.*" She said it flatly. "You gave me a riddle."

"I gave you the time. She asked me for the first half. Gus's half. She said she'd come down out of that booth and go with you after—"

"She *asked* you."

"She gave me this." He took the envelope out of his tailcoat and held it out to her. *Det. Sgt. Theresa Calloway — by hand.* "She wrote it last night. When they charged Ivy. She said it was everything. She said it went to you tonight, either way."

Tess looked at the envelope for a long moment without taking it. Then she took it. She didn't open it. She put it inside her coat.

June was standing very still beside him.

"Dad," she said. Her voice was very small and very clear. "Who's *she*?"

He turned and looked at his daughter in her grandfather's velvet jacket, with her hair up and her face white, and understood that he had kept her in the audience all day. At breakfast, at seven, she'd asked him, *Did you check the Danny thing?* and he'd said *I'm looking into it*, and handed her a piece of toast, and driven her to school in the hearse, which she had for once allowed. He had kept it from her exactly the way he'd kept every method from her for sixteen years, and told himself it was a gift.

"Hollis," he said.

June's face didn't change. She'd been at the kitchen table at two in the morning with him; she'd read the same tiny handwriting in the same small books. She had known, he realized. Somewhere under *lots of people are named Danny*, she had known as well as he had, and she'd let him put her to bed and keep it from her, because he was her father and she'd thought he'd tell her.

"You knew this morning," June said.

"Yes."

"And you didn't—" She stopped.

Vivienne's phone rang.

His mother was in the wing behind June, in her black and her diamonds, and she'd heard all of it; he saw that in her face. She looked at the screen and answered it. "Ozzie," she said, and listened, and her free hand went to her throat.

"Ozzie's on the wharf," she said. "For the children and their silly dare. He says somebody just took Elwin's skiff off the town float. No lights. Heading out." She swallowed. "Toward the bell."

Nobody said anything.

Out past the end of the point, in the cold black water off Shag Ledge, where Gus Fairweather's skiff had been found drifting in 1988 with his cloak folded on the seat. Where a lobsterman named Peter Langley had put his boat on the rocks in a November gale, and drowned, because a voice in the dark had told him the water was warm.

Tess already had her phone to her ear. "This is Calloway. I need Marine Patrol and Coast Guard Station Rockland to Merrow Harbor, a fourteen-foot skiff, one person aboard, no lights, heading for the bell off Shag Ledge—" She was already moving toward the stairs. She stopped at the side door and turned and pointed at Archie.

"You stay here," she said.

"Tess—"

"You *stay here.* This is mine now. This is my job, and it's my town, and she was my teacher, and you've done enough." She looked past him at June, and at the empty chair with the sheet over its back, and her voice changed very slightly. "You've got a show."

She went down the stairs. Kayla went with her.

Archie stood in the wing.

He could hear the house through the curtain, the cheerful roar of it. In about twelve minutes, Lenny would flash the house lights and they would all go back to their seats, and the second half would start, and at the end of the second half, forty minutes from now, the stage would go dark, and a single chair would stand in a single spotlight, and June would come out in Gus's jacket and say the words he'd taught her on Wednesday night in Lenny's workshop, and pull the sheet, and her grandmother would be sitting there, and he would be standing beside the chair looking surprised. He had promised her that. He'd promised her, in a hotel room in Boston, a real year in a real place, and in the opera house on Wednesday he'd promised he'd be beside her on the stage when she did it, and before either of those, last spring, without saying it out loud, he had promised her the one thing that mattered — that he would never again walk off a stage and leave her sitting in the audience wondering where he'd gone.

And out there in the fog, a woman he loved was sitting in a skiff with no lights at the place where her husband had drowned.

He couldn't do both. There wasn't a trick for it. He'd been looking for one for the last ninety seconds, the way he'd looked at every locked thing his whole life, and there wasn't one.

"Go," said June.

He turned.

She was standing in front of the Empty Chair in the blue velvet jacket with both her hands in fists at her sides, and her eyes were wet, and furious, and absolutely steady.

"June—"

"I'm still mad at you. I'm going to be mad at you for a really long time." Her voice shook and she pushed straight through it, the way Shannon had on the wharf. "But you know what she's going to do. You know better than anybody. So go."

"The Chair—"

"I know how to do the Chair. You taught me. All of it." She lifted her chin. "Grandma's in the chair anyway. You were only going to stand there and look surprised."

"I can do the second half, darling," said Vivienne, from behind her, in her stage voice — and then, in her real one: "Lenny and I can do the second half in our sleep. We did it with your grandfather for twelve years. I know every word of his patter. I wrote half of it."

"Mattie has the cue book," said Lenny. "Hollis showed her the board. Thursday." He looked at Archie under his enormous white eyebrows. "Go."

Archie looked at the three of them — his mother in her diamonds, his oldest friend in his fourth sweater, his daughter in his grandfather's jacket with her fists clenched — standing in the narrow wing in front of the chair.

He had spent his whole life giving people a show. He had never once in his life let anybody give him one.

"Okay," he said.

He let June see it. He let her see his face while he chose, with nothing over it at all — no patter, no grin, no trick. Then he bent down and kissed the top of her head, the way he had when she was small enough to let him, and she let him, and he turned and went through the side door and down the back stairs two at a time, with his grandfather's opera cloak flying out behind him over his tails.

Tess was at the bottom of the stairs, at the stage door, on her phone. She looked up at him coming down.

She opened her mouth.

Then she shut it again, and turned, and shoved the stage door open into the alley, and held it for him.

The cold came in off the harbor like a wall. The fog had come up while they were inside. It hung in the alley and in the street beyond, thick and gray and glowing under the streetlights, and it smelled of salt and kelp and woodsmoke, and through it, from somewhere far out past the end of the point, came the sound of the bell buoy off Shag Ledge, ringing and ringing.

They ran.
