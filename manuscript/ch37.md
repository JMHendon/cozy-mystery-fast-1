# Chapter 37

## Intermission

The note was four lines in pencil, on the back of a page torn from the cue book, in the same upright hand as the envelope in his pocket.

*Archie —*
*Thank you for the lights. I'm sorry about the second half.*
*Ivy Crane didn't do it. You know that.*
*— H.*

He read it twice. Then he turned it over. On the other side, in Hollis's writing from some earlier year, faded, it said *Q.142 — blue to pin on 4th bird — GO*.

"The booth's empty," said Lenny. "Board's on the act-one preset. Her hat's gone. The fire-escape door at the end of the balcony's propped open." He paused. "The one the fire marshal made her unlock."

The stage-left wing was a narrow dim space between the back wall and the edge of the curtain, crowded with Lenny's rope rail and a prop table and the Empty Chair itself — a plain bentwood chair with a white sheet folded over its back, waiting for the finale. Through the curtain came the noise of the hall, three hundred people in the aisles with their cider. Somebody was laughing about Lionel.

"Dad." June was at his elbow in the blue velvet jacket. Her eyes went from his face to the note in his hand. "What's wrong?"

He didn't answer, and he saw her see that he didn't.

The side door to the balcony stairs banged open, and Tess Calloway came through it fast with Kayla Bissonnette behind her, a folded legal document in one hand and her phone in the other.

"Where is she?"

"Gone." He held out the note. "Out the fire escape. I don't know where."

Tess took the note and read it. Kayla had gone white to the lips. "I had the balcony stairs, Sarge. The whole time. She never came down them, I swear—"

"I know. It's not on you." Tess didn't look away from Archie. "The balcony fire door is supposed to be locked."

"Fire marshal made her unlock it on Thursday," said Lenny. "For the show. She grumbled."

Tess closed her eyes for one second.

"We executed the warrant at six-forty," she said, quietly. "Her house and her shed. There's a blue glass jar on the second shelf of that potting shed with a few ounces of something brown in it, and a pair of black cotton gloves rolled up beside it like socks, and the evidence tech wouldn't let any of us through the door until he'd put his own gloves on." Her voice was level. "We were going to take her at intermission. Quietly. Out the back." She looked at the note. "You knew. When you sent me that text, you knew."

"Yes."

"*Intermission. Please.*" She said it flatly. "You gave me a riddle."

"I gave you the time. She asked me for the first half. Gus's half. She said she'd come down out of that booth and go with you after—"

"She *asked* you."

"She gave me this." He took the envelope out of his tailcoat. *Det. Sgt. Theresa Calloway — by hand.* "She wrote it last night, when they charged Ivy. She said it was everything. She said it went to you tonight, either way."

Tess looked at the envelope without taking it. Then she took it, and put it inside her coat unopened.

"Dad," said June. "Who's *she*?"

He looked at his daughter in her great-grandfather's jacket, with her hair up and her face white. At seven that morning, at breakfast, she had asked him, *Did you check the Danny thing?* and he'd said *I'm looking into it*, and handed her a piece of toast, and driven her to school.

"Hollis," he said.

June's face didn't change. She had sat at the kitchen table half the night with him, reading the same tiny handwriting in the same small books. Under *lots of people are named Danny*, he saw now, she had known. She'd let him send her to bed, because he was her father and she'd thought he would tell her.

"You knew this morning," June said.

"Yes."

"And you didn't—" She stopped.

Vivienne's phone rang.

His mother was in the wing behind June, in her black and her diamonds, and she'd heard all of it. She looked at the screen and answered. "Ozzie," she said, and listened, and her free hand went to her throat.

"Ozzie's on the wharf," she said. "For the children and their silly dare. He says somebody just took Elwin's skiff off the town float. No lights. Heading out." She swallowed. "Toward the bell."

Out past the end of the point. Shag Ledge.

Tess already had her phone to her ear. "This is Calloway. I need Marine Patrol and Coast Guard Station Rockland to Merrow Harbor, a fourteen-foot skiff, one person aboard, no lights, heading for the bell off Shag Ledge—" She was moving toward the stairs. At the side door she turned and pointed at Archie.

"You stay here," she said.

"Tess—"

"You *stay here.* This is mine now. It's my job, and it's my town, and she was my teacher, and you've done enough." She looked past him at June, and at the chair with the sheet over its back, and her voice changed slightly. "You've got a show."

She went down the stairs. Kayla went with her.

Archie stood in the wing.

In twelve minutes Lenny would flash the house lights and the second half would start, and at the end of it the stage would go dark and a single chair would stand in a single spotlight, and June would come out in Gus's jacket and say the words he'd taught her on Wednesday night and pull the sheet. He had promised he'd be standing beside that chair when she did. Before that he'd promised her a real year in a real place. Before that, without saying it out loud, he had promised her he would never again walk off a stage and leave her in the audience wondering where he'd gone.

And out there in the fog, a woman he loved was sitting in a skiff with no lights at the place where her husband had drowned.

He couldn't do both. There wasn't a trick for it.

He turned to June, and he didn't put anything over his face. No patter, no grin. He let her watch him choose.

"I'm going," he said. "I'm breaking my promise. I'm sorry, June. I'm going after her."

June stood in front of the Empty Chair with her hands in fists at her sides. Her eyes were wet, and furious, and steady.

"I'm still mad at you," she said. "I'm going to be mad at you for a really long time." Her voice shook. She kept going. "But you know what she's going to do. You know better than anybody. So go."

"The Chair—"

"I know how to do the Chair. You taught me. All of it." She lifted her chin. "Grandma's in the chair anyway. You were only going to stand there and look surprised."

"Darling," said Vivienne, behind her, in her stage voice — and then, in her real one: "I can do the second half in my sleep. I was your grandfather's assistant for twelve years. I know every word of his patter; I wrote half of it. And Lenny built everything else."

"Mattie has the cue book," said Lenny. "Hollis showed her the board. Thursday." He looked at Archie from under his enormous white eyebrows. "Go."

Archie bent and kissed the top of June's head, and she let him. Then he went through the side door and down the back stairs two at a time, with his grandfather's opera cloak flying out behind him over his tails.

Tess was at the bottom of the stairs, at the stage door, on her phone. She looked up at him coming down.

She opened her mouth.

Then she shut it, and turned, and shoved the stage door open into the alley, and held it for him.

The cold came in off the harbor like a wall. The fog had come up while they were inside; it hung in the alley and in the street beyond, thick and gray and glowing under the streetlights, and from somewhere far out past the end of the point came the bell buoy off Shag Ledge, ringing and ringing.

They ran.
