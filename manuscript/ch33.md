# Chapter 33

## Blueberry or Apple

At ten minutes to five on Halloween morning the town wharf was black and wet and empty, and the fog was so thick that the lights of the Galley seemed to hang in it by themselves, with no building around them.

Archie knocked on the glass.

Bev Thibodeau was behind the counter with her back to him, sliding pies into the case. She turned, looked at him through the door, and pointed at the clock over the coffee urn. Then she went back to the pies.

He stood on the step in his buffalo-check coat with his hands in his pockets and waited. Out in the harbor, somewhere beyond the moorings, the bell buoy was ringing, slow and uneven, and a boat engine he couldn't see turned over and caught and went away toward it. At five o'clock exactly the bolt went back.

"Sit down," said Bev. "You're letting the fog in."

He sat on the stool at the end of the counter by the register. The Galley at five in the morning was a different room from the Galley at seven — chairs still upside down on the tables, the booths empty, the windows gray, the grill only beginning to tick as it heated. It smelled of coffee and hot sugar and the bleach she'd used on the floor last night. The radio on the shelf by the kitchen door was reading the marine forecast: *fog, visibility under one mile, seas two to four feet.*

Bev took the chairs down off the liars' table, one at a time, and set them around it at the angles they liked. She filled the urn. She poured a mug of coffee and put it in front of him without being asked, and then she looked at him properly for the first time, over her reading glasses, with the pot still in her hand.

"You've been up all night."

"Yes."

"You look it." She put the pot back on the warmer. "Drink that."

He drank some of it. It was too hot and too strong and exactly what he needed, and it didn't help.

"Your mother was out half the night herself," said Bev. "Ozzie Lund had her at the Lobster Pound in Lincolnville till all hours. Fried clams. Two forks on one whoopie pie." She set out the sugar. "Whole wharf'll know by six."

Archie looked at her.

"Ozzie," he said.

"Ozzie," said Bev. "Drink that."

He had driven down Shore Road in the dark with the whole thing laid out in his head, and somewhere on the way he had decided what he wanted. He wanted Bev to laugh at him. He wanted her to say *No, that's some other Langley, there were Langleys all up and down this coast,* and put a piece of pie in front of him and tell him he looked like something left on a mooring, and send him home to bed. He'd driven down here to be told he was wrong by the one person in Merrow who'd never once pretended he was right.

"Bev," he said. "Settle something for me."

"I'm opening."

"It's a small thing. A name."

She was wiping down the counter with a gray cloth, in long strokes. She didn't stop. "Go on."

"Langley," said Archie. "Peter Langley. He's on the memorial."

The cloth stopped.

Bev stood there with her hand flat on the counter. Then she folded the cloth into a square and set it down by the register.

"Langley?" she said. "That's Hollis."

He didn't say anything.

"Hollis Langley, she was, back when. Married Peter in — eighty-three, it would've been. Roland and I went." Bev picked the cloth up again and found nothing to do with it. "She kept Tibbetts at the school. Said she'd taught two hundred kids to spell it already and she wasn't starting over. Everybody else called her Langley. Then after Peter she went back to Tibbetts for all of it. Mail, bank, everything." She looked at him. "You didn't know that."

"No."

"No reason you would. It's twenty years."

"She had a boy."

"Danny." Bev said it the way you'd set down something heavy you'd carried a long way. "Up the quarry. August, oh-one. He was fifteen. Went off the high ledge with the other boys and didn't come up." She was quiet a moment. "Danny Langley ate a whole blueberry pie on that stool you're sitting on, when he was twelve, on a bet. Roland bet him he couldn't. Roland paid up, too. Five dollars."

Archie put both hands around the mug.

"And Peter—" he said.

"Two years after. Near enough. November." Bev's flat Down East voice didn't change, which was how he knew what it cost. "Took the *Hollis T.* out in a gale warning, middle of the afternoon, all by himself. Nobody knew why. Nobody ever did know why. She fetched up on Shag Ledge. They found him two days after." She looked past Archie, out the gray window, toward the place in the fog where the bell buoy was. "Hollis sat in that back booth every morning that winter. Wouldn't eat. I fed her anyhow."

The urn began to gurgle. Neither of them looked at it.

---

He had thought, at three in the morning at his own kitchen table, that it would feel like a trick coming clear — the moment in the second row when you finally see the thread. It didn't. It felt like a room he'd been living in for a week, filling up slowly with water, and everything in it was something he had already touched.

A letter to a television company in May, before he'd ever come back to Merrow. *Don't cancel her. Beat her. In Gus's house.* Page one-twelve — Mrs. Tibbetts's séance unit. Teddy's voice from the wing, *Hollis, sorry, can you slide the horn left*, in the forty seconds the camera spent looking at the harbor. A boy on a dock. Shannon's fingertip going around the rim of the horn at seven forty-five, which had frightened him for two days, and which had only ever been a woman saying hello to her husband.

*Oh, sweetheart. The water's never warm here.*

He had heard it. He had stood on the step and heard it, and thought it was the kindest thing anybody had said in that building all week, and gone back down so as not to interrupt.

He looked up. Bev was watching him.

She hadn't asked a single question. She had been watching faces across this counter for forty years, and she had seen his, and she had heard the name he'd put next to it. That was all she had. It was enough. Her square, plain face held, and then, slowly, it gave way, and Archie watched Bev Thibodeau — who had never once sat down behind her own counter in his presence — reach for the stool by the register and sit on it.

"Archie," she said. "What is this."

He couldn't tell her. The words wouldn't come up past his chest.

Bev nodded, once, the way she nodded when somebody told her they were paying cash.

"All right," she said. "I don't want to know. I don't." She took off her reading glasses and pressed her thumb and finger into the corners of her eyes, and put the glasses back on. "But whatever it is, it goes to Tess Calloway. Not to you. Not to Dale. To Tess."

"I know."

"Today."

"I have to talk to Hollis first."

Bev's eyes narrowed. "Why."

"Because I owe her that." He heard how it sounded. He said it anyway. "Because she gave me the keys, Bev. In August. She brought me a pie and the keys to the opera house and told me I looked like I needed both. I'm not going to let her find out from a cruiser in her dooryard."

Out on the wharf, a truck door slammed, and then another.

"Show's tonight," said Bev.

"Seven o'clock."

"Done by ten?"

"About ten."

"Then at ten o'clock," said Bev, "I'm calling Tess myself. I don't care what it is. I'll give Hollis her show, if that's what this is. I'm not carrying it one minute past ten." She stood up off the stool. "You hear me?"

"I hear you."

She went to the pie case and slid the door open.

"Blueberry or apple."

"Bev, I'm not—"

"I didn't ask if you were hungry. There's two. Pick."

He looked at the pies. "Blueberry," he said.

She cut him a slice, a wide one, and put it on a plate in front of him with a fork, and went back to the urn. He sat looking at it. He didn't eat it.

---

The bell over the door rang at a quarter past five, and the cold came in, and the liars' table came in with it — Elwin Coombs first, then Dickie Thibodeau, then the long silent shape of Pickle, in their barn coats and their caps, stamping the fog off their boots. They went past the counter toward their round table by the kitchen door, and Bev was already pouring their coffee.

Elwin slowed beside the end stool. He looked at the man sitting there, at his gray face and his untouched pie, and he stopped.

"Morning, Archie," said Elwin Coombs.

It was the first time in sixty-six days that the liars' table had spoken to him. He had kept count of every one of them.

Archie was looking out the window, at the fog where the bell buoy was ringing, and he didn't hear it.
