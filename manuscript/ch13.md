# Chapter 13

## Rosie and Liam

Shannon Mayo lived in a small gray-shingled house on the lane behind the lobster co-op, close enough to the water that the dooryard smelled of bait. There were traps stacked four high along the fence, green wire, with a buoy color Archie had seen on the stern of a boat called *Rosie & Liam* — yellow over blue. Nobody had sold them. On the front step sat a pumpkin carved by someone about seven years old, with a great deal of enthusiasm and no fear of triangles.

It was four o'clock, and the sun was already low over the harbor, laying long yellow light across the street. Archie stood on the step in his own coat with his hands in the pockets. He had considered bringing something — flowers, a pie — and decided you didn't bring a present to say you were sorry. Now he was here with nothing in his hands, and his hands didn't like it.

He knocked.

Shannon opened the door. She was in sweatpants and a man's navy hooded sweatshirt much too big for her, with the sleeves pushed up, and her red-gold hair was tied back any old way. Her eyes were red. When she saw who it was, her face closed like a hatch.

"No," she said.

"Shannon, I only want two minutes. I came to say—"

"I don't want two minutes." She started to shut the door.

A small boy ducked under her arm.

He was seven or so, in socks and a Red Sox shirt, with a gap where his front teeth should have been, and his mouth fell open.

"Mom," he said. "*Mom.* It's the guy. It's the riverboat guy."

"Liam, go inside."

"You made a *whole boat* disappear." Liam pulled the door wide with the absolute strength of seven. "Mom, he made a *boat* disappear. On the *Mississippi.* Can you come in? Can you do one? Mom, can he come in?"

Behind him, in the hall, a little girl of about four appeared, wearing a red ladybug costume with black spots and two wobbly antennae on a headband, and holding a plastic pumpkin bucket that was empty. She stared at Archie in silence.

"That's Rosie," Liam said. "She's been a ladybug since Thursday. She won't take it off."

"It's a very good ladybug," said Archie.

Shannon looked at her son's hand on the door, and at her daughter in the hall, and at Archie. He watched her do the arithmetic of a mother who could not have a fight on her own doorstep in front of her children, and lose.

"Kitchen," she said. "Not past the kitchen."

The kitchen was small and warm and clean, with a table under the window and a refrigerator covered in drawings held up by magnets from the marine supply in Rockland. Most of the drawings were of boats. One was of a man in yellow oilskins, very tall, with a smile that went off the edge of his face. A pair of rubber boots stood by the back door, men's, size twelve or so, with the tops folded down. Nobody had moved them either.

"Do one," said Liam. "Please. Do one, do one."

Archie looked at Shannon. She'd folded her arms and leaned against the counter. He took that as permission, and knew it wasn't.

"Name a card," he said to Liam.

"Um. The king of — the seven of — the ace of spades!"

Archie showed him both hands, empty, front and back, and pushed his coat sleeves up past the wrist. Then he reached into the air a foot in front of Liam's nose and closed his fingers, and when he opened them there was a card standing between the first two, and it was the ace of spades.

Liam screamed. Rosie, who had been hiding behind her mother's leg, came out.

"Me," she said. It was the first thing she'd said.

"What card would you like, ladybug?"

Rosie thought about it hard. "The princess one."

He reached up next to one of her wobbling antennae, slowly, so she could watch, and brought down the queen of hearts, and gave it to her. She held it in both hands and looked at it, and then up at him, and then she laughed — a small, astonished, hiccuping laugh — and ran to show her brother, and the two of them collided in the doorway and fell over together in a heap of ladybug and Red Sox, shrieking.

Liam sat up first, with the ace held against his chest. He looked at the card, and at Archie, and at his mother, and the shrieking went out of him.

"Is my dad really talking to Mom?" he said. "On the phone? Mom says he says stuff."

The kitchen went quiet. Rosie, on the floor, looked from one of them to the other. Shannon didn't move off the counter, and she didn't help him.

Archie had a dozen answers ready. There was one that would make the boy smile, and he could have done it in his sleep.

He crouched down so his face was level with Liam's.

"I don't think so, Liam," he said. "I think the lady on the phone was guessing, and she was very good at guessing. That was her job." He waited until Liam looked at him. "But your mom wasn't guessing. She listened because she misses your dad. That part's true."

Liam thought about it. He looked at his mother.

"Okay," he said, and looked down at his card.

"Liam," said Shannon, without taking her eyes off Archie. "Take your sister and go put those somewhere safe. In your room. Go on."

They went, thundering. A door banged upstairs.

"That's what you do," Shannon said. Her voice was low and even. "Isn't it. You come in and you make them look at the pretty thing over here, and they laugh. That's the same thing. That's the exact same thing she did."

"Shannon, I came to say I'm sorry."

"For what?"

"I promised I wouldn't humiliate you. And last night, in the dark, in front of all those cameras, with Tommy's—" He stopped. "With that voice. It was worse than anything I promised you about. I should never have let you sit there. I picked you. I'm sorry."

"You think I don't know how it's done?" She pushed herself off the counter. "You think I'm stupid? I went to Penobscot Regional. I had Mrs. Tibbetts for senior English. I did the séance unit. I wrote the five-page paper — *How the Grieving Are Fooled*, typed, double-spaced. I got an A-minus." Her voice cracked. "And Hollis already sat at this table this morning, at seven o'clock, and gave me the whole thing again. The research. The Facebook. The obituary in the *Packet*. Page one-twelve. She was kind about it. She was so kind I wanted to throw the coffeepot at her."

"Then you know—"

"I know. I've always known. That's not the point. I don't need you to explain it, and I don't need her to explain it, and I don't need every person in the Galley tomorrow looking at me like I'm the poor dumb widow who cried at a tin horn." She was breathing hard. "He talked to me. Since the summer, he talked to me. I could stand in the kitchen at night, and he — and now she's dead, and everybody's going to say he was never there, and you'll be on television explaining how. That's what you did. You and your bet. You took him away again."

Archie stood in her kitchen with his empty hands at his sides.

"I'm sorry," he said. It was all there was.

The front door opened without a knock.

"It's me," called Hollis Tibbetts from the hall. "I've brought American chop suey, and I don't want to hear a word about it, because you haven't eaten and neither have those children." She came into the kitchen in her men's cardigan and a wool hat, with a covered Pyrex dish held in two oven mitts, and stopped when she saw Archie.

"Aunt Hollis!" Rosie came down the stairs much faster than a ladybug should, and hit Hollis around the knees.

"There's my girl. Careful — hot." Hollis set the dish on the stove, pulled off one mitt and put her hand on Rosie's head. Then she looked at Shannon's face, and at Archie's, and took the measure of the room in about the time it took to put down a casserole.

"Archie," she said. "Go home."

"I was just—"

"I know what you were just. Go home." She said it warmly and without any give in it. "Shannon, sit down before you fall down. Rosie, show me what you've got there. Is that the queen of hearts? Well. Aren't you grand."

Archie went. He went back down the little hall past the stairs, where Liam was sitting halfway up with the ace of spades held against his chest, watching him with enormous eyes. Archie lifted a hand. Liam lifted the card.

Shannon caught him at the front door.

She'd followed him down the hall, fast, and she held the door so he couldn't open it the rest of the way, and spoke low so the kids wouldn't hear.

"You didn't lie to him," she said. "Everybody lies to him. I lie to him." She held up her right hand. Across the backs of two fingers there was a line of small dark bruises, in a row, where stones had pressed. "So here. For your investigation. She never let go of my hand. Not once, start to finish. She held on so hard her rings cut into me. Four readings. The first one was free. Three at three-fifty. That's ten-fifty, borrowed against the boat. The last one was Friday night — after you, after the wharf. She called me at nine o'clock to get me ready. She said Saturday was going to be something special. Just for me." Her mouth twisted. "And it was."

She let go of the door. Archie went out onto the step, and she shut it behind him, not hard. He heard the lock turn.

He stood beside the pumpkin with its fearless triangles. The sun was nearly down.

After a minute the door opened again, and Hollis came out onto the step, pulling it to behind her. She had her car keys in her hand, and an empty oven mitt.

"Rolls," she said. "In the car. I forgot the rolls."

"Hollis, I didn't mean—"

"I know you didn't." She looked up at him. "She'll forgive you, you know. She was always a good girl." She considered it. "Not this week, though. Possibly not this month. Go home, Archie, and eat something."

She went down the path to her car. Upstairs, behind a lit window, the children were laughing about something. Archie listened until they stopped, and then he went home.
