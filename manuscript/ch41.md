# Chapter 41

## Keys

At half past ten on Saturday morning Tess Calloway's gray Interceptor was parked in the dooryard at Fairweather House, exactly between two ruts, with the engine running.

She had come straight from Rockland. "Ivy Crane walked out at ten," she said, when he got in. "I dropped her at the Spindrift for her things." She pulled out onto Shore Road. "Hollis made her statement at eight. She's asking for you. Her lawyer's furious and she doesn't care."

Archie had a fever of a hundred, a cough, and Hollis's ring of keys hooked on his belt under his buffalo-check coat. Neither of them said anything else all the way to Pen Bay.

There was a state trooper on a chair outside room 214, reading a paperback. He stood up when he saw Tess.

"Twenty minutes," Tess said to Archie, and sat down in the trooper's chair. "And if her lawyer comes up those stairs, I wasn't here."

The room was small and bright, with a window over the parking lot and a long gray stripe of Penobscot Bay beyond it. Hollis was sitting up in bed in a hospital gown and somebody's blue cardigan, with an IV in the back of one hand. Somebody had brushed her hair.

She looked at him over the top of her reading glasses.

"Sit up straight," she said. "You look like you were dragged through a hedge backward."

"I was dragged through a harbor."

"That's no excuse for slouching. Sit. You've got a fever. I can see it from here."

He sat.

"How's June?" said Hollis.

"Mad at me."

"Good. She should be. Was she good, last night? Before you came up through the floor like a drowned rat?"

"She was wonderful."

"I knew she would be. She has your hands and her own head." Hollis looked out at the bay. "I'd have liked to see it." She folded her hands on the blanket. "Go on. Ask me. I've told Tess and a man from Augusta and a lawyer who says I'm making a terrible mistake, and I'll tell you, and then I'm done telling it."

"I know about Peter," Archie said. "I read her books. I know about Danny."

"Then you know the part that matters." She nodded once. "So I'll tell you the rest. I wrote to that producer in May to *expose* her. I want you to know that. In the spring she did a fisherman's widow in Gloucester on the television — the same horn, the same *it's warm here* — and I sat down that night and wrote to Los Angeles about Gus's theater and Gus's anniversary. I was going to stand up on her own camera with Peter's notebook and read it out. Every word."

"And then I came to town."

"And then you came to town, and I thought, oh, *better*. Archie Fairweather will catch her red-handed in front of the whole country." Her mouth tightened. "In July the town signed their contract. Two weeks after that, Shannon came to my kitchen and told me a woman from Lorelei Crane's show had found her on the computer and offered her a free reading. They'd been through the obituaries, looking for sitters." She looked at him. "My letter brought that woman to Shannon's door, Archie. I did that."

Archie didn't say anything.

"In September Shannon came for coffee. She'd had another reading. She sat at my table and told me, so happy, that Tommy had said he missed her on the water." Hollis's voice didn't change. "That's how it starts. That's exactly how it started with Peter. I gave her the whole unit — sixteen years I taught it, and nobody ever asked me why I'd added it. I gave her Gus's book. And she said, *Mrs. Tibbetts, I know it's probably not real, but it's the only time I get to hear his voice*, and she paid that woman another three hundred and fifty dollars." She looked at her hands. "You can't debunk a widow. Exposing her wouldn't have stopped Shannon. It wouldn't have stopped Peter. It would have made her famous for a week."

"So you dug up the garden."

"So I dug up the garden. I thought it would look like her heart. A woman her age, in the dark, at a séance — her fans would have said the spirits took her. I didn't think anybody in Merrow would know what a numb mouth meant. On Sunday morning I was unlocking the radiators and heard Tess say *wolfsbane* to you, and I went down to the green room and was sick in the sink."

"I watched you," Archie said. "From the back of the hall, with Dale, talking about a riverboat. I watched you set out the glasses and slide the horn. I was looking right at you. And I still don't know how you did it."

Hollis reached over with her free hand and took the lunch menu off the tray table.

"Pass me that cup."

He passed her the plastic water cup. She rolled the menu into a cone, quickly, with the narrow end tight, and set it on the tray table, and stood the cup a few inches from it.

"That's the candle," she said, tapping the cup. "I put it a hand from the horn on purpose. Thirty years I've lit that stage; I know what a flame does to polished tin on a camera. On Thursday, at the walkthrough, I watched that boy with the handheld flare on it twice. So on Saturday I set the candle where it would flare, and I waited for somebody to ask the woman with the matches to move the horn." She shrugged. "If Teddy hadn't, I'd have moved it myself. For fire safety. Nobody argues with fire safety."

"The gloves."

"Black cotton, out of the crew's box. And under the right one, a nitrile finger cot from the costume shop. You can't feel it through the cotton." She held up her left hand flat, palm up. "The tray. And under the tray, in the same hand, a tin about the size of a quarter. In the wings, I put one finger in it. Once." She picked up the paper cone with her right hand, the way anybody picks up a funnel, her forefinger hooked into the narrow end, and moved it six inches across the tray table, and set it down. "One second. Less. Then the gloves and the cot went in my cardigan pocket, and I went up to the booth and watched that table until the lights went out. If anybody else had picked it up, I'd have brought the work lights up and called a fire hold."

Archie looked at the paper cone on the tray table.

He had spent thirty years watching hands, and he felt what he always felt at a clean piece of work — the small, helpless lift in the chest — and under it, at the same moment, something cold, because the clean piece of work was a woman who had died a week ago, downstairs in this same building.

"It was the best trick I've ever seen," he said. "And I never saw it."

"Nobody looks at the stagehand, dear. Gus taught me that, in that very building. I was twenty-nine. And everybody said nobody fools Archie Fairweather. It was on the posters for your challenge. I used to like that."

"Well," said Archie. "You did."

"I did." She didn't sound proud. She didn't sound sorry, either. "I'd rather it had been anybody else."

"Why the bell, Hollis?"

She was quiet for a while.

"At a quarter to seven Dale's girl took a phone call at the back of the hall and looked straight up at my window. I taught her to read. I can read her. And there was a cruiser on Main Street with its lights off when I came in." She took off her glasses and folded them. "So they'd been through my house. And I could not stand to be walked out of Gus's theater in front of Shannon and those children. I thought I'd go and tell Peter first." She put the glasses on the blanket. "That's all I thought."

He waited. She didn't say anything else about it.

"Ivy," he said.

Her face changed then, for the first time.

"I told myself it was the scarf. A fine, a night in jail, back in California by the weekend. I told myself that for two days. Then on Thursday they said *murder*, and I came home from your rehearsal and wrote it all out, and I've never in my life been so ashamed of anything as I am of those two days." She looked at him. "Tell her that. Will you? I'd like her to hear it from somebody."

"I'll tell her."

"Would you do it again?" Archie said.

Hollis didn't answer for a long time.

"I'd like to tell you I wouldn't," she said finally. "I'm a schoolteacher. I know what the right answer is. I've graded it." She turned her head on the pillow. "Ask me in a year."

He nodded.

She patted his wrist, twice. Then, in the clipboard voice: "Now listen. I have three things for you, and then you're going to go and take something for that fever. One. The Players. Vivienne runs the costume shop already; she can run the rest. *Our Town* in March, and don't let them talk her into a musical. Two. Rosie. I'm her godmother. Somebody's got to do it properly now, from closer than Knox County. I'd like it to be your mother, if Shannon will have her. Ask Shannon, not Vivienne. Vivienne will say yes before she's finished hearing the question."

"And three?"

"Malvolio. Dale says he went in your front door like he owned it. He'll eat anything except fish. He'll sleep on your chest if you let him and on your face if you don't. Keep him, Archie."

"I'll keep him."

"Good." She let her head go back against the pillow and closed her eyes.

He stood up carefully, so the chair wouldn't scrape.

"Archie."

Her eyes were open again, on the ceiling.

"One more thing. Something I've wondered about for thirty-seven years, and I'd like to give it to somebody who'll wonder about it properly." She turned her head. "Gus's last night. I was in the booth; I ran the board for the Chair. Afterward he went up the center aisle in that cloak, shaking hands, and at the back door Walt Calloway was standing where he always stood, in his uniform, in case. And Gus stopped in front of him, and looked at him, and winked."

Archie didn't move.

"Just winked," said Hollis. "And went out, and walked down to the harbor, and we never saw him again." She closed her eyes. "I always wondered what that was about."

At the door the trooper was standing, and Tess was standing, and the twenty minutes were up.

He walked out to the car in the cold bright sun with Hollis's keys knocking against his hip. Tess started the engine and looked at him.

"Home," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The Galley," said Archie. "She gave me some things to deliver."
