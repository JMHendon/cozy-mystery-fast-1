# Chapter 41

## Daylight

On Saturday morning the fog burned off by nine, and by eleven the Galley was so full that people were eating standing up at the pie case.

Archie had slept for nine hours in his clothes on top of the covers, with a cat he didn't own lying on his chest — Hollis's Malvolio, who had been brought up from Pudding Lane at midnight by Dale Pinkham in a cardboard box, on Hollis's instructions relayed by Tess from the hospital, and who had walked into Fairweather House as if he'd been expected. Archie had woken with a cough, a fever of a hundred, every muscle in his body informing him of its existence, and a mild but unmistakable conviction that he'd been run over by a riverboat.

He'd come to the Galley anyway, because there were people he owed.

When he came in, the bell over the door rang, and the room went quiet the way it had on Friday a week ago — heads lifting, forks slowing. Then somebody at the counter started clapping, and somebody else did, and Bev Thibodeau said "*Oh*, for heaven's sake, let the man sit down," and it stopped, and they let him sit down.

The liars' table had a chair pulled out.

Archie looked at it. He looked at Elwin Coombs, and Dickie Thibodeau, and Lionel, who was eating eggs and did not look up. Elwin pushed the chair another three inches out from the table with his boot.

Archie sat in it. Nobody said anything. Bev put a coffee in front of him and a piece of blueberry pie, at eleven in the morning, without being asked.

"I didn't order pie."

"You're eating pie," said Bev.

He ate the pie.

---

Ivy Crane came in at half past eleven, with Tess Calloway one step behind her.

Ivy was in the same oversized gray sweater she'd been arrested in, and she was paler than ever, and she had a manila envelope from the Knox County Jail property office under one arm. A bail commissioner had come in at eight and released her on her own recognizance on the remaining charge, the one about the scarf. The attorney general's office would move on Monday to dismiss the rest. Tess had driven her up from Rockland herself, and was standing just inside the door now with her hands in her coat pockets, the way she'd stood at the back of the opera house.

Ivy looked around the full diner. Her eyes found Archie at the liars' table and went past him, and found Shannon Mayo behind the counter with a coffee pot, and stopped.

She walked down the length of the counter. The room watched her do it.

"Mrs. Mayo," said Ivy. "I'm Ivy Crane."

Shannon put the coffee pot down on the warmer.

Ivy took a check out of the pocket of her sweater and laid it on the counter between them. It was for one thousand and fifty dollars. "That's what you paid," she said. "For the readings. I did the research for them. I found Tommy's obituary, and the boat's name, and your Facebook page, and the pictures of your kids." She didn't look away. "Everything my mother told you about your husband, I gave her. None of it came from him. I'm sorry."

Shannon looked at the check. She didn't pick it up.

"I'm going to write to everyone," Ivy said. "Everyone who ever sat with her. There are thirty books. I'm going to give them all back their money out of hers and tell them the truth. It's going to take a long time." She took a breath. "I wanted you to be first. Because of the — because it was here."

Shannon still didn't pick up the check. When she spoke, her voice was very quiet.

"Hollis did it for me," she said. "That's what they're saying. All of it. Because of what that woman told me on the phone."

Ivy didn't answer. There wasn't an answer.

Shannon looked down at the check for a long time. Then she folded it in half and put it in the pocket of her apron, and picked up the coffee pot again, and went down the counter to refill the man at the end, who didn't need it. Her hand was shaking. She didn't spill a drop.

Ivy turned around. For the first time since Thursday in the jail, she looked straight at Archie.

"Margaret Okafor says you're paying her," she said.

"I am."

"I told you not to help me."

"You did," Archie agreed.

Ivy looked at him a moment longer. Then something happened to her face that he had never seen it do: it moved toward a smile, and didn't quite get there, and stayed, halfway, as if it had forgotten the rest of the way.

"Vermont," she said. "In January. Vet tech school. I'm going to clean out kennels and get bitten by Labradors." She shrugged. "Nobody's going to hold anybody's hand in the dark."

"Sounds wonderful," said Archie, and meant it so much that she looked faintly startled, and went back to Tess at the door.

---

Shannon came to the liars' table twenty minutes later with the coffee pot, and refilled his cup, and stood there.

He didn't know what to say to her. He'd promised he wouldn't humiliate her, and then he'd sat in the dark and held hands while her husband's voice came out of a tin horn, and then he'd turned on the lights on all of it, and her children's godmother was in a hospital room in Rockport with a trooper at the door.

"I'm not going to forgive you," Shannon said. "I want you to know that. Not for a while."

"That's fair."

"I'm not saying it's fair. I'm saying it's how it is." She looked out the window at the harbor, where the moorings sat bright in the cold sun. "Liam wants to know if you'll teach him the card one. The one you did for him on Sunday."

Archie looked at her.

"I said I'd ask," said Shannon, and went back to the counter.

---

Warren Gilley came in at noon, alone, in his camel coat and his bow tie, and stopped dead in the doorway when he saw Archie at the liars' table.

Archie got up. It hurt. He went over to him before Warren could leave, and said, low, so that only Warren and possibly Bev could hear it, that he was sorry — not for the Galley, which he could never take back, but for every word of it, and that there was no excuse, and he wasn't going to offer one.

Warren listened to all of it with his face set like plaster.

"Noted," he said.

Archie held out the cardboard tube he'd carried in under his arm. It had come by overnight courier on Thursday, before any of the rest of it. Inside, rolled in tissue, were two posters, seventy years old, in red and black and gold: THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER WALKS THROUGH WALLS! and RADIO'S MASTER OF MYSTERY. A man in Philadelphia and a woman in Connecticut had bought them in September on the internet. They'd been very reasonable, in the end.

"These are from my mother," said Archie. "And from me. They were Gus's, and they're hers to give, and she wants Carol to have them. For her room. My mother says Carol used to sit in the front row every Halloween when she was a girl, with her hair in braids. She says she remembers her."

Warren looked at the tube in Archie's hands for a long time. He didn't take it.

"I won't take anything from you," he said.

"They're not for you," said Archie. "They're for Carol."

Something went across Warren Gilley's face. He reached out and took the tube. He held it in both hands against the front of his good coat, carefully, the way Carol had held the queen of hearts.

"For Carol," he said. Then he turned and went out without his coffee, and the bell rang behind him.

---

At half past twelve the liars' table rose as one, the way it always did, and put on its caps.

Elwin Coombs stopped beside Archie's chair. He looked down at him, and over at Dickie, and at Lionel, who nodded, very slightly.

"That trick," said Elwin. "The chair. Last night."

"Yes?"

"How'd you do it?"

The whole Galley went quiet again. Archie looked up at the three of them — at Elwin's weathered red face, at Dickie's suspenders, at Lionel, who had stood up in front of the whole town last night and told them his name — and he thought about a crawlspace forty feet long and eighteen inches high, and a girl's voice coming through the floorboards, and an old man's hand gripping his arm in the dark.

"I'll never tell you," Archie said. "Not as long as I live."

Elwin considered that for a moment. Then he nodded, once, slowly, with deep satisfaction.

"Correct answer," said Elwin Coombs, and the liars' table went out into the sunshine.

Archie was still looking at the door when Tess Calloway sat down in Elwin's empty chair.

"Hollis is asking for you," she said. "She's at Pen Bay. She's made her statement. Her lawyer's furious and she doesn't care." Tess looked at him across the table, at the pie plate and the fever in his face. "Can you stand up?"

"Probably."

"Then come on," said Tess. "I'll drive."
