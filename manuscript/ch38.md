# Chapter 38

## The Bell Buoy

Ozzie Lund had the engine running.

The harbormaster's boat was a twenty-one-foot aluminum workboat with a little wheelhouse, a blue light on a mast, and HARBORMASTER — MERROW stenciled in white down the side. Ozzie stood at the wheel at the bottom of the fuel-dock ladder in a float coat and a watch cap, with the spotlight already lit and swinging. He was seventy and slow-talking, and he had courted Archie's mother since September with a thermos of coffee and a seriousness Archie had found hilarious right up until this moment, when he came down the ladder behind Tess and Ozzie put out one big hand and steadied him onto the deck without a word.

"Vivienne called," Ozzie said, and shoved the throttle forward.

The boat came off the dock with its bow up and its stern dug in, and the wharf lights went away behind them into the fog in about four seconds.

Then there was nothing.

Archie had been out on this harbor at dawn on Monday in Dale Pinkham's boat, with the light coming up gold and Merrow Light standing white on its point and every mooring visible for a mile. It was a different place now. The spotlight on the wheelhouse roof made a white cone in front of them, full of swirling wet gray, and showed them nothing but more of it. Beyond the cone there was no harbor and no town. There was the throb of the engine and the slap of the hull and, out ahead somewhere, the bell.

*Clang.*

The swell had come up while they were inside the opera house. The boat climbed it and fell off the back, climbed and fell. Archie held the edge of the wheelhouse with both hands and felt the old seasickness rise and told it, firmly, that he did not have time.

Tess was on her radio. "Marine Patrol, Calloway. Outbound past the moorings, heading for the bell off Shag. Visibility maybe fifty feet." She listened. "Copy." She lowered the radio. "Coast Guard's coming out of Rockland. Twenty minutes in this."

"Ayuh," said Ozzie. "Won't need twenty."

*Clang.* Closer.

Archie stood braced in the stern in Gus's opera cloak and his tailcoat and his good shoes, with salt water coming over the side in sheets and soaking him to the knees. He was thinking about an old wooden skiff, thirty-seven years ago, drifting near Merrow Light at dawn with nobody in it and a black opera cloak folded on the seat.

"There," said Ozzie, and swung the spotlight.

The bell buoy loomed out of the fog: a rust-red iron cage on a float, taller than a man, rolling heavily, its bell clanging inside it. Beyond it, so close that Archie's stomach turned over for a different reason, a long black hump of rock rose out of the water with white foam breaking over it and the swell sucking back off it with a sound like a breath. Shag Ledge.

And between the bell and the ledge, rising and falling, a small open skiff with its outboard tipped up out of the water.

Hollis was sitting in the stern.

She was in her men's cardigan and the old felt hat she'd worn in the garden, with no life jacket, sitting upright with her hands folded in her lap, facing the ledge. When the spotlight hit her she didn't turn around. She lifted one hand against the glare and then put it back in her lap.

Ozzie throttled down and brought the workboat around in a wide, careful arc until it lay off the skiff's side, between her and open water, about twenty feet away, rolling. He kept the engine just ticking over. He had been pulling people off this ledge for forty years.

"Hollis," said Archie.

She turned her head then and looked at him across the dark water, and he saw her see the cloak.

"Oh, Archie," she said. "You were supposed to be doing the second half."

"June's doing it."

Her mouth tightened, and then eased. "Is she," said Hollis softly. "Good for June."

The swell lifted the skiff and set it down. Each time, it came down a little closer to the rocks.

"I don't have a trick," Archie said. He had to raise his voice over the bell and the engine and the sea, and he hated that; he wanted to say this quietly. "I want you to know that. I've been trying to think of one the whole way out here and I don't have one. I've just got me."

"I know, dear. You always have." She looked back toward the ledge. "That's the trouble."

"Hollis, come on board."

"Do you know what she said to me?" Hollis was still looking at the rocks. "On Thursday. The first day. I took her around the building — the stairs, the green room, the booth. I showed her Gus's trapdoor. And at the end of it she put her hand on my arm, and she smiled at me, and she said, *Honey, where's the ladies' room?*" Her voice didn't change at all. "She'd sat across a table from my husband forty-one times. She'd talked to him in my son's voice. She'd taken our house. And she stood in Gus's theater and looked me in the face and asked me where the ladies' room was." She was quiet a moment. "She didn't remember the town, Archie. She didn't remember *any* of us."

Archie held on to the wheelhouse. The boat rolled. Behind him Tess was on the radio, low, giving their position.

He thought about a hatbox on a kitchen table at two o'clock in the morning, and thirty small books in cramped private shorthand, and his daughter's finger moving down a page.

"She remembered Peter," he said.

Hollis turned her head.

"She wrote him down," Archie said. "In her book. She kept a book for every year — every sitter, every Thursday, every dollar. Ivy kept them all. I read them last night." He made himself keep his voice steady. "November 2003. There's an entry. *P.L. lost at sea. Saw it in the paper.* And under that, in the same handwriting, it says, *God forgive me.*" He swallowed salt. "And then she put the trumpet away. She didn't use it for a private sitting again for three years. Her fans have a name for it. She remembered him, Hollis. She remembered Peter."

Hollis didn't move.

Then her shoulders came down. Her folded hands came apart in her lap. She looked at him across twenty feet of black water with a face he had never seen on her in nine weeks — a face with no teacher left in it at all.

"Oh," said Hollis Tibbetts. "Oh, Peter."

She stood up in the skiff.

"Hollis, *sit down*—" That was Ozzie, sharp, the first time Archie had ever heard him raise his voice. He had the orange ring buoy off its bracket, and he threw it, a hard low throw that landed right against the skiff's side with its line paying out from the cleat.

Hollis looked down at it. She didn't reach for it.

And then the swell came.

It was bigger than the others. Archie saw it come out of the fog behind the skiff, a long black shoulder of water, and the skiff rose with it, and rose, with Hollis standing in the stern holding nothing, and at the top of it the skiff swung broadside and the sea dropped out from under it and set it down hard on the edge of the ledge with a crack that carried over the engine.

The skiff went over on its side. Hollis went into the water.

She went under at once. Archie saw the felt hat floating, and the white wash breaking over the rock, and nothing else.

His hands had the silver clasp of the opera cloak open before he knew they'd moved. They had the cloak off his shoulders and folded — lining out, shoulders in, thirds, collar on top, the clasp centered — and laid down on the seat beside the wheel, and he was kicking off one good shoe and then the other.

"Archie—" Tess, behind him. "Don't."

He looked at her. She had the radio in one hand and her other hand out toward him. She could see what he was going to do. So could he.

He decided.

"Line," he said to Ozzie. "Around my waist. I don't go in without a line. Never have."

Ozzie looked at him for one second. Then he had the end of a coil of rope around Archie's middle and knotted in a bowline in about the time it took to say it, and he braced his boots against the gunwale and took the strain.

Archie went over the side.

The cold was the worst thing he had ever felt.

He'd known it would be. He had done ice-water tanks for television; he knew that the first thing cold water does is try to make you breathe, and if your head is under when it happens, you breathe in the sea. None of that had been forty-seven years old in fifty-two-degree water at night in a swell.

He came up gasping anyway, the cold like a band of iron around his ribs, and got his breath back by main force — *slow, slow, slow* — and kicked toward the hat.

She came up beside the rock, an arm's length from him, her white hair plastered to her head.

He got hold of her cardigan. He got an arm around her.

And for ten seconds — he counted them; he always counted — she fought him.

It wasn't much. She was sixty-six and the cold had her too. But she twisted in his arm and pushed at him with both hands against his chest, pushing him away, toward the boat — and he understood, with the sea in his mouth and the ledge looming, that she was not fighting to stay. She was trying to make *him* go. She was pushing him back to the line, and the boat, and his daughter in a blue velvet jacket on a stage, and she was going to keep pushing until one of them let go.

"*Hollis,*" he said into her ear, with what breath he had. "Hold on. I'm not letting go. So you have to."

The swell lifted them both.

Five more seconds. He felt her stop.

Then her hands, which had been pushing against his chest, closed into fists in the wet front of his tailcoat, and held on.

"Pull!" Archie shouted, to nobody, to the fog, and the line around his waist went bar-tight and dragged them both back across the black water toward the light, and a hand came down out of the glare and closed hard on the collar of his coat, and held, and did not let go — and he looked up through salt and fog and saw Tess Calloway leaning half out over the gunwale with her braid in the sea and her teeth gritted, hauling.
