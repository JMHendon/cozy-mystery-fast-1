# Chapter 10

## Pancakes

The plan was pancakes shaped like playing cards.

Archie had worked it out at four in the morning, lying awake listening to the bell buoy out past Shag Ledge, which meant fog. A normal Sunday. A family at a kitchen table, being ordinary at one another. If breakfast happened to be a full deck with blueberry pips, nobody had ever said ordinary had to be boring.

By seven the fog was pressed white against the kitchen windows, and the results were on a platter in the middle of the farm table. They did not look like playing cards. Pancake batter, it turned out, had no respect for corners. Every rectangle he poured relaxed at once into something rounder and more opinionated. The three and four of clubs had fused into a single lumpy continent. The queen of hearts looked like Ohio.

Harold watched from his bucket in the sink. Somebody had given him fresh seawater before anyone was up — there were wet footprints on the porch steps — and June had denied it.

"What is that," said June, from the doorway.

She was in pajama pants and a gray sweatshirt that said PENOBSCOT REGIONAL DEBATE, with her hair down, which meant it was early and she hadn't decided yet how to feel about the day.

"The king of spades."

"It's a potato."

"It's a king of spades in a transitional phase." "Sit. Eat. Normal people talk about the weather on Sundays."

June sat. She looked at her father, who had not been to bed and was still in the shirt he'd worn at the hospital, with a dishtowel over one shoulder.

"Okay," she said carefully. "What's the weather?"

"Fog, burning off by ten. Fifty-two." He'd looked it up at four-thirty, in case anybody asked. "Syrup?"

He heard his mother on the back stairs and was waiting at the bottom with a cup of coffee, two sugars and the good cream, when she came round the turn.

Vivienne was dressed. Not in a robe — in a dark green wool suit, a silk blouse, coral lipstick, and a small black hat with a feather in it, pinned at an angle that could have started a war. She took the coffee without breaking stride.

"You're dressed," said Archie.

"It's Sunday. I'm in the choir." She sat at the head of the table and inspected the platter. "What is the queen of hearts doing in Ohio?"

"You don't have to go. I'll call the minister. They can manage one Sunday without an alto."

"Darling, they can't manage one *measure* without an alto. You've heard the tenors." She took something that had been the seven of diamonds and cut it into small squares she didn't eat. "And you can stop looking at me like that. I'm not a soufflé."

He sat down across from her. He had rehearsed this too, at four in the morning: how to keep the night out of the kitchen and away from her. Pancakes, weather, choir.

"I was thinking," he began, "that today we could all just—"

"He called you Sparrow," said Vivienne.

Archie stopped.

His mother laid her fork down and lined it up with the edge of her plate.

"I'd forgotten it," she said. "I lay awake last night trying to remember the last time I heard anybody say it, and I couldn't. Some summer. Eighty-six, eighty-seven. You'd been at the cookie jar, and he said, *That sparrow's been at the jar again.*" "It was his. Nobody else called you that. Not me. Certainly not your father. Only Dad." She raised her eyes. "So how did that woman know?"

"Research." It came out too fast, and he slowed down. "Mother, it's always research. An old interview in the *Packet*. Some magazine in nineteen-seventy-something — *The Great Fairweather at Home*, a photograph of me on his knee, a caption. She had a researcher. Ivy does everything."

"Then show it to me."

"Show you—"

"The interview. The magazine. If it's written down somewhere, you'll find it. You find everything." She picked up her coffee. "Until then, I heard my father say your name in a dark room last night, and I'd thank you not to tell me I didn't."

The griddle ticked as it cooled. A half-dollar had come out of Archie's pocket and was walking across the backs of his fingers, knuckle to knuckle, index to pinky and under and back. He didn't remember deciding to do it. He never did.

"You're doing the coin," said June.

He closed his hand on it.

The back door opened on a breath of fog, and Lenny came in from the carriage house in all four sweaters and a watch cap. He looked at the platter.

"Those are cards," Archie told him.

"They're pancakes." Lenny rolled one up and ate half of it standing at the counter. "Dale called the shop phone at six. Said you weren't answering. He said the State Police took the table."

"They'll have taken the trumpet. Lenny, it was the trumpet. Two hands loose in that circle, and one of them was hers. That's how it flew."

"Rod or a hand," said Lenny. "You showed the whole diner."

"A hand. Her left — Shannon had her right. She put the small end to her mouth to do the voices, and something on it hurt her. Her mouth went numb. She *said* so. Then the voice went thick, and then her heart. That's not a heart attack. That's something on the horn."

"Grandma said you gave her tea," said June.

Archie turned.

"I'm just saying Grandma said it. Last night, to Ozzie."

"Chamomile. Out of Bev's thermos. I drank the same tea, Junebug, out of the same thermos — the whole cup — and I'm sitting here making pancakes." He spread his hands. "It wasn't the tea. It was the trumpet."

June put down her fork and lined it up with the plate. Archie knew exactly where she'd learned that.

"Okay. Premise. Somebody put poison on the trumpet."

"Yes."

"Then they had to know two things. One, she was a fake — she'd have a hand loose and pick it up herself. Two, she'd put her mouth on the small end."

"Exactly, that's—"

"I'm not done." One finger went up, and he closed his mouth. He had watched her do this to a boy from Exeter in a regional final, and the boy had gone home and taken up the cello. "Who knows that? Everybody who's read Gus's book, which half the town has had out of the library. Everybody who took Mrs. Tibbetts's senior English, which is everybody in Merrow under about sixty. Mattie can do the whole chapter. In voices." Second finger. "Everybody in the Galley on Friday, because you stood up and taught them." Third finger. "And everybody on that stage last night who watched you pick it up, look in it, and put your mouth on it. On camera."

"It was a raspberry."

"And then you're the one who told the police what poison it was. Before anybody even knew it was poison." She picked her fork back up. "I'm just making the list. But if you're right, Dad, it's a really long list. And you're at the top of it."

Lenny took another pancake.

"That," said Archie, "was beautiful."

"It was a list." June pushed a blueberry around her plate. "So let the police do it. That's what they're for. You promised me a regular year, and regular people don't put themselves at the top of murder lists."

"Nobody said murder."

"You did. With your face. All night." She didn't look up. "I heard you come in at one. You went up to the turret. I heard the dials."

He hadn't known she'd heard.

The right thing was in plain sight. He could say *okay*, the way he had in the library on Friday, and let Tess Calloway, who seemed to him the most competent person he'd met in Maine, do her job. He could keep his whole family in this kitchen with the fog against the glass, where nothing could get at them.

He looked at his mother. She was sitting very straight in her war hat with the cup in both hands. She was waiting, the way she'd waited in the wings for fifty years — for somebody to tell her how a dead woman knew her father's name for her son.

*Watch closely, Sparrow.* A screen door. A silver dollar. He'd been four.

"I'm going to find out," Archie said.

June shut her eyes.

"No cameras," he said quickly. "No show. No circus. Quietly. You won't even know I'm doing it."

"Dad. You can't do anything quietly. You drive a *hearse*."

"I inherited a hearse."

"You had it painted."

"I had it *restored*," said Lenny. "There's a difference." He rinsed his fingers at the sink, on the side away from Harold, and went out.

Vivienne set down her cup. She didn't thank him, and he didn't need her to. She reached across the corner of the table and straightened the dishtowel on his shoulder, as if he were about to go on, and stood up and pulled on her gloves.

"Ozzie's taking me to church," she said. "And Archibald — Bev's thermos is still rolling around in the back of the hearse. Take it back to the Galley today, before she sends somebody. She counts them."

"Today. I promise."

"You promise a great many things." She kissed June on the top of the head and, after a moment's thought, kissed Archie on his. "And wear a coat. That poor girl still has yours."

She went out in a breath of perfume and fog.

June was looking at him.

"What?"

"Nothing." She cut the king of spades in half. "Quietly."

The phone on the kitchen wall rang. It was Gus's yellow rotary, which rang loud enough to empty a school, and nobody called it except Hollis and the oil company.

Archie picked it up.

"Mr. Fairweather. Tess Calloway."

"Sergeant. Good morning. We're having pancakes."

There was a short silence on the line, in which a decision not to ask was clearly made.

"I'm at the opera house with Chief Pinkham and an evidence technician," she said. "Can you be here at ten."

"May I ask why?"

"You told me last night you know how these things are done. I'd like to know what I'm looking at before I bag it." A pause. "Ten o'clock. Don't touch anything when you get here."

She hung up. Archie stood holding the yellow receiver and felt the grin arrive before he could do a thing about it.

"Dad," said June. "Your face."

He hung up the phone and took the dishtowel off his shoulder.

"That," he said, "was the State Police. Asking for my help."

"That's not what that was," said June.
