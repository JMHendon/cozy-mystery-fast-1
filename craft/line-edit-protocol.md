# Line-Edit Protocol — sentence-level clarity

Run sentence by sentence. Each rule names a habit drafted prose (human or machine) commonly has; each has a mechanical test. The pass changes sentences, never events, lines' content, or beats. **Run it last** — line-editing before structural or energy work polishes sentences that pass will replace. **The lead's voice is not a tic.** Read the lead-voice guard below before cutting anything the lead says.

## The clarity tests

**1. The gnomic weld.** *Habit:* a past-tense action clause joined by `because`, a semicolon, or a relative to a present-tense aphorism, so the tense border falls mid-sentence ("…which ended the service, because there is no arguing with a woman who has already started carrying things").
**Rule:** an aphorism gets its own sentence. Full stop at the tense border; the aphorism may stand alone after it. And ration narrator aphorisms: keep the best one in any run of three and cut or convert the others to action. Aphorisms in the lead's own dialogue are voice, not narration; judge them under the lead-voice guard.

**2. Broad-reference "which" carrying a plot event.** *Habit:* `which` attaches to a whole clause and is then asked to advance the action or land the joke ("which ended the service," "which achieved a second failure").
**Rule:** a which-clause may comment; it may not act. If it contains an event verb (*ended, achieved, stood*), promote it to its own sentence with a named subject. ("…which was how he announced that the car was waiting" passes: *was* comments.)

**3. Pronoun across an interrupter.** *Habit:* `it/one/them` separated from its antecedent by a participial or parenthetical phrase, landing on the wrong noun ("came in with a clean blouse over her arm, having lost this argument in advance…, and put it into the case" — *it* lands on *argument*).
**Rule:** after any interrupting phrase of six or more words, repeat the noun. No pronoun with a competing noun of matching number between it and its antecedent.
**Same-gender pronouns.** When two or more people of the same gender are in the sentence (or were in the one before), *her/his/she/he* stops pointing anywhere. Name the person instead. "Lorelei Crane across from him, her daughter at her left elbow, and at her right Shannon Mayo" has three women and three *her*s; write "Lorelei Crane sat across from Archie, with her daughter, Ivy, on her left. On Lorelei's right sat Shannon Mayo."

**4. The overloaded suspension.** *Habit:* three or more finite verbs plus an em-dash insertion or embedded quotation in one sentence ("rose mid-sentence — '…' — gathered the vase, carried it out, came back, sat, and picked the sentence up").
**Rule:** maximum two finite verbs when the sentence also carries a quotation or a dash-insertion. Split at the third verb.

**5. Reified abstractions whose verb collides with idiom.** *Habit:* remarks, grins, silences, hurt handled as physical objects — fine once, fatal when the chosen verb already means something else ("whatever came down the line **stood him up** mid-sentence"; a grin that "landed like a tipped hat" — a hat neither lands nor arrives).
**Rule:** one reified abstraction per paragraph. Before keeping the verb, test: does verb + object already form a fixed idiom with a different meaning? If yes, change the verb. If a simile's vehicle cannot literally perform the main verb, change one or the other.
*Scope note:* the **abstract-subject + elegant-human-verb** construction ("relief reached the surface," "his attention arrived complete") is this same family at chapter scale — each instance may be fine; the *density* is the defect. Rule 5 fires on idiom collisions sentence by sentence; density and clustering belong to the structural/register review. Don't double-flag: if a sentence is clean but the chapter is saturated, that's a register finding, not a line edit.

**6. Inverted temporal joints.** *Habit:* `when/before/so` clauses whose subordinate event is really the purpose or consequence of the main clause ("They stopped **when** Maggie bought two apples" — the stop enables the buying; a thank-you delivered "instead" to a person who has already left the scene).
**Rule:** the event in a *when*-clause must be datable independently of the main clause. If the main clause enables the subordinate event, recast ("They stopped in a market town, where Maggie bought two apples").

**7. The manner clause.** *Habit:* finishing an action with a generic-actor comparison — "the way X does Y," "in the manner of a woman who…," "with the air of…," "as though…," "like a man who…". A chapter that reads cluttered often runs at five or more per 1,000 words; a clean one at about one.
**Rule:** ration to ≤1 per ~500 words and ≤1 per scene; in a sweep, cut to the budget keeping only clauses whose vehicle ADDS information (physical precision, character knowledge, plot freight). Kill on sight: vehicles that repeat the verb they modify ("the room began to fray the way rooms fray when…") and manner-codas welded to already-complete actions.
*Worked repairs:*
> "…and shut the notebook on it, in the manner of a woman putting away a receipt she intends to dispute later." → "…and shut the notebook on it." *(the gesture already says it)*
> "Maggie took over the side door the way other people take over a lifeboat — dishes logged, names in a column…" → "Maggie took over the side door — dishes logged, names in a column…" *(the specifics ARE the lifeboat)*

**8. Semantic order.** *Habit:* locative inversion ("At a farm track stood a plank…", "Beneath the cash tin lay…"), delayed or missing subjects, a LIST as delayed subject, mismatched parallels yoking nouns to gerunds ("covered the diary and research, but not finding her charger").
**Rule:** in an action sentence, default to person → action → object → consequence. Depart only when the departure buys a real effect, not a more literary sentence. One verb may not organise four unlike things. Give an action its actual subject. This is not a ban on description: a room, garment or light source may be the subject of a plain descriptive sentence, and not every sentence needs an event.

**9. The inference budget.** *Habit:* sentences containing a small riddle — metonymy asked to carry action ("argued with a ladder"), speech-act verbs that read physical first ("she raised the east range"), emotions as abstract-noun pairs ("caught between affront and hope"), causal links supplied only by implication, meaning that arrives only retrospectively. Readability formulas cannot see inference load: a chapter can score at a low grade level and still feel hard.
**Rule:** at most ONE inferential turn per sentence; the next-sentence test — if the reader needs the following sentence to understand this one's ordinary meaning, rewrite this one. Emotions arrive as behaviour ("The girl frowned, then glanced down at the prospectus"), not abstraction-pairs. **When two or more clarity flags land in one paragraph, stop word-patching and RECONSTRUCT the paragraph from its factual content.**

**10. Setup/payoff adjacency.** *Habit:* successive revision passes cut locally and orphan payoffs — "There may be sheep" surviving after "You're leaving ninety minutes early" was cut; a glancing hypothetical ("a charming letter") promoted four lines later into an established routine ("the charming letters").
**Rule:** for every joke, callback, and echo in the draft, verify its setup still exists in THIS draft, close enough to be alive, at matching scale. Also check the cue for every objection, demand, deflection and concession. A reply to a participation requirement cannot rely on a line that now sounds merely like an invitation; conditions after failed resistance must not be moved ahead of that resistance. Locate a historical incident before referring to it as an event both speakers know. An orphaned payoff is cut or re-set-up, never left to be understood in retrospect. This is a comprehension test, not a rule to bring clue and consequence closer together. Mystery emphasis across an interval belongs to the structural review below.

**11. The overloaded sentence.** *Habit:* one sentence tries to carry a whole moment: a list after a colon, an appositive, a *which/who* clause, an opening absolute phrase, and a chain of *and … and … and*. Each piece is grammatical; together the reader loses the subject.
**Rule:** one main action or idea per sentence. Split a narration sentence when it has two or more of these: a list of three or more items; an appositive; a *which/who/whom* clause; an opening absolute or participial phrase ("with the house lights up and two cameras on him, he…"); more than two actions joined by *and*. Put the subject and verb near the front. Turn an opening absolute into a plain clause ("while the house lights blazed and two cameras recorded him") or move it to the end. A narration sentence over about 25 words is a flag to check, not a ban; a long sentence that is one clear line of action can stay. Dialogue may run long when that is how the character talks.
*Worked repairs:*
> "There were seven of them round a small oak table, close enough to bump knees: Lorelei Crane across from him, her daughter at her left elbow, and at her right Shannon Mayo, whom Archie had put there himself because she was the one person at the table he was sure wasn't in on it." → "Seven of them sat around a small oak table, close enough to bump knees. Lorelei Crane sat across from Archie, with her daughter, Ivy, on her left. On Lorelei's right sat Shannon Mayo. Archie had put Shannon there himself, because she was the one person at the table he was sure wasn't in on it."
> "Half an hour ago, with the house lights up and two cameras on him, he had searched everything Lorelei had brought, down to the trumpet itself, a long dented aluminum cone wrapped in luminous bands, which he had looked through, put to his lips at the narrow end and blown a long wet raspberry into." → "Half an hour ago, he had searched everything Lorelei brought while the house lights blazed and two cameras recorded him. He even searched the trumpet itself, a long, dented aluminum cone wrapped in luminous bands. After looking through it, he put his lips to the narrow end and blew a long, wet raspberry into it."

## Description check — paragraph and encounter scale

Check literal comprehension before polishing the adjectives:

- Is the threshold clear, or does “beyond” require a destination the reader has not been shown?
- Can the reader place the selected close-up within a comprehensible room or body? If not, restore the missing frame; do not add another isolated detail.
- Are light source, ambient light, visibility, season and clothing compatible? “Dim” and “flashing” can coexist, but the wording must tell us what is dark and what produces the flashes.
- Do consecutive portraits repeat the same age/hair/face/clothes structure? Change the selection, placement or scale, not merely the opening verb.
- Does a proposed interpretation exceed the visible or established fact? Grooming, colour and wear are not automatic proof of wealth, neglect, age anxiety or private intent.
- Has tightening removed the coherent picture while preserving a scattering of adjectives?

A sentence repair can clarify a source of light or an antecedent. If the scene needs new descriptive material or a different portrait design, flag that for the authorised prose revision rather than silently inventing canon during a copyedit. Protect useful static description and ordinary visual identity; no detail quota or compulsory symbolic meaning.

## Also in the pass (mechanical)

Subject–verb agreement · dialogue tags never comma-spliced to action beats ("She composed herself, 'Where should we begin?'" is an error) · no said-bookisms doing joke work ("'…' Maggie smiled.") · numerals spelled out in dialogue and running prose · terminal punctuation present · dialect, idiom and filler words consistent with each speaker's card and the book's setting · the book's chosen spelling convention (US or UK) applied consistently.

## The worked example

> "She took the fern out under one arm, which ended the service, because there is no arguing with a woman who has already started carrying things."

Fails rules 1, 2, and 5 at once: *took out* has no completion, so "under one arm" arrives as a false destination; "the service" is a metaphor born and put to work inside a subordinate clause; the gnomic weld sits at the third joint of a right-branching chain. The punchline lands winded. Repair:

> She tucked the pot under one arm and carried it out, and that ended the service. There is no arguing with a woman who has already started carrying things.

## Compression calibration — preserve the speaker, not only the instruction

Example: “Fetch them. We're having a banquet” became “Go and get them. We'll make them last.” Both fetch the biscuits. The replacement is longer and turns occasion-making into sensible rationing. This is a loss of character, not improved clarity. These are comparative manuscript examples, not required lines for future scenes.

Before neutralising a flourish, ask whether the reader understands it immediately and whether it belongs to this character's present want. Keep clear, character-specific play; repair genuine ambiguity without removing the invitation, evasion or joke. Check the complete exchange: cutting its setup or its ordinary answering voice can spoil the surviving line. Preserve narrative footing and real emotional breaks. Do not repair this failure by making every speaker equally witty.

## Scope discipline

The pass touches roughly one sentence in four in an unedited draft. If it wants to touch more, the problem is upstream (register or structure) — stop and say so. If a repair changes what happens, who acts, or what a line means, it is not a line edit — flag instead.

**Causal-visibility and reading-time guard:** do not conceal a mechanism by replacing a clear object with "something," making handling physically vague, suppressing known facts, or adding an unsupported substance, action or alternative culprit. Nor does "a quarter of an hour later" fix two conspicuously adjacent actions in the reader's experience. Flag cumulative emphasis across the relevant scenes/chapters for structural review. When tightening, check that the cut has not removed the interesting human event and left only a setup immediately followed by its suspicious consequence. Protect engaging character interaction as well as explicit clue facts; line editing does not assign those facts greater priority because they are easy to verify.

**Character-conduct guard:** adding “may I,” changing a decision into a request, softening an expectation into a hope, supplying grateful explanation or cutting evasion into immediate acceptance changes character and power. It is not a courtesy polish. Compare the relevant card and local relationship state; flag a needed behavioural correction upstream. Do not make a scene easier to read by making its people easier to deal with.

**Emotional-timing guard:** do not insert a penitent explanation, silence a defensive joke to make the lead nicer, or turn a small apology into full self-recognition. Do not remove an earned acknowledgement simply to make the chapter lighter. Changing what the lead understands, whether she repairs a hurt or how a relationship responds is voice/structure work to flag, not a sentence repair.

**Lead-voice guard:** the lead's spoken wit, theories, boasts, patter and delight are the product, not a prose tic. Tic caps (manner clauses, aphorisms, cappers, exit lines, repeated phrases) apply to the narration and the supporting cast; inside the lead's dialogue, cut only a genuine repetition, an unclear line or a line the lead card says they would not say. Never fix a narrator habit by cutting the lead's line beside it. Never move a joke from the lead's mouth into the narration. If a whole-book cap (for example "no more than one chapter in three ends on a quip") would remove the lead's best lines, flag it upstream instead of applying it. After the pass, confirm the lead still has the most and best lines in each chapter (`cozy-revision-protocol.md`, "Lead-presence check").
