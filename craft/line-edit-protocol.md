# Line-Edit Protocol — sentence-level clarity

Run sentence by sentence. Each rule names a habit this project's prose actually has; each has a mechanical test. The pass changes sentences, never events, lines' content, or beats. **Run it last** — line-editing before structural or energy work polishes sentences that pass will replace.

## The clarity tests

**1. The gnomic weld.** *Habit:* a past-tense action clause joined by `because`, a semicolon, or a relative to a present-tense aphorism, so the tense border falls mid-sentence ("…which ended the service, because there is no arguing with a woman who has already started carrying things").
**Rule:** an aphorism gets its own sentence. Full stop at the tense border; the aphorism may stand alone after it. And ration: this narrator's aphorism habit is constitutive, so the edit is rationing, not detox — roughly one in three survives; the best one in any run of three, the others cut or converted to action.

**2. Broad-reference "which" carrying a plot event.** *Habit:* `which` attaches to a whole clause and is then asked to advance the action or land the joke ("which ended the service," "which achieved a second failure").
**Rule:** a which-clause may comment; it may not act. If it contains an event verb (*ended, achieved, stood*), promote it to its own sentence with a named subject. ("…which was how he announced that the car was waiting" passes: *was* comments.)

**3. Pronoun across an interrupter.** *Habit:* `it/one/them` separated from its antecedent by a participial or parenthetical phrase, landing on the wrong noun ("came in with a clean blouse over her arm, having lost this argument in advance…, and put it into the case" — *it* lands on *argument*).
**Rule:** after any interrupting phrase of six or more words, repeat the noun. No pronoun with a competing noun of matching number between it and its antecedent.

**4. The overloaded suspension.** *Habit:* three or more finite verbs plus an em-dash insertion or embedded quotation in one sentence ("rose mid-sentence — '…' — gathered the vase, carried it out, came back, sat, and picked the sentence up").
**Rule:** maximum two finite verbs when the sentence also carries a quotation or a dash-insertion. Split at the third verb.

**5. Reified abstractions whose verb collides with idiom.** *Habit:* remarks, grins, silences, hurt handled as physical objects — fine once, fatal when the chosen verb already means something else ("whatever came down the line **stood him up** mid-sentence"; a grin that "landed like a tipped hat" — a hat neither lands nor arrives).
**Rule:** one reified abstraction per paragraph. Before keeping the verb, test: does verb + object already form a fixed idiom with a different meaning? If yes, change the verb. If a simile's vehicle cannot literally perform the main verb, change one or the other.
*Scope note (2026-08-30):* the **abstract-subject + elegant-human-verb** construction ("relief reached the surface," "his attention arrived complete") is this same family at chapter scale — each instance may be fine; the *density* is the defect. Rule 5 fires on idiom collisions sentence-by-sentence; density and clustering belong to the ALTITUDE critic (style contract, "Narrative altitude"; the O/P/I/F classification). Don't double-flag: if a sentence is clean but the chapter is saturated, that's an altitude finding, not a line edit.

**6. Inverted temporal joints.** *Habit:* `when/before/so` clauses whose subordinate event is really the purpose or consequence of the main clause ("They stopped **when** Penn bought two apples" — the stop enables the buying; a thank-you delivered "instead" to a person who has already left the scene).
**Rule:** the event in a *when*-clause must be datable independently of the main clause. If the main clause enables the subordinate event, recast ("They stopped in a market town, where Penn bought two apples").

**7. The manner clause** *(added 2026-08-29 — human-reader flag: "far too much" of it)*. *Habit:* finishing an action with a generic-actor comparison — "the way X does Y," "in the manner of a woman who…," "with the air of…," "as though…," "like a man who…". Measured density when flagged: Ch 2 at 5.0 per 1,000 words; Ch 1 reads clean at ~1 per 1,000.
**Rule:** ration to ≤1 per ~500 words and ≤1 per scene; in a sweep, cut to the budget keeping only clauses whose vehicle ADDS information (physical precision, character knowledge, plot freight). Kill on sight: vehicles that repeat the verb they modify ("the room began to fray the way rooms fray when…") and manner-codas welded to already-complete actions.
*Worked repairs (the reader's own examples):*
> "…and shut the notebook on it, in the manner of a woman putting away a receipt she intends to dispute later." → "…and shut the notebook on it." *(the gesture already says it)*
> "Penn took over the side door the way other people take over a lifeboat — dishes logged, names in a column…" → "Penn took over the side door — dishes logged, names in a column…" *(the specifics ARE the lifeboat)*

**8. Semantic order** *(2026-08-31, from Jeremy's read)*. *Habit:* locative inversion ("At a farm track stood a plank…", "Beneath the cash tin lay…"), delayed or missing subjects, a LIST as delayed subject, mismatched parallels yoking nouns to gerunds ("covered the diary and research, but not finding her charger").
**Rule:** in an action sentence, default to person → action → object → consequence. Depart only when the departure buys a real effect, not a more literary sentence. One verb may not organise four unlike things. Give an action its actual subject. This is not a ban on description: a room, garment or light source may be the subject of a plain descriptive sentence, and not every sentence needs an event.

**9. The inference budget** *(2026-08-31)*. *Habit:* sentences containing a small riddle — metonymy asked to carry action ("argued with a ladder"), speech-act verbs that read physical first ("she raised the east range"), emotions as abstract-noun pairs ("caught between affront and hope"), causal links supplied only by implication, meaning that arrives only retrospectively. Measured: the chapters read at FK grade 5.5–6.7 while feeling hard — formulas cannot see inference load.
**Rule:** at most ONE inferential turn per sentence; the next-sentence test — if the reader needs the following sentence to understand this one's ordinary meaning, rewrite this one. Emotions arrive as behaviour ("The girl frowned, then glanced down at the prospectus"), not abstraction-pairs. **When two or more clarity flags land in one paragraph, stop word-patching and RECONSTRUCT the paragraph from its factual content.**

**10. Setup/payoff adjacency** *(2026-08-31)*. *Habit:* successive revision passes cut locally and orphan payoffs — "There may be sheep" surviving after "You're leaving ninety minutes early" was cut; a glancing hypothetical ("a charming letter") promoted four lines later into an established routine ("the charming letters").
**Rule:** for every joke, callback, and echo in the draft, verify its setup still exists in THIS draft, close enough to be alive, at matching scale. Also check the cue for every objection, demand, deflection and concession. A reply to a participation requirement cannot rely on a line that now sounds merely like an invitation; conditions after failed resistance must not be moved ahead of that resistance. Locate a historical incident before referring to it as an event both speakers know. An orphaned payoff is cut or re-set-up, never left to be understood in retrospect. This is a comprehension test, not a rule to bring clue and consequence closer together. Mystery emphasis across an interval belongs to the structural review below.

## Description check — paragraph and encounter scale

Check literal comprehension before polishing the adjectives:

- Is the threshold clear, or does “beyond” require a destination the reader has not been shown?
- Can the reader place the selected close-up within a comprehensible room or body? If not, restore the missing frame; do not add another isolated detail.
- Are light source, ambient light, visibility, season and clothing compatible? “Dim” and “flashing” can coexist, but the wording must tell us what is dark and what produces the flashes.
- Do consecutive portraits repeat the same age/hair/face/clothes structure? Change the selection, placement or scale, not merely the opening verb.
- Does a proposed interpretation exceed the visible or established fact? Grooming, colour and wear are not automatic proof of wealth, neglect, age anxiety or private intent.
- Has tightening removed the coherent picture while preserving a scattering of adjectives?

A sentence repair can clarify a source of light or an antecedent. If the scene needs new descriptive material or a different portrait design, flag that for the authorised prose revision rather than silently inventing canon during a copyedit. Protect useful static description and ordinary visual identity; no detail quota or compulsory symbolic meaning.

## Also in the pass (mechanical)

Subject–verb agreement · dialogue tags never comma-spliced to action beats ("She composed herself, 'Where should we begin?'" is an error) · no said-bookisms doing joke work ("'…' Penn smiled.") · numerals spelled out in dialogue and running prose · terminal punctuation present · register: no Americanisms or fillers in Penn's mouth (*I guess*, *basically*) · UK spelling.

## The worked example

> "She took the fern out under one arm, which ended the service, because there is no arguing with a woman who has already started carrying things."

Fails rules 1, 2, and 5 at once: *took out* has no completion, so "under one arm" arrives as a false destination; "the service" is a metaphor born and put to work inside a subordinate clause; the gnomic weld sits at the third joint of a right-branching chain. The punchline lands winded. Repair:

> She tucked the pot under one arm and carried it out, and that ended the service. There is no arguing with a woman who has already started carrying things.

## Compression calibration — preserve the speaker, not only the instruction

From two existing Chapter 1 drafts: “Fetch them. We're having a banquet” became “Go and get them. We'll make them last.” Both fetch the biscuits. The replacement is longer and turns occasion-making into sensible rationing. This is a loss of character, not improved clarity. These are comparative manuscript examples, not required lines for future scenes.

Before neutralising a flourish, ask whether the reader understands it immediately and whether it belongs to this character's present want. Keep clear, character-specific play; repair genuine ambiguity without removing the invitation, evasion or joke. Check the complete exchange: cutting its setup or its ordinary answering voice can spoil the surviving line. Preserve narrative footing and real emotional breaks. Do not repair this failure by making every speaker equally witty.

## Scope discipline

The pass touches roughly one sentence in four in an unedited draft. If it wants to touch more, the problem is upstream (register or structure) — stop and say so. If a repair changes what happens, who acts, or what a line means, it is not a line edit — flag instead.

**Causal-visibility and reading-time guard (2026-09-19):** do not conceal a mechanism by replacing a clear object with "something," making handling physically vague, suppressing known facts, or adding an unsupported substance, action or alternative culprit. Nor does "a quarter of an hour later" fix two conspicuously adjacent actions in the reader's experience. Flag cumulative emphasis across the relevant scenes/chapters for structural review. When tightening, check that the cut has not removed the interesting human event and left only a setup immediately followed by its suspicious consequence. Protect engaging character interaction as well as explicit clue facts; line editing does not assign those facts greater priority because they are easy to verify.

**Character-conduct guard (2026-09-19):** adding “may I,” changing a decision into a request, softening an expectation into a hope, supplying grateful explanation or cutting evasion into immediate acceptance changes character and power. It is not a courtesy polish. Compare the relevant card and local relationship state; flag a needed behavioural correction upstream. Do not make a scene easier to read by making its people easier to deal with.

**Emotional-timing guard (2026-09-05):** do not insert a penitent explanation, silence a defensive joke to make Penn nicer, or turn a small apology into full self-recognition. Do not remove an earned acknowledgement simply to make the chapter lighter. Changing what Penn understands, whether she repairs a hurt or how a relationship responds is voice/structure work to flag, not a sentence repair.
