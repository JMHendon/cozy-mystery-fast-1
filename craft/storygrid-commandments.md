# Scene Standard — how a scene earns its place

## The floor — name the dramatic unit before testing it

For a complete dramatic scene or sequence, explicitly identify the **inciting incident**, **progressive complications and turning point**, **crisis**, **climax** and **resolution**. Complications must alter the person's options or stakes, not merely add information. A crisis poses genuinely incompatible courses of action with meaningful stakes — best bad choice or irreconcilable goods. The climax answers it through an enacted choice; the resolution registers the resulting state.

A chapter is a reading unit; a scene is a dramatic unit; a sequence joins related dramatic movements. Apply the scene/length default and reasoned-exception policy in `cozy-mystery.md`, “Bite-sized chapters.” A chapter may deliberately interrupt an unresolved scene, but do not make incomplete fragments the norm merely to hit a length target. Map the five elements at their actual scale and mark absent elements **incomplete at this unit**, identifying where the developing sequence carries them. Do not mark an incomplete scene complete by renaming surprise as choice. At sequence review, verify that the promised decision and resulting change actually occur; do not keep deferring a missing crisis indefinitely.

**Count dramatic resets, not doorways or asterisks.** Follow the immediate pursuit, opposition and resulting change. Continuing the same live negotiation in another room need not start a scene; finishing one visit and beginning a different encounter usually does. A short bridge may connect scenes without becoming a separate one. Several encounters sharing the general purpose “get to know the house” are not automatically one scene. Conversely, an ordinary digression or change of tactic within an encounter is not automatically a new scene. When separating chapters, preserve causal continuity and end on a turn or forward pull the story actually earns.

A collapse, discovery or revelation may end a chapter and turn the larger story without being a voluntary climax. Do not manufacture an emergency dilemma such as preserving an innocently arranged room versus helping a dying person when the character has no reason to consider preservation. Rescue can have an unintended evidentiary cost; that consequence is not a conscious sacrifice. Earn a choice in the human situation that actually exists.

## Chapter cohesion — what carries forward

**Subject is not movement.** “Get to know the household” identifies material, not a developing situation. Name the immediate pursuit, uncertainty or relationship the reader follows, what makes it difficult, how it changes and what remains at the exit. A question can concern pleasure, work or a relationship before it concerns a crime. The reader need not formulate it consciously; the protagonist need not announce it or restate it at every transition. A broad question written over an itinerary does not make the prose cohere.

**Carry-over test.** At substantial transitions, identify what is still alive from the previous encounter and how it affects the next. For example: a refusal changes the approach; an answer changes the next question; a promise makes the reader await a meeting; a breach restricts access; enjoying somebody's company makes the coming separation matter. Report the particular link in the prose, not a justification supplied by the outline. Bridge through the live situation, not just the next room. A clarifying sentence may help, but do not require explanatory narration where action already makes the reason legible.

Shared location, continuous time and a common topic do not prevent resets. Deleting asterisks cannot join independent encounters; an overnight gap need not break a pursuit. A break with no elapsed time is a transition to examine, not a defect to delete. Count genuine changes of dramatic business. Continuous time can help readers follow a real connection; it cannot manufacture one.

**Repeated obstacles are not automatically progressive.** Several refusals or postponements may leave the same options intact. Retain them where their consequences differ or accumulate: changed terms, a lost route, new exposure, a decision that cannot be deferred again. Otherwise combine, remove or redesign the repetition. Do not invent another obstruction merely to fill a complication slot.

**Reused events must change something now.** Characters learning something is not the reader learning something. When an interview, statement, briefing, debrief or reconstruction returns to an event the reader has already seen, identify what the reuse changes in this encounter: a decision, tactic, interpretation, obligation, relationship or available route. Summarise routine coverage in a clause. Keep repetition that earns a distinct emotional or dramatic effect. Do not make every familiar fact incriminating merely to supply novelty, and do not apply this test as a ban on description or conversational pleasure. Judge it with the preceding chapter in view: a reader who has not read it cannot see the repetition. Apply it first when choosing the encounter, not after the draft.

**Set-pieces belong to the developing chapter.** Name how a substantial event fulfils a prior promise, redirects a pursuit, tests a relationship, creates a wanted experience or defeats an expectation. An enjoyable ceremony may pay an earlier participation challenge while an interview remains pending; it need not discuss the interview throughout. If the protagonist and reader merely wait out an unrelated event, reconsider its placement or the chapter's design. Don't turn the pleasure into a clue to justify it.

**Deletion and rearrangement are diagnostics, not verdicts.** Ask what disappears with a passage: causal understanding, expectation, attachment, visual footing, distinctive pleasure or nothing substantial. Several principal encounters that can be freely reordered without changing the developing situation warrant scrutiny. A joke, portrait or game need not cause a later plot action. Assess supporting material at the scale of the whole chapter, not as a chain of compulsory mini-turns.

**Verify the experience.** Report the opening expectation, the encounters that develop it, any point of reset and what the ending answers, interrupts or newly makes important. A retrospective five-commandment map is not evidence that readers experienced the movement. Keep scene/sequence mechanics explicit, but test them against the prose and unprimed readers.

## Variation — no two consecutive units in the same configuration

Consecutive scenes, and consecutive chapters, must differ in **place**, in **who is
present**, and in **scale** (one-to-one, small group, full room). Changing one axis
while holding the others is not variation: the same two people in a new room, or the
same room with one substitution, reads as continuation.

Name the configuration of the previous unit before designing the next: location ·
cast · number · register. If two or more axes repeat, redesign before drafting.

**Three consecutive units in one location is a defect, not a judgement call.** Two is
an allowance to be spent deliberately and recorded in the brief — typically when a
location is the protagonist's own base and the cast and register invert across the
pair. Do not spend it twice in a sequence.

This governs the plan, not the prose: solve it by relocating or re-casting an
encounter, never by inventing travel to break up a chapter that is genuinely one
movement. Where a required encounter appears locked to a place, test whether the
place is actually required before accepting the repeat — a formal interview, a
handover or a refusal will often play harder somewhere the protagonist controls less.
Relocation varies an encounter; it does not make one. Before moving an interview, test
whether it should be an interview at all ("Reused events must change something now").

Variation is designed at sequence altitude, not discovered at draft time. A chapter
brief cannot see three-in-a-row; the sequence's chapter allocation can, and is where
this is checked.

## The polarity test (the scene-end gate)

Name the principal value at stake (for example trust/betrayal, freedom/constraint, belonging/exclusion or justice/injustice), its position at the start and its position at the end. Give evidence from the scene, not merely the brief's label. If there is no turn, decide whether this is useful connective or experiential material within a larger movement, or a genuinely flat dramatic unit. Pre-crime scenes need not turn on justice. More reversals are not automatically better; secondary values may move without displacing the scene's central pressure.

## Complications made by people

At major encounters, name each person's immediate want — stated positively, not only as what they refuse — and the action by which those wants collide. A refusal should change the approach; a concession may carry conditions; an ally can have an incompatible obligation. Information withholding alone is not a full scene engine. The protagonist's charm can change the terms without dissolving the other person's goal. Do not require an obstructive person in every short passage, and do not mistake a polished retort for consequential resistance.

A consequential conversation may contain exchanges whose value is companionship rather than a new fact or obstacle. Apply the change test to the dramatic unit, not every line. Do not let a causal repair preserve all clues while deleting the enjoyable human exchange around them, or invent a local crisis to justify familiar play.

## Reading time is not story time

"Twenty minutes later" separates events on the clock, not in the reader's attention. If the last concrete action is a drink and the next substantial action is a collapse, a sentence summarising the interval still makes them neighbours. Conversely, pages of repetition can make a brief event dominate memory.

Plan the intervening experience where the event warrants one: a compelling exchange, a contested demonstration, an enjoyable activity or a change of allegiance with its own immediate interest. Do not pad merely to bury a clue or introduce unsupported alternatives. Preserve the true chronology, visible handling and viewpoint knowledge. In mystery review, track the last substantial actions, repeated objects and isolated sensory details across adjacent scenes and chapters; assess the resulting hypothesis, not a target word-gap or mention-count. A clue can be clear once in ordinary use without receiving a separate setup, reminder and ominous close-up.

## The obligatory scenes (cozy)

Inciting crime · investigation committed to · survey of suspects · at least two herrings honestly pursued and dismissed · the click (reader and sleuth arrive together) · the confrontation (in motion) · the reveal/confession (motive made legible, tragic) · restoration (real, with one visible cost). The book bible places them; this file only requires they exist.

## The parallel internal genre

Every book runs a worldview arc under the external mystery (this series: the wound sequence — see `Series Documents/penn-mysteries.md`). Review its development across the book and its relationship to the external climax; do not require a wound beat, introspective pause or lesson in every chapter. Pleasure and ordinary work can occupy the foreground. Protect established disclosure order and earned development without turning every encounter into a rehearsal for emotional resolution.

## Scene anti-patterns (the dramaturgy critic's list)

1. The flat dramatic unit — no material complication or change, with missing commandments concealed by labels or indefinitely deferred to later chapters.
2. The unearned shift — a crisis with only one viable option.
3. The unexplained shift — prose and brief name different outcomes. Check the actual scene and downstream causality; reconcile the brief if the better scene legitimately changed the plan.
4. The premature climax — mid-book scene fires information saved for the click.
5. The early wound-resolution — Acts 1–2 scene lands what only the ending may land.
6. Competing focal pressures with no hierarchy; nothing receives enough development to turn.
7. Escalation mistaken for a completed choice — a revelation is falsely labelled the climax of a resolved dramatic unit.
8. Resolution without agency — the world capitulates; the protagonist merely attends. An interruption may leave the choice unresolved, but the sequence must eventually confront it.
9. *(Series addition)* The interview chain — a "scene" that is one competent extraction of information; see the group-scene engine on the lead's card. Its commonest form is the retelling: an aftermath interview or debrief that walks back through events the reader watched, one subject at a time. It starts in the brief — a Development listing topics and a counterpart defined by what they refuse — so diagnose it there.
10. *(Series addition)* The summarised set-piece — the text claims an event was absorbing, moving or entertaining, while rendering only its logistical preparation. Play the human interaction that earns the experience; compress routine intervals, not the reason the event matters.
11. The chapter as a container for a whole itinerary — successive introductions or encounters keep restarting the reader, with no dominant dramatic movement. Recompose the chapter units rather than stripping description and banter, hiding scene breaks or inventing miniature crises to make each stop qualify.
