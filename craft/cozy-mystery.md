# Genre Standard — what a cozy mystery is

## The global value

Cozy mystery is a subgenre of crime, and crime's global external value is **justice**. Its ladder runs justice → unfairness → injustice → tyranny, where tyranny is injustice wearing justice's authority. Every act and sequence turns on it, and most chapters will. Each book also names a **global internal genre and value** in its own foundation, for example a worldview arc (naïveté ↔ sophistication) or a status arc. Every act, sequence and chapter states its position on both values, with polarity, even when one holds. Other values (trust, access, understanding, life) are how justice is won and lost: name what was spent, but never as a substitute for the shift. A stated value shift that never says what happened to the case is an incomplete shift. Moving *away* from justice is a legitimate shift; having no position on it is not.

## The reader promise

The crime is serious; the world is gentle. The reader's pleasure is **companionship**: they return because they want to spend time in the lead's company, and many want to *be like* the lead. The plot must satisfy a fair-play reader without ever becoming the point. The world is fixable: cases resolve, communities reset, and the ending leaves the world genuinely better, with one visible human cost (usually the killer's tragedy) unresolved by design.

## The hard gates (pass/fail)

These are not conventions to weigh. They are checked in the outline's gate table, after drafting, and after every revision pass. Only the author can grant an exception, in writing, in the book's brief. No planner, critic, reviewer, reader panel or score can waive one, and no strength elsewhere offsets a failure. A failed gate is reported first in any review, ahead of every other finding. `checklist.md` carries the one-page version for briefs.

### A. Opening and structure

1. **The murder happens in Chapter 1 or Chapter 2.** By the end of Chapter 2 someone is dead on the page, witnessed or found, and the reader knows it. The crime is the book's global inciting incident: it *opens* the Beginning Hook and is never the Beginning Hook's turning point or climax. It need not yet be *known* to be murder (a collapse thought natural, a fall, an accident awaiting toxicology), but the death itself cannot wait.
   - Everything a planner is tempted to stage first (the arrival, the wager, the feud, the gathering, the ensemble, the home base) is delivered around the death and through the investigation that follows, never as a run-up to the body.
   - If the death must happen at a scheduled event (a wedding, a festival, a séance, an opening night), that event is in Chapter 1 or 2. If the calendar says otherwise, change the calendar and open on the day of the death.
   - A pre-crime hook (a threat, a bet, an ominous arrival) does not substitute for the crime.
   - None of these is an exception: "it's Book 1," "the setup is charming," a set-piece, strong complications, an event deadline, a market-percentage benchmark.
   - The author may approve a death in Chapter 3. Nothing later is permitted.
2. **The lead appears within the first three pages** (about the first 750 words). The book may open on the victim, the body or the scene of the crime rather than on the lead, but the lead arrives fast. On first appearance the lead is recognisably themself, doing something characteristic (working, playing, showing off, enjoying something), so the reader knows within pages why this is someone worth following.
3. **The lead commits to the investigation by the end of Chapter 3,** for a reason rooted in character (appetite, loyalty, guilt, curiosity, a personal stake), not mere proximity.
4. **The official foil is on the page no later than the chapter after the lead commits** (so by Chapter 4 at the latest): a police officer, coroner, constable or similar authority. Where the book has a recurring partner or romantic foil, the two spar from their first meeting and share scenes throughout, with no gap longer than three chapters.
5. **Every suspect, including the killer, is on the page by the end of the first act** (about 25% of the book). No new suspect after the midpoint.
6. **The five commandments hold at every level.** Every chapter is a complete scene with an inciting incident, progressive complications and a turning point, a crisis, a climax and a resolution, and a value that measurably shifts. A deliberately interrupted scene is recorded in the brief and completed in the next chapter. Every act and the global story are mapped with their five commandments and the chapter that carries each (`storygrid-commandments.md`).

### B. Chapter mechanics

7. **Chapters are bite-sized: one scene, 1,800–2,200 words.** Each chapter is one dramatic scene (at most two closely connected scenes) of 1,800–2,200 words. This is what makes a page-turner: the reader always feels there is time for one more chapter. Exceptions need a specific reading-experience reason and the author's approval, recorded in the brief; aim for at least 95% of chapters inside the rule.
8. **Every chapter changes the configuration.** Each chapter differs from the one before in at least two of place, cast and scale (one-to-one, small group, full room). Never three consecutive chapters in the same location.
9. **Book length** is within the brief's target (default 65,000–85,000 words).

### C. The mystery

10. **Fair play.** Every clue the solution depends on is on the page, legibly, before the reveal. The sleuth reaches the solution through deduction from evidence the reader held. A confession may confirm and complete the solution; it may not supply it. On re-read the reader can see they had everything.
11. **The sleuth is an amateur with a legitimate reason to be present** and a procedurally credible relationship with the authorities. Their work, connections or community give them access. A wish to help confers neither police powers nor a right to a grieving person's cooperation.
12. **The killer is human, never a monster.** The motive is comprehensible and, at best, tragic: the reader should feel *why*, not just *that*.
13. **The climax is in motion, never a drawing-room gathering.** The sleuth is briefly at real risk, scaled honestly to their body, and the sleuth causes the outcome rather than receiving it.

### D. The cozy contract

14. **Nothing on the page breaks the cozy promise.**
    - The death may be witnessed (a collapse or a discovery); the violence is never rendered. No gore, no clinical detail of injury, no torture.
    - No sex on the page; romance stays at the level of tension, a kiss or a closed door.
    - No profanity beyond the mildest (the series style guide lists what is allowed, if anything).
    - No harm to children or animals on the page; a pet never dies.
    - No sexual violence, no graphic drug use, no cruelty played for effect.
    - The register is light-energy (`light-energy-structure.md`): never as dark as a thriller or a police procedural.
15. **The furniture is fixed:** a closed circle of suspects, a home base, a recurring ensemble and a procedural foil. Their specifics rotate per book.

### E. The ending

16. **Restoration, and a reason to come back.** The case resolves completely and justice is done; the community resets, altered; one visible human cost remains. The mystery never ends on a cliffhanger, but at least one personal or ensemble thread pulls the reader toward the next book.

## Conventions (strong defaults)

- **Misdirection is fair.** Preserve what the viewpoint character plainly sees or knows. Never make an observable fact vague merely to hide its significance. An innocent use, a plausible wrong interpretation or genuinely competing evidence can occupy attention. Not every clue needs its own staged false explanation. Judge the cumulative emphasis across neighbouring chapters as well as whether each required fact appears.
- **The case mirrors the personal, and nobody says so on the page.**
- **At least two red herrings are honestly pursued and dismissed,** each with real evidence behind it and a cost to dismiss.

## Anti-patterns (the critic's list)

- The sleuth who is only ever told things (charm as a skeleton key with no cost).
- The sleuth who is only ever humbled: losing, apologising and being put in their place, chapter after chapter, until there is nobody left to want to be.
- The suspect interviewed once and forgotten; the herring with no honest evidence behind it.
- Grief used as set-dressing; a victim nobody mourns.
- The village that exists only to be quaint; warmth with no asymmetry or cost anywhere in it.
- A resolution the sleuth receives rather than causes.
- Cruelty, cynicism, or a world revealed to be rotten. That is another genre.
- The slow fuse: chapters of setup, introductions and atmosphere before the body (gate 1).

## The lead

The lead is the book's product. Readers buy the series to spend time with this person, and the best leads make readers want to *be like* them.

- **Write the lead from behaviour, not adjectives.** "Charming, larger than life, lovable" is easy to agree with and easy to lose. The lead card says how the lead enters a room, talks, theorises, wins, loses, spends, flirts, fears and enjoys, with sample lines at several registers.
- **A named reference is a specification.** If the author models the lead on an existing character, the lead card translates that model into concrete behaviour, and that section travels word for word into every drafting and revision brief. Test chapters against it directly ("Would that character say this out loud?").
- **The lead owns the room.** The lead is the brightest, most distinctive voice in most scenes, and their wit lives in their own dialogue, said to people, rather than in the narration. Supporting characters are vivid in their own currency (plain speech, literalism, task, silence, a different kind of authority) and may win exchanges. The house restraints on wit, epigram, cleverness and display exist to stop *ambient* wit (everyone sounding equally polished). They apply to the supporting cast and the narrator, **never as a ceiling on the lead**.
- **Introduce the lead at strength** (and within three pages, gate 2). The opening chapters show the lead at their best: competent, delighted, in their element. A flaw can show early, but the reader must first see what makes this person magnetic. Penance, exile and humiliation are not an introduction.
- **Lovability is not humiliation.** The lead can lose, be caught, be wrong and pay for it. Across any run of chapters they also win, are right, delight people and get the better of someone. Count both; a lead who mostly loses has been flattened.
- **The lead grows; they don't shrink.** An internal arc may cost the lead something, but the book never treats the lead's essential nature (their size, appetite, generosity, showmanship, curiosity) as the defect to cure.

## Bite-sized chapters (gate 7)

Every book plans roughly **95% of chapters as one dramatic scene, no more than two closely connected scenes, and 1,800–2,200 words for the whole chapter**. A chapter under 1,800 usually means the encounter hasn't been fully played; one over 2,200 usually means two scenes. Never pad to reach the floor: play the scene more fully instead. The principle comes from Shawn Coyne's Story Grid: a chapter that is one complete scene ends on a turn, and a short one always leaves the reader feeling there is time for another. That is how a cozy becomes a page-turner.

A scene is a developing dramatic encounter, not a room. A live negotiation can continue from a hall into a study; several unrelated visits in one house are still several scenes. Brief travel, orientation or an aftermath beat need not be independent scenes, but their words and cumulative load still count. Do not relabel a string of encounters as "one arrival scene" to satisfy the rule. See `storygrid-commandments.md` for the distinction between chapter, scene and sequence.

Give each chapter a dominant immediate purpose, a meaningful development or turn, and a reason to continue. Prefer a complete scene; an interrupted scene is an occasional deliberate choice, not the default way to meet the length target. End at a consequential change, decision or live question, not an arbitrary word count. A quieter relational turn can be enough; cliffhangers and invented dangers are not compulsory.

**Solve overcrowding in the plan before compressing the prose.** Split at an earned turn, relocate an encounter, combine genuinely compatible business or remove a dispensable function. Protect the chosen scene's conversation, physical setting and character pleasure. Do not keep every introduction by reducing each person to a name, each room to a prop and each exchange to its information. Distribute the ensemble across the opening sequence. If the material needs more chapters, review chapter count and downstream references rather than forcing it into the old containers.

Longer chapters or more than two scenes need a specific reading-experience reason and the author's approval. A continuous set-piece may justify an exception; "all these items are required," "it is Chapter 1" and "it is the finale" do not automatically do so. Record the reason briefly in the brief or revision report. The gate fails when unapproved exceptions accumulate, not on a single recorded exception.

**Capacity is editorial, not arithmetic.** Budget developed encounters and the attention they require, not a fixed number of words per beat or named person. A cameo does not automatically need a scene; a three-person conversation can still be overfull. Allocate enough room for the chosen experience before promising every introduction. A book's event deadline may justify an opening *length* exception; it never justifies delaying the death past Chapter 2 (gate 1).

## Commissioning a book and reviewing its outline

**Benchmark against published comps before planning.** Read at least two published comparison titles the author supplies or approves, ideally bestselling cozies in the same register. Record for each: the chapter of the first death, when the sleuth and the official foil first meet, typical chapter length, how the lead is introduced, and when the sleuth commits to investigating. Keep the table in the book's foundation and use it as the structural benchmark. If a planned structure departs from every comp on one of these points, treat that as a finding to justify to the author, not a creative choice to defend.

**Start the outline from the gates.** Fill in the gate table before the chapter briefs. If the gate table cannot be filled, the plan is wrong: redesign the premise, calendar or opening rather than writing around it.

**Separate three kinds of planning material.** (1) Invariants: the causal truth of the crime, established canon, viewpoint knowledge, evidence needed for the solution and the latest point by which the reader needs it. (2) Dramatic functions: what a person wants, what interferes, what changes and what question or appetite draws us onward. (3) Possible execution: an illustrative setting, activity or encounter. Label the third as optional; do not promote examples into required dialogue, blocking or a parade of objects. Record an invariant once at its source and reference it; adjacent briefs should distinguish a new reveal from information the reader already has.

**Develop a situation, not an itinerary.** Brief the pursuit, counterpressure, development and exit, with essential chapter-available facts separately labelled as limits a draft must not contradict, not as business to put on the page. Write the development as tactics and consequences (first approach → the response that changes it → next action), not as an ordered list of subjects. At major transitions, something the reader cares about should carry forward or change; a shared subject, room or uninterrupted clock is not enough. The lead need not announce the objective. Repeated refusals must change the options rather than simply delay the same question. A major event may fulfil another live promise or become the wanted experience itself. Keep ordinary pleasure and description; neither needs to earn a clue. The full carry-over standard is in `storygrid-commandments.md`, "Chapter cohesion — what carries forward."

**Review the calendar before filling it.** First confirm the calendar puts the death in Chapter 1 or 2 (gate 1). If the days before a scheduled event would fill chapters ahead of the body, cut or fold them and open on the day of the death. Then, if a chapter mainly occupies the interval before a scheduled event, ask whether that interval is needed. Combining days or relocating encounters may strengthen pressure, but record losses as well as gains: duration can build attachment, atmosphere or an ongoing shared amusement. Do not compress the calendar if it breaks causal truth, human plausibility, earned relationships or the reader's experience of a clue's timing.

**Character contract before encounter design.** For each principal, preserve professional method, default conduct, customary tactics and the relationship state at entry. The author's current character decisions and approved cards outrank an outline's convenient staging. Before issuing a brief, test its main encounter against those properties. If the scene only works by making a guarded subject confide, an intrusive lead ask routine permission, or an independent professional adopt a new working method, redesign the scene. A required outcome does not prescribe an out-of-character route. Flag a real unresolved canon conflict rather than overriding either source silently.

**Defaults are not the full range.** Label voice samples by the conditions that earn their register: public performance, early professional contact, established friendship, acute loss, later exposure. A useful new sample must not quietly redefine the baseline. When revising a card in response to feedback, replace the conflicting rule and review the adjacent examples; adding an attractive new trait while keeping contradictory examples feeds the next error. Voice includes actions, timing, access and concessions, not just sentence style. A card that restricts a supporting character's wit or speech must also give a positive aim and a range of responses to pressure: a ceiling limits display, not agency. A card built from a few repeated gestures produces a wall, not a person. **The lead's card carries no ceiling on wit or display**; it may name what the lead never does (cruelty, punching down), and it states what the lead does at full size.

**Design encounters, not information deliveries.** At the important turns, give the other person an independent goal and a credible action that interferes with the lead's immediate aim: they redirect, bargain, recruit, compete, deny access, insist on an obligation or act first. The lead must adapt. Resistance can arise from care, work, pride, limited time or incompatible loyalties, not only guilt. Cooperation and uncomplicated kindness remain possible. Do not make the killer the only active person, or the authorities foolish so the amateur can proceed. **Choose that collision before the setting:** name what the lead tries, the other person's positive aim and what changes inside the encounter, then choose place and cast. A knowledge-ledger row ("the police test the account") is a fact to deliver, not a scene. The day after a shocking event defaults to people recounting it; an interview, statement or debrief about events the reader has watched needs a stated change it makes now (`storygrid-commandments.md`, "Reused events must change something now"), or it shrinks to a clause inside a different encounter.

**Generosity can succeed.** For an abundant, socially fearless lead, money and connections are often shared pleasures rather than evidence for a moral prosecution. Let a favour work and a group enjoy it without attaching a rebuke. Distinguish an unwanted solution from exploitation; human opposition does not require every helper to resist or every gift to impose a debt.

**Keep four clocks separate.** Record when an object was interfered with, when a substance was encountered or consumed, when harm became apparent, and when investigators learned each fact. A medical estimate of consumption cannot date earlier tampering. Separate provisional suspicion, a laboratory finding, a medical cause and evidence of deliberate harm. If story pacing compresses a forensic turnaround, label that convention for the author; do not make a fictional deadline a medical fact. Keep solution records planner-facing and translate only chapter-available facts into briefs.

**Make room for a life worth joining.** During outline review, look across the book for genuinely wanted pleasures, excursions, work, friendships and obligations, including changes of company and location. A game, meal, toy or outing may affect a decision or an encounter without producing a clue. Do not add one example of every card trait or force every pleasure to earn its place through investigative usefulness. If all allocated space belongs to clue delivery and emotional fallout, revise the encounters rather than ordering the novelist to insert joy between them.

**Companionship is substantive, not leftover space.** Protect conversations the reader enjoys for these particular people, not just for their information. A principal encounter should change the situation, but each exchange need not advance the case, impose a cost or produce insight. Physical appetite and conversational play can reinforce each other. When compressing, select the exchange that carries pleasure or relationship and cut redundant explanation or business around it. Do not keep every plot item by reducing every person to efficient answers. Familiar relationships can appear across the sequence; there is no requirement to introduce the entire recurring cast in the opening.

**Leave the novelist room to create the event.** An important host, performance or celebration must be interesting in its own right; cards and samples supply ranges, not lines to copy. A brief can require that a person visibly wins interest or meets resistance without dictating how. Protect the truth and necessary evidence when a better scene changes the means; reconcile the plan to that better scene instead of repairing the prose back into a checklist.

**Review both experience and inference.** Ask what the reader is enjoying, wanting and expecting before asking what has been planted. Then ask what explanation a first-time reader would now suspect, and which repetitions or juxtapositions suggest it. Fair play is not a requirement to spotlight each mechanism. Reading-time proximity and event-time proximity are different; see `storygrid-commandments.md`.

These are planning decisions and review lenses, not compulsory questions to dramatise in every chapter. The commissioned outline should preserve a causal spine while leaving room for character-led discovery.
