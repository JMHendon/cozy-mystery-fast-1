# Genre Standard — what this series' cozy is

## The global value

Cozy mystery is a subgenre of crime, and crime's global external value is **justice**
(ladder: justice → unfairness → injustice → tyranny, injustice wearing justice's
authority). Every act and sequence turns on it, and most chapters will. Each book also
names a **global internal genre and value** — for the Penn series, Worldview–Education,
*rent ↔ home* (see the book's `acts-and-sequences.md`) — and every act, sequence and
chapter states its position on both values, with polarity, even when one holds. Other
values — trust, access, understanding, life — are how justice is won and lost; name
what was spent in the description, never as a substitute for the shift. A stated value
shift that never says what happened to the case is an incomplete shift. Moving *away*
from justice is a legitimate shift; having no position on it is not.

## The reader promise

The crime is serious; the world is gentle. The reader's pleasure is **companionship** — they return because they want to be in the protagonist's company, and the plot must satisfy a fair-play reader without ever becoming the point. The world is fixable: cases resolve, communities reset, and the ending leaves the world genuinely better, with one visible human cost (the killer's tragedy) unresolved by design.

## Non-negotiables (rulings, not conventions)

1. **The death may be witnessed; the violence is never rendered.** A collapse on the page is permitted (Book 1 requires it — the seat-taking must be seen by room and reader); gore, physical suffering and violent acts stay off-page. (Amended 2026-08-27: the old wording — "the body is found, never witnessed" — contradicted the bible and would have 'fixed' the best beat in Ch 2.)
2. **The murder happens in Chapter 1 or Chapter 2.** By the end of Chapter 2 someone is dead on the page — witnessed or found — and the reader knows it. The crime is the book's global inciting incident: it *opens* the Beginning Hook; it is never the Beginning Hook's turning point or climax. It need not yet be *known* to be murder (a collapse thought natural, a fall, an accident awaiting toxicology), but the death itself cannot wait. Everything a planner is tempted to stage first — the arrival, the wager, the feud, the gathering, the ensemble and home base — is delivered around the death and through the investigation that follows, never as a run-up to the body. If the death must occur at a scheduled event (a séance, a wedding, a festival), that event is in Chapter 1 or 2; if the calendar says otherwise, change the calendar and open on the day of the death. A pre-crime hook (a threat, a bet, an ominous arrival) does not substitute for the crime. "It's Book 1," "the setup is charming," a set-piece, strong complications and an event deadline are not exceptions. Only the author may approve a later death, in writing, in the brief — no planner, critic or reviewer can grant it — and even then never later than Chapter 3. (Added 2026-10-08 after a draft placed its death in Ch 8, ~20% in, and every review layer rationalised it.)
3. **The sleuth is an amateur with a legitimate reason to be present** (this series: memoir work, the host's permission and a procedurally credible relationship with the authorities) and a *character-driven* reason to investigate (appetite — she cannot leave a locked thing locked). A publisher's wishes confer neither police powers nor a right to a grieving person's cooperation.
4. **The killer is human, never a monster.** Motive comprehensible and tragic; the reader should feel *why*, not just *that*.
5. **Misdirection is fair.** On re-read, the reader can see they held the evidence. Preserve what the viewpoint character plainly sees or knows; never make an observable fact vague merely to hide its significance. An innocent use, a plausible wrong interpretation or genuinely competing evidence can occupy attention. Not every clue needs its own staged false explanation. Judge the cumulative emphasis across neighbouring chapters as well as whether each required fact appears.
6. **The climax is in motion, never a drawing-room gathering.** The sleuth is briefly at real risk, scaled honestly to her body.
7. **The case mirrors the personal, and nobody says so on the page.**
8. **Closed circle, home base, recurring ensemble, procedural foil** — the furniture is fixed; its specifics rotate per book.

## Anti-patterns (the critic's list)

- The sleuth who is only ever told things (charm as a skeleton key with no cost).
- The suspect interviewed once and forgotten; the herring with no honest evidence behind it.
- Grief used as set-dressing; a victim nobody mourns.
- The village that exists only to be quaint; warmth with no asymmetry or cost anywhere in it.
- A resolution the sleuth receives rather than causes.
- Cruelty, cynicism, or a world revealed to be rotten — that is another genre.

## Bite-sized chapters — the house default

Plan roughly **95% of chapters as one dramatic scene wherever possible, no more than two closely connected scenes, and normally fewer than 2,000 words for the whole chapter**. Shorter chapters are welcome; there is no minimum to pad towards. This is the series' chosen reading rhythm, not a universal definition of the genre.

A scene is a developing dramatic encounter, not a room. A live negotiation can continue from a hall into a study; several unrelated visits in one house are still several scenes. Brief travel, orientation or an aftermath beat need not be independent scenes, but their words and cumulative load still count. Do not relabel a string of encounters as “one arrival scene” to satisfy the rule. See `storygrid-commandments.md` for the distinction between chapter, scene and sequence.

Give each chapter a dominant immediate purpose, a meaningful development or turn, and a reason to continue. Prefer a complete scene; an interrupted scene is an occasional deliberate choice, not the default way to meet the length target. End at a consequential change, decision or live question, not an arbitrary word count. A quieter relational turn can be enough; cliffhangers and invented dangers are not compulsory.

**Solve overcrowding in the plan before compressing the prose.** Split at an earned turn, relocate an encounter, combine genuinely compatible business or remove a dispensable function. Protect the chosen scene's conversation, physical setting and character pleasure. Do not keep every introduction by reducing each person to a name, each room to a prop and each exchange to its information. Distribute the ensemble across the opening sequence. If the material needs more chapters, review chapter count and downstream references rather than forcing it into the old containers.

Longer chapters or more than two scenes need a specific reading-experience reason and author approval. A continuous set-piece may justify an exception; “all these items are required,” “it is Chapter 1” and “it is the finale” do not automatically do so. Record the reason briefly in the brief or revision report, not a new form. Roughly 95% is a whole-book aspiration, not an exact quota or an automatic generation failure. Flag legacy briefs that conflict and propose a restructuring; do not silently cut canon or rewrite accepted chapters.

**Capacity is editorial, not arithmetic.** Budget developed encounters and the attention they require, not a fixed number of words per beat or named person. A cameo does not automatically need a scene; a three-person conversation can still be overfull. Allocate enough room for the chosen experience before promising every introduction. A book's explicit event deadline may justify an opening *length* exception; it does not exempt the rest of an act, and it never justifies delaying the death past Chapter 2 (non-negotiable 2). Distinguish approved exceptions from legacy targets still needing review.

## Commissioning a book and reviewing its outline

**Separate three kinds of planning material.** (1) Invariants: the causal truth of the crime, established canon, viewpoint knowledge, evidence needed for the solution and the latest point by which the reader needs it. (2) Dramatic functions: what a person wants, what interferes, what changes and what question or appetite draws us onward. (3) Possible execution: an illustrative setting, activity or encounter. Label the third as optional; do not promote examples into required dialogue, blocking or a parade of objects. Record an invariant once at its source and reference it; adjacent briefs should distinguish a new reveal from information the reader already has.

**Develop a situation, not an itinerary.** Brief the pursuit, counterpressure, development and exit, with essential chapter-available facts separately labelled — as limits a draft must not contradict, not as business to put on the page. Write the development as tactics and consequences (first approach → the response that changes it → next action), not as an ordered list of subjects. At major transitions, something the reader cares about should carry forward or change; a shared subject, room or uninterrupted clock is not enough. The protagonist need not announce the objective. Repeated refusals must change the options rather than simply delay the same question. A major event may fulfil another live promise or become the wanted experience itself. Keep ordinary pleasure and description; neither needs to earn a clue. The full carry-over standard is in `storygrid-commandments.md`, “Chapter cohesion — what carries forward.”

**Review the calendar before filling it.** First confirm the calendar puts the death in Chapter 1 or 2 (non-negotiable 2); if the days before a scheduled event would fill chapters ahead of the body, cut or fold them and open on the day of the death. If a chapter mainly occupies the interval before a scheduled event, ask whether that interval is needed. Combining days or relocating encounters may strengthen pressure, but record losses as well as gains: duration can build attachment, atmosphere or an ongoing shared amusement. Do not compress the calendar if it breaks causal truth, human plausibility, earned relationships or the reader's experience of a clue's timing.

**Character contract before encounter design.** For each principal, preserve professional method, default conduct, customary tactics and the relationship state at entry. The author's current character decisions and approved cards outrank an outline's convenient staging. Before issuing a brief, test its main encounter against those properties. If the scene only works by making a guarded subject confide, an intrusive lead solicit routine permission, or an independent professional adopt a new working method, redesign the scene. A required outcome does not prescribe an out-of-character route. Flag a real unresolved canon conflict rather than overriding either source silently.

**Defaults are not the full range.** Label samples by the conditions that earn their register: public performance, early professional contact, established friendship, acute loss, later exposure. A useful new sample must not quietly redefine the baseline. When revising a card in response to feedback, replace the conflicting rule and review the adjacent examples; adding an attractive new trait while keeping contradictory examples feeds the next error. Voice includes actions, timing, access and concessions, not just sentence style. A card that restricts a principal's wit or speech must also give a positive aim and a range of responses to pressure: a ceiling limits display, not agency. A card built from a few repeated gestures produces a wall, not a person.

**Design encounters, not information deliveries.** At the important turns, give the other person an independent goal and a credible action that interferes with the protagonist's immediate aim: they redirect, bargain, recruit, compete, deny access, insist on an obligation or act first. The protagonist must adapt. Resistance can arise from care, work, pride, limited time or incompatible loyalties, not only guilt. Cooperation and uncomplicated kindness remain possible. Do not make the killer the only active person, or the authorities foolish so the amateur can proceed. **Choose that collision before the setting:** name what the protagonist tries, the other person's positive aim and what changes inside the encounter, then choose place and cast. A knowledge-ledger row ("the police test the account") is a fact to deliver, not a scene. The day after a shocking event defaults to people recounting it; an interview, statement or debrief about events the reader has watched needs a stated change it makes now (`storygrid-commandments.md`, "Reused events must change something now"), or it shrinks to a clause inside a different encounter.

**Generosity can succeed.** For an abundant, socially fearless protagonist, money and connections are often shared pleasures rather than evidence for a moral prosecution. Let a favour work and a group enjoy it without attaching a rebuke. Distinguish an unwanted solution from exploitation; human opposition does not require every helper to resist or every gift to impose a debt.

**Keep four clocks separate.** Record when an object was interfered with, when a substance was encountered or consumed, when harm became apparent, and when investigators learned each fact. A medical estimate of consumption cannot date earlier tampering. Separate provisional suspicion, a laboratory finding, a medical cause and evidence of deliberate harm. If story pacing compresses a forensic turnaround, label that convention for the author; do not make a fictional deadline a medical fact. Keep solution records planner-facing and translate only chapter-available facts into briefs.

**Make room for a life worth joining.** During outline review, look across the book for genuinely wanted pleasures, excursions, work, friendships and obligations, including changes of company and location. A game, meal, toy or outing may affect a decision or an encounter without producing a clue. Do not add one example of every card trait or force every pleasure to earn its place through investigative usefulness. If all allocated space belongs to clue delivery and emotional fallout, revise the encounters rather than ordering the novelist to insert joy between them.

**Companionship is substantive, not leftover space.** Protect conversations the reader enjoys for these particular people, not just for their information. A principal encounter should change the situation, but each exchange need not advance the case, impose a cost or produce insight. Physical appetite and conversational play can reinforce each other. When compressing, select the exchange that carries pleasure or relationship and cut redundant explanation or business around it. Do not keep every plot item by reducing every person to efficient answers. Familiar relationships can appear across the sequence; there is no requirement to introduce the entire recurring cast in the opening.

**Leave the novelist room to create the event.** An important host, performance or celebration must be interesting in its own right; cards and samples supply ranges, not lines to copy. A brief can require that a person visibly wins interest or meets resistance without dictating how. Protect the truth and necessary evidence when a better scene changes the means; reconcile the plan to that better scene instead of repairing the prose back into a checklist.

**Review both experience and inference.** Ask what the reader is enjoying, wanting and expecting before asking what has been planted. Then ask what explanation a first-time reader would now suspect, and which repetitions or juxtapositions suggest it. Fair play is not a requirement to spotlight each mechanism. Reading-time proximity and event-time proximity are different; see `storygrid-commandments.md`.

These are planning decisions and review lenses, not compulsory questions to dramatise in every chapter. The commissioned outline should preserve a causal spine while leaving room for character-led discovery.
