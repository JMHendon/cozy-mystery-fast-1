# Archie Fairweather — the lead

## Reference character (copy this section word for word into every drafting and revision brief)

**Archie is modelled on Richard Castle from *Castle* (ABC, 2009–16).** His job, age and setting differ; the energy does not. On the page that means:

- **He enters at the top of his game.** The world's most famous magician, rich, adored, delighted with his life. Leaving Las Vegas was his choice and he's having a wonderful time: he bought a theater, a town and a hearse. The case is a new toy, not penance.
- **He is the funniest, most delighted person in most rooms,** and his wit is in his own dialogue, said to people's faces, not parked in the narration.
- **He is thrilled by murder in a slightly inappropriate way,** and says so. An impossible crime is the best present anyone has ever given him.
- **He theorises out loud, constantly:** wild, entertaining, delivered with total conviction and real reasoning ("Hear me out: smugglers"). Often wrong; sometimes exactly right before anyone can see it. Tess swats them.
- **He is often right, and annoying about it.** A magician's eye (misdirection, method, how a room is managed) wins real points in the case. He sees how impossible things are done because he does them for a living.
- **He uses fame, money and connections freely, and it usually works.** A favour from a famous friend, an extravagant delivery, the best lawyer in New England on speed dial. People have a wonderful time.
- **Vain and self-deprecating at once.** He knows he's Archie Fairweather and enjoys it; he also loves a joke at his own expense. He flirts, especially with Tess, who doesn't want him to. Brave without thinking about it.
- **At home he's the kid and his family are the grown-ups,** but he is a devoted, tender father to June.
- **His flaws can cost him,** but the book never treats his size, appetite, generosity or showmanship as the thing to cure. He grows; he doesn't shrink.

**Tests for every chapter:** "Would Castle say this out loud?" and "Would a reader want to *be* Archie in this chapter?"

## Identity

**Archibald Augustus Fairweather, 47.** The most famous magician in the world. Stage name inherited from his grandfather: *the Great Fairweather.*

**Look.** Six-one, long-limbed, built like a heron that does Pilates. A thick mess of dark hair going silver at the temples that he will not cut ("It's part of the act." "You don't have an act anymore." "Then it's part of the man."). A face mostly made of grin, deep laugh lines, very fast, very dark eyes. Beautiful, restless hands, always doing something: a coin walking across the knuckles, a card appearing between two fingers, a pen spinning. Dresses like he got lost on the way to a better century and stopped at an outdoor-gear store: velvet jackets and brocade waistcoats over Henley shirts, a moth-eaten emerald scarf of Gus's that's eight feet long, expensive sneakers, a vintage watch he winds when thinking. In Maine he has added a red-and-black buffalo-check wool coat he bought on day one and wears with total sincerity.

**History.** Born in Boston; raised by his mother Vivienne; father "a trombonist, allegedly." Summers in Merrow with his grandfather Gus, who taught him his first trick at four and called him "Sparrow" (small, loud, always hungry); Archie has forgotten the nickname. He was ten, in the second row, on Halloween night 1988 when Gus played his last show at the Merrow Opera House, walked down to the harbor and was never seen again. Street magic in Harvard Square at nineteen; cruise ships; Vegas at twenty-six; network specials by thirty (*Archie Fairweather: Impossible*). Walked through Fenway's Green Monster. Vanished the *Delta Belle* riverboat on live TV. Escaped from a sealed safe at the bottom of Lake Tahoe. Also America's most famous debunker of psychics: **the Fairweather Challenge**, a million dollars to anyone who can demonstrate a paranormal power under his controls, never paid out. Divorced, fondly, from Simone, a French-Canadian aerialist from his show, now remarried in Montreal. One daughter, June.

**Why Merrow.** Last spring June, fifteen, told him (not angrily, which was worse) that she had seen him more often on television than in person. At the end of his 3,000th Vegas show he stepped into a glass box, vanished, and flew to Boston to sit in the third row of her debate final, which he'd missed three years running. When Simone moved to Montreal, June didn't want to go. Archie promised her "a real year in a real place." Vivienne suggested Merrow: Gus's house had sat empty for thirty-seven years. They arrived in August for June's junior year. In September Archie outbid a Boston developer for the town's crumbling 1899 opera house (deed-restricted as a theater forever; town meeting vote 312–41; the sale closes in mid-November). It is now late October.

**Want (Book 1).** To make this the best year of June's life, through spectacle, because spectacle is the love language he's fluent in. To beat Lorelei Crane in his grandfather's theater. And, secretly and embarrassingly, for something to be real: "I've debunked four hundred mediums. I would give everything I own for one of them to be the real thing."

**Need.** To let the people he loves backstage. He keeps everyone in the audience, even June, and calls it a gift. (This is the internal arc. It never shrinks him; he learns to share the method, not to stop performing.)

**Appetite (the engine).** Archie cannot leave a locked thing locked. A mystery is not a problem to him; it's a present someone forgot to hand him. Interest becomes participation instantly: he asks, tries, joins, recruits, buys, climbs, bets. He takes things apart to see how they work: tricks, locks, people. He *wants* things: the best pie, the biggest pumpkin, to haul a lobster trap himself, to win at poker, to make a sad person laugh. He enjoys things out loud.

## Signature moves (use at full size; vary them)

- **The theory.** A wildly improbable explanation, delivered with total conviction and real reasoning.
- **The demonstration.** He shows how a "miracle" was done, in a diner, a hospital waiting room, a squad car, turning the room into his audience. *Group-scene engine:* he starts a public game the room must join (pick a card, place a bet, hold this, watch closely). People join, resist, heckle or ignore him.
- **The lift.** Pickpocketing as argument; he returns the thing with a flourish. Tess is the one person who might catch him.
- **The read.** He cold-reads a person, accurately and intrusively, as kindness and surveillance in one gesture. It can wound.
- **The gift.** Money, access, connections, an extravagant meal, a favour from a famous friend. Often it simply works and everyone has a wonderful time. Occasionally it's the wrong gift.
- **The sincerity drop.** The patter stops mid-sentence and he says one true, plain thing. Rare, so it lands.

## Competence and limits

**Elite:** sleight of hand, lock-picking, escapes (straitjackets, water tanks; he can hold his breath for four minutes), misdirection theory, reading an audience, cold reading, illusion design (with Lenny), stagecraft, fearless with heights and water, memory (a "memory theater" technique). He knows a great deal about poisons from building a Borgia routine years ago.

**Limits (comic, human, never humiliating):** can't cook (tries elaborate breakfasts; mostly fails), can't drive stick, hopeless at actual police procedure, impatient, overconfident, talks too much when nervous, seasick on small boats, a terrible liar *to people he loves* (he can fool a stadium but not his mother), can't keep a surprise, mistakes a grand gesture for an answer, loses at poker because he refuses to cheat ("It's against the code") and can't stop telegraphing joy at a good hand. Sometimes confidently, charmingly wrong about a person.

## Voice

Exuberant, quick, warm, playful. Showman's patter deployed at people ("Ladies and gentlemen—" to an audience of one irritated detective). Extravagant vocabulary for comic effect, never pompous. Questions as games. Callbacks. Nicknames: "Sergeant" (to Tess, with relish), "Junebug" (June hates it), "Mother" (when she's being outrageous), "Lenny" (never shortened further). Boston-raised but long gone; "wicked" only under stress. Never swears: "Holy *Houdini*" and similar, sparingly. Never cruel; his wit punches up or at himself. Does not make speeches about the theme or explain his own psychology.

- *Peak:* "Sergeant, I have spent thirty years making things impossible for a living. This isn't impossible. This is *rude.*"
- *Delight:* "Somebody committed a murder *with a séance trumpet.* In my theater. Do you understand what a gift this is? — I'm going to stop saying it's a gift."
- *Theory:* "Hear me out. The opera house was a rum-runner's drop in 1924."
- *Wrong:* "It's Warren. It's obviously Warren. Look at his *shoes.*"
- *Plain:* "I don't know. I really don't know." / "Is she going to be all right?" / "Thanks, Bev."

## Wound (never diagnosed on the page)

Everyone he has loved has had a front-row seat and no backstage pass. He was the boy in the second row the night his grandfather vanished, and he has never stopped trying to work out the trick. He does not say this.
