# Chapter 2

*Monday, October 27 · 9:05 p.m. · The hospital in Rockport*

The family room off the emergency department had a watercolor of a lighthouse on every wall, four of them, a box of tissues on every surface, and a coffee machine that said OUT OF ORDER in three different handwritings. Across the corridor was a pair of swinging doors with small square windows, through which, every so often, Archie could see people running.

He was not in the family room. He was in the corridor outside it, as close to the swinging doors as the charge nurse would let him stand, which was not close.

"Sir. I need you behind the line."

"I'm behind the line." He looked down. There was a line, yellow, on the linoleum. He was on it. "I'm *on* the line. That's practically behind."

"Sir."

"Archie." He held out a hand. She didn't take it. "I know, I know, you don't care who I am. I respect that enormously. Could you tell me one thing? Has anybody said the word *aconite* in there?"

She looked at him for a long second, then at the doors. "The doctor's busy."

"I know he is. He's busy because her heart's doing something stupid that doesn't respond to anything, and the shocks aren't holding. Am I right?"

That got him a different look entirely. She went through the doors.

He had driven the hearse behind the ambulance from Merrow at a speed Lenny would not have approved of, with his mother holding her hat on in silence the whole way. Vivienne was in the family room now, sitting beside Ivy Crane, not touching her, which for Vivienne was a form of prayer.

Ivy sat with her coat buttoned to the throat and both hands deep in her pockets. She hadn't taken the coat off. Archie had noticed that the way he noticed everything, without deciding to. She hadn't cried. She was staring at the lighthouse over the coffee machine as if it owed her money.

Across the room, Teddy Marchand walked up and down in his fleece vest and white sneakers, a phone at each ear.

"Medical event," he was saying into the left one. "A *medical event*, Jen. No. No. We do not say that word. Nobody says that word." Into the right one: "I don't know, she's in with the doctors, I'll call you back." He lowered both and looked at Archie with naked resentment. "Somebody told the chief it was *poison*. In front of a *superfan* with a *phone*."

"That was me."

"I know it was you." Teddy pointed one phone at him. "You understand that clip is going to be everywhere by midnight? Archie Fairweather screaming poison at Lorelei Crane's séance? The special airs Friday. Halloween night. We had a *plan*."

"Teddy," Archie said, as gently as he could, "she might be dying."

Teddy opened his mouth, closed it, and went back to the window with both phones.

The swinging doors banged open and a doctor came through them: forties, scrubs, a stethoscope worn like a scarf. His badge said RAO.

"Who's Fairweather?"

"Me." Archie stepped off the line entirely.

"You told the police chief aconite."

"I did."

"Why?"

"Numbness of the lips first. Then tingling, spreading over the face, she said *pins*. Then her voice went, thick, slurring. Then she collapsed. And now, I'd bet, a ventricular rhythm you can't shock out of." Archie had stopped smiling somewhere in the middle of it. "Aconitine. Monkshood. It won't be on your routine screen. Somebody has to ask for it."

"Who are you?" said Dr. Rao.

"Archie Fairweather."

"I meant medically."

"Medically I'm nobody. I did a Borgia routine in Las Vegas. You'd be amazed what you pick up."

Rao looked at him the way doctors look at an X-ray that shows something unexpected. "Did you eat or drink anything with her tonight?"

"Chamomile. Out of the same thermos. Different cups."

"Any numbness? Tingling? Your mouth?"

"Only emotionally."

The doctor didn't smile, but he took Archie's chin in one gloved hand and tipped it up toward the ceiling light, looked, pressed two fingers to his wrist for a count of ten, let go. "If anything changes, you come straight back. Not in an hour. Straight back."

"Is she going to be all right?"

Rao was already turning. "We're treating the rhythm," he said, and the doors swung shut behind him.

Archie went and sat down next to his mother. "She'll make it," he told her. "She's too good to die in a room with four lighthouses."

Vivienne didn't answer.

That was when the detective arrived.

He knew she was a detective by the way she came through the outer door: no hurry, no wasted motion, car keys still in her hand, scanning the room left to right the way he scanned an audience. Five-seven or so, a runner's build, dark-blonde hair in a braid that had clearly been a professional decision, a charcoal wool coat, good boots. Gray eyes. She took in the lighthouses, Teddy, Vivienne, Ivy, the nurse, and finally Archie, and her face did nothing at all.

"Detective Sergeant Calloway. State Police, Major Crimes." She showed a badge to the room in general. "Who's next of kin?"

"I am." Ivy didn't look up.

"I'm sorry for what you're going through, Ms. Crane. I'll need to talk to you, not now." The gray eyes moved to Archie. "You're the one who told Chief Pinkham it was poison."

"Sergeant!" Archie said, with genuine joy. "Thank God. Somebody who can order things."

"I asked you a question."

"I'm the one who knows it was poison."

"Do you," said Sergeant Calloway, and there was no question mark on it anywhere.

He'd been arrested twice on purpose for television and once, in Reno, by accident. Most police, meeting him, asked for a selfie or made a point of not asking. She did neither. She just waited.

"I'd shake your hand, Sergeant," Archie said, "but you look like you'd count your fingers afterward."

"I would."

"There's a sticker on your coat," Archie said, before he could stop himself. A little round visitor's badge, the kind they hand out at front desks, half peeled. SEA VIEW. "You were visiting someone. Ten minutes away, so they called you. You grew up in Merrow, because the chief called you Tess on the radio and you didn't correct him. And whoever you were visiting…"

"Don't," she said.

It was very quiet. She didn't raise her voice. She peeled the sticker off her coat and put it in her pocket.

"Sorry," Archie said, and meant it. "Occupational hazard."

"Find a different occupation." She turned to Ivy. "Ms. Crane, I only need one thing tonight. When the lights went out, who was sitting beside your mother?"

"Me," said Ivy. "On her left. The widow on her right."

"And during the séance, did either of you let go of her hands?"

"No." Ivy finally lifted her eyes. They were dry and very steady. "She held my hand. The whole time."

Archie felt it go into him like a splinter. Ivy's chair creaking at seven minutes past eight. The click of moonstone on oak. The bell walking. The trumpet floating in her mother's free left hand. And two seconds of gray silk, fast and practiced, in the white light, while everybody else looked at Lorelei.

He said nothing. Sergeant Calloway, writing, glanced at him once, sideways, and then back at her notebook.

At 9:42 by the clock over the door, the swinging doors opened again and Dr. Rao came through them slowly, and Archie knew. He knew from the walk. He had watched a thousand people walk onto a stage and he knew the walk of someone carrying something heavy they were about to put down.

"Ms. Crane?" Rao went to Ivy and crouched in front of her chair so their eyes were level. "I'm very sorry. We weren't able to restore a normal rhythm. Your mother died at nine-forty."

Ivy didn't move. Then she said, "Okay," in a perfectly ordinary voice, and folded forward over her own knees as if someone had cut a string. Vivienne's hand came down on her back and stayed there, and this time Ivy let it.

Teddy said, "Oh, no. Oh, *Lori*," and sat down hard on a chair, both phones in his lap, and for a moment he looked exactly his age.

Archie stood in the middle of the four lighthouses and found he had nothing to say. Ten minutes ago he had promised his mother she'd make it. He had spent forty minutes in the dark beating her. He had been going to buy her lobster.

Then he walked across the room and said quietly, "Sergeant. A word."

She looked at him for a beat, then stepped out into the corridor. He followed. The charge nurse watched them from her station like a referee.

"When the lights came up," Archie said, low and fast, "I was on the floor beside Lorelei. Ivy picked up the trumpet and wiped the narrow end with her silk scarf. Gray silk, the one she wears round her neck. Two seconds. She knew exactly what she was doing. She pushed it into her coat pocket. Left side. She hasn't taken the coat off since." He took a breath. "And she didn't hold her mother's hand the whole time. She let go at seven minutes past eight. Somebody had to, or the trumpet couldn't fly."

Calloway's face didn't change. "Is that a statement, Mr. Fairweather?"

"It's a gift. You'll want to put a bow on it."

"You've just told me a woman whose mother died three minutes ago is a murderer."

"No." The patter went out of him all at once. "I told you what she did. I don't know why she did it. There are reasons a medium's assistant wipes a trumpet that have nothing to do with poison. That's your job. Mine was noticing." He nodded at the doors. "And somebody has to ask for the screen. Antemortem blood. Aconitine, specifically. If nobody asks tonight, it'll be a heart attack in a dark room and the fans will say the spirits took her, and nobody will ever look."

She studied him the way she'd studied the room: left to right, no hurry. He had the strange sensation of being read by someone who did it differently than he did, and better than he'd like.

Then she turned away from him without a word, caught Dr. Rao at the doors, and said, "Doctor. Hold her blood, please, everything you drew before she died. The ME will be calling about an unattended death. I want it flagged for a targeted screen. Aconitine. Tell them I asked."

Rao glanced past her at Archie. "Already drawn. I'll flag it."

"Thank you." She left a card with the charge nurse and came back up the corridor, putting her notebook away. She stopped in front of him. Up close, the gray eyes were tired, and something in them was still angry about the sticker.

"Go home, Mr. Fairweather."

"I'll need to give a statement."

"You'll give it tomorrow. You'll give it to me. Go home."

He nodded. He'd won the only thing he had come for. It didn't feel like winning.

"I liked her," he said.

Sergeant Calloway's eyebrow went up a sixteenth of an inch, and stayed there, and she didn't say anything at all.

Out in the parking lot the hearse was waiting under a sodium light, long and black and absurd, with THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER — MARVELS & MYSTERIES in gold leaf down its side. Somewhere a dog started barking at it. Archie stood beside it in the cold and waited for his mother, and turned a quarter over and over across his knuckles, and thought about a voice in the dark saying *Sparrow*.
