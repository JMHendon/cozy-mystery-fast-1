# Chapter 2

*Monday, October 27 · 9:05 p.m. · The hospital in Rockport*

Through the small square window in the swinging doors, Archie could see Lorelei's feet.

That was all. Two bare feet at the end of a gurney, very white, and around them people in blue moving fast. A monitor he couldn't see made a sound he didn't like. Someone called out a number, someone else said "Clear," and the feet jumped.

"Sir. I need you behind the line."

He looked down. There was a yellow line on the linoleum, and he was standing on it. "I'm *on* the line. That's practically behind."

"Sir." The charge nurse had the build and the patience of a woman who had been moving people off that line for twenty years.

"Has anybody in there said *aconite*? Because somebody needs to. It's not on your routine screen. If nobody asks for it—"

"The doctor's busy."

"I know he's busy. He's busy because her heart's doing something stupid and the shocks aren't holding." The feet jumped again. "Am I right?"

She looked at him for a long second and didn't answer, which was an answer.

Behind him was the family room. It had a watercolor of a lighthouse on each of its four walls and a coffee machine that said OUT OF ORDER in three different handwritings. Vivienne sat beside Ivy Crane, not touching her, which for Vivienne was a form of prayer. Ivy had her coat buttoned to the throat and both hands deep in its pockets. She hadn't taken it off. She hadn't cried. She was staring at the lighthouse over the coffee machine as if it owed her money.

Teddy Marchand walked up and down in his fleece vest and white sneakers with a phone at each ear. "Medical event," he was saying. "A *medical event*, Jen. We do not say that word. Nobody says that word." He lowered both phones and glared at Archie through the doorway. "Somebody told the chief it was *poison*. In front of a *superfan*. With a *phone*."

"That was me."

"I know it was you! The special airs Friday. Halloween night. We had a *plan*." He jabbed a phone at Archie. "And legal wants to know about your million. Technically, something just happened in that room you can't explain."

"Teddy," Archie said, "she might be dying."

Teddy opened his mouth, shut it, and turned to the window.

Through the little square of glass, the people in blue were doing it again. Nobody was looking at the door. Nobody was going to come out and ask the man on the yellow line what he'd seen. They were treating a heart. By the time a lab ran a screen nobody had asked for, there'd be nothing left to treat.

Archie went through the doors.

"Excuse me. Sorry. Hello." The room was blazing white and crowded and smelled of alcohol and hot dust. Lorelei lay on the gurney with a mask over her face and her dark-red lipstick smeared up one cheek. "Archie Fairweather, huge admirer of medicine. It's *aconite*. Aconitine. Monkshood. The mouth goes numb first, then the face, then the voice, then the heart. I watched it happen in that order. You can't shock it out, you have to—"

A hand closed on the back of his collar. It belonged to a security guard roughly the size of a vending machine.

"Out."

"Absolutely. One second." Archie pointed past him at the doctor by the bed, a man in his forties with a stethoscope worn like a scarf. "Her *lips*, Doctor. Look at her lips."

The doctor didn't look up from the monitor. "Wait. Say that again."

"Aconitine. From monkshood. Numb mouth first."

"Got it. Get him out of here. Thank you. *Out.*"

The guard took him out by both arms, as easily as moving a stepladder. He set Archie against the corridor wall and stood in front of him.

That was where he was when the detective arrived.

She came in through the outer door without hurry and without wasted motion, car keys still in one hand. She scanned the corridor from left to right, the way Archie scanned an audience. She was five-seven, with a runner's build and a dark-blonde braid down her back, beaded with fog from the parking lot. She wore a charcoal wool coat and good boots. Her eyes were gray. They went over the nurse, the guard and the doors. Then they reached Archie, flattened against the wall with Gus's eight-foot emerald scarf hanging off one shoulder, and stayed on him a moment longer than he liked.

"Ray," she said to the guard.

"Tess."

"I'll take him." She took Archie by the elbow, the way you'd move a coat rack, and set him a yard down the corridor. The guard went back to his post. Archie straightened his scarf.

"Sergeant!" he said. "Thank God. Somebody who can order things."

"Detective Sergeant Calloway. State Police, Major Crimes." She didn't offer her hand. "You're the one who told Dale Pinkham it was poison."

"I'm the one who knows it was."

"Do you," she said, with no question mark on it anywhere.

"I'd shake your hand," Archie said, "but you look like you'd count your fingers afterward."

"I would."

"Smart. Most people forget the watch." He held up a plain steel watch on a black strap and turned it so she could read the face. Then he held it out to her. "It's nine-nineteen."

She looked at her bare wrist. Then at the watch. She took it back without a word and put it on. For the first time, something in her gray eyes was paying attention.

He liked her immediately. He liked her so much that he did the thing he should never do on a first meeting: he looked properly. A little round visitor's sticker, half peeled off, was stuck to her coat. It said SEA VIEW, the memory-care home ten minutes away. She'd been close by when the call came. The guard knew her first name, and she knew his.

"You grew up in Merrow," he said. "You were visiting someone tonight. And whoever you were visiting—"

"Don't."

She didn't raise her voice. She peeled the sticker off, put it in her pocket and walked past him into the family room without another word. It was the first time in a long while that Archie had been left standing in a corridor with a sentence still in his mouth.

"Ms. Crane," she was saying. "I'm sorry. I won't keep you. When the lights went out, who was beside your mother?"

"Me. On her left. The widow on her right."

"Did either of you let go of her hands?"

Ivy lifted her eyes for the first time. They were dry and very steady. "She held my hand. The whole time."

It went into Archie like a splinter. A chair creaking at seven minutes past eight. Moonstone clicking on oak. A bell walking across a table on a rod, and a trumpet floating in a hand that wasn't being held. And two seconds of gray silk in the white light, fast and practiced, while everyone else looked at Lorelei.

He said nothing. Sergeant Calloway, writing, glanced at him once, sideways.

He went and sat down next to his mother. "She'll make it," he told her quietly. "She's too good to die in a room with four lighthouses."

Vivienne didn't answer.

At 9:42 the swinging doors opened, and the doctor came through them slowly. Archie knew at once. He had watched a thousand people walk onto a stage, and he knew the walk of someone carrying something heavy they were about to set down.

"Ms. Crane?" The doctor's badge said RAO. He crouched in front of Ivy's chair so that their eyes were level. "I'm very sorry. We couldn't restore a normal rhythm. Your mother died at nine-forty."

Ivy didn't move. Then she said, "Okay," in a perfectly ordinary voice, and folded forward over her knees as if a string had been cut. Vivienne's hand came down on her back, and this time Ivy let it stay.

Teddy said, "Oh, no. Oh, *Lori*," and sat down hard, both phones in his lap.

Dr. Rao stood. On his way back to the doors he stopped beside Archie.

"Whatever you think it was," he said, low, "nothing we did touched it."

Archie nodded. Ten minutes ago he had promised his mother she'd make it. An hour ago he had heard her voice start to go and admired the craft.

Then he got up and said, "Sergeant. A word."

She looked at him for a beat and stepped out into the corridor.

"Ask her about her scarf," Archie said, low. "Gray silk. When the lights came up, she wiped the narrow end of that trumpet with it. Two seconds, very practiced. Then she put it in her left coat pocket, and she hasn't taken the coat off since. And ask her why she let go of her mother's hand at seven minutes past eight. Somebody had to, or nothing in that room could fly."

Calloway kept writing. "Is that a statement, Mr. Fairweather?"

"It's a free sample. The full statement comes tomorrow, with a lawyer and a better jacket."

"You're telling me a woman whose mother died three minutes ago is a murderer."

"No." The patter went out of him all at once. "I'm telling you what she did. I don't know why. There are reasons a medium's assistant wipes a trumpet that have nothing to do with poison. Why is your job. Mine was noticing." He nodded at the doors. "And somebody has to ask for the screen tonight. Her blood, from before she died. Aconitine, by name. Otherwise it's a heart attack in a dark room, and the fans say the spirits took her, and nobody ever looks."

She studied him the way she'd studied the corridor, left to right, no hurry. He had the strange sensation of being read by someone who did it differently than he did, and better than he'd like.

Then she turned and caught Dr. Rao at the doors. "Doctor. Hold everything you drew before she died. The ME's office will call about an unattended death. I want it flagged for a targeted screen. Aconitine. Tell them I asked."

"Already drawn. I'll flag it." Rao looked past her at Archie. "And you. You drank something with her?"

"Chamomile. Different cups."

Sergeant Calloway's pen stopped.

"Any numbness?" said Rao. "Tingling? Your mouth?"

"Only emotionally."

Rao took Archie's chin in a gloved hand and tipped it toward the light. He looked, counted Archie's pulse against his watch and let go. "Anything changes, you come straight back. Not in an hour. Straight back."

He went through the doors. Calloway wrote something down, underlined it, and left a card with the charge nurse. Then she came back and stopped in front of Archie. Up close she looked tired, and something in her face was still angry about the sticker.

"Go home, Mr. Fairweather."

"I'll need to give a statement."

"Tomorrow. To me. Go home."

He'd won the only thing he came for.

"I liked her," he said.

Sergeant Calloway looked at him for a long moment, and didn't say anything at all.

Out in the parking lot, the hearse waited under a sodium light, long and black and absurd. THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER — MARVELS & MYSTERIES ran down its side in gold leaf. Somewhere a dog started barking at it. Archie stood beside it in the cold, waiting for his mother and walking a quarter across his knuckles. He thought about a voice in the dark saying *Sparrow*.
