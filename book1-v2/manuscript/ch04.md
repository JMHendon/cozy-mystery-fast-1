# Chapter 4

*Tuesday, October 28 · 6:00 a.m. · The Galley*

At six in the morning, the Galley was the only lit building on the wharf. It was a low, gray-shingled box with steamed-up windows and a bell over the door that rang like a ship's. At the curb the hearse idled while two dogs on the fuel dock lost their minds.

Archie came in with Bev's thermos under his arm and the cold on his coat.

"Morning, Merrow!"

Nobody looked up. Five old lobstermen in feed caps sat at the liars' table by the kitchen door. They had been there since five and would be there at noon. They went on not talking to him, as they had every morning since August.

"Sit down," said Bev Thibodeau.

He sat. Bev was sixty-seven, with reading glasses pushed up into her gray hair and forearms like a stevedore's. She had been unimpressed by him every day for three months. He adored her.

"I brought your thermos back." He set it on the counter. "With gratitude. The tea was a triumph. Diplomatically, I mean. As a beverage it was chamomile."

Bev unscrewed the cap without a word, slid the two little nested cups off the top and carried the lot to the sink behind the counter. Hot water, soap, a bottle brush, rinse. She stood the thermos upside down in the rack, the cups beside it, and dried her hands.

"Everything that comes back gets washed," she said. "Coffee?"

"Please. Bev, light of my life, what's the parliament saying?"

"The parliament's saying eat your eggs."

Chief Dale Pinkham was two stools down, hunched over a mug with his walrus mustache drooping into it. He looked as if he'd slept in the cruiser.

"State took the table," he said heavily. "Everything on it. The cups, the bell, the trumpet. Lieutenant from Augusta came in at midnight with a van." He turned the mug in his big hands. "I've got the riverboat DVD in the cruiser. Was going to ask you to sign it. Feels wrong now."

"Dale. I'll sign it on Friday. I'll sign it in gold. I'll draw a little boat." Archie swung around on his stool. The Galley was Merrow's parliament. If you wanted to know who in town had something to hide, you said a name out loud there and watched who flinched. So he said it at full volume. "Hear me out, though. Warren Gilley. Twenty minutes before the lights went out, Lorelei leaned over and whispered in his ear, and Warren went the color of skim milk. Not pale. *Translucent.* I was standing right there. What does a TV psychic whisper to the chairman of the select board that turns him into a—"

"Mr. Fairweather," said Pinkham, low.

"—into a ghost, and then, fifty minutes later, she's—"

"Mr. Fairweather."

Archie stopped. At the liars' table, one of the old men had put down his fork. He was small and neat, and he wore a bow tie under his barn coat. Archie had seen that bow tie before, on a bigger man, at every select board meeting. The old man laid three dollars beside his plate, took his cap from the hook and went out without a word. The bell rang behind him.

"That's Warren's cousin Alden," said Bev, to the coffee urn.

"Ah," said Archie.

"He'll be at Warren's house in four minutes."

"Three," said Pinkham. "He drives fast for his age."

Archie looked at the door, and then at his coffee. "Well," he said. "That's a theory with legs."

A mug landed in front of him hard enough to slop. Shannon Mayo set the cream jug beside it and turned to go. She was in her Galley apron, with her ponytail scraped back and her eyes swollen.

"Shannon," Archie said.

She stopped. When she turned around, her face was flat and closed. It was the face of a woman who had worked a double shift and come back for breakfast because her kids needed shoes.

"He talked to me," she said. Not loud. Down the counter, everybody went very interested in their toast. "Last night. For six minutes Tommy was there. He said it was warm. And you stood up and yelled for the lights."

"Shannon, she was dying."

"I know she was dying. I'm not stupid." Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "I'm saying you took him. You took him away from me in front of everybody, and now they're all going to say he was never there."

He should have said he was sorry. He knew it while he was opening his mouth. What came out instead, because his brain had been chewing on it all night, was: "How did they find you? The TV people. Did somebody call you, before last night?"

Shannon looked at him for a long second.

"April," she said. "A girl called me in April. Free reading, because of Tommy. Then I paid for three more." She picked up the coffeepot. "Is that what you wanted? You should write it down." She went down the counter filling mugs, and didn't come back.

At the liars' table somebody said, not quite under his breath, "Tommy Mayo talking through a tin horn. Lord." Somebody else chuckled.

Archie got off his stool. He walked the length of the room and stopped beside the round table, and the four remaining feed caps turned up to him.

"Gentlemen," he said pleasantly. "I've been in Merrow three months and you haven't said a word to me, and I've respected it. Here's my first word to you. That's her husband." He let it sit. "Enjoy your eggs."

Nobody laughed. Then the oldest of them, a man with a face like a split cedar shingle, said without looking up, "She's not your business, mister. You're from away."

Archie walked back to his stool. At the far end of the counter, Shannon set down the pot.

"I don't need you to do that," she said flatly, and went into the kitchen.

The bell over the door rang.

He knew who it was by the way the room changed. The liars' table looked up for the first time all morning. Pinkham sat up straight.

Sergeant Calloway stood inside the door in her charcoal coat, with a navy State Police fleece under it. She looked as if she hadn't slept either and had decided not to mention it.

"Morning, Tessie," said Bev.

"Morning, Bev. Coffee. Black." She came to the counter and didn't sit. "Mr. Fairweather. Where's the thermos you took home last night?"

"Right here. I brought it back first thing, it's Bev's—"

"Washed it," said Bev.

The Galley went completely silent.

Sergeant Calloway turned her head, slowly, and looked at the drying rack. The thermos stood upside down in it, steaming faintly, its two little cups beside it.

Archie could see exactly what she was seeing. Here was a man who had poured a dead woman a drink on camera and named the poison before any doctor did. Then he had brought the cups back at dawn to be scrubbed. There were several charming things he could say. He could feel them lining up behind his teeth.

"I brought it back at six," he said instead, "because it's Bev's, and that's what you do with things that aren't yours. Bev washed it because that's what Bev does. Everybody in this room knows that." He turned his palms up on the counter. "And I can see exactly how it looks. I'd arrest me."

Something moved at the very corner of her mouth and went away again.

"That," Archie said, pointing at it, "was nearly a smile. Bev, you saw it."

"I saw a woman with a sore tooth," said Bev.

"It was a smile. I've played to forty thousand people a night, Sergeant. I know the face a room makes right before it gives in."

"Are you finished, Mr. Fairweather?"

"Not even slightly. But I'm going to call my lawyer now, because I always know when I'm ahead."

He took out his phone, scrolled, and pressed a name. It rang four times. A woman's voice came through the speaker, sleep-thick and dangerous.

"Archie. It is six thirty-one in the morning. If this is not about a wedding cake, I'm going to have you killed."

"Margaret, good morning. I'm at a diner in Merrow with a State Police sergeant and a very clean thermos."

A pause. The sound of bedclothes being thrown back.

"Do not say one more word," said Margaret Okafor, the best criminal defense attorney in New England. "Not one. I'm getting in the car."

"Also, I'm writing a million-dollar check to her charity this morning."

"You are *not*."

"She's getting in the car," Archie told the sergeant.

"I heard." Calloway took the coffee Bev slid across to her. "Lieutenant Brannock watched the footage at two this morning. He'd like to meet you. Voluntary interview, nine o'clock, select board room at the town hall. You can say no." A beat. "He'd enjoy that."

"I'll be there. I'll bring the best lawyer in New England. She'll bring a scowl. You'll like each other." He leaned in. "And Ivy's scarf?"

"Ms. Crane declined to hand over her coat. Without a judge, it's her coat. There's a trooper outside her door at the Spindrift until a judge wakes up." She drank. "There's also an evidence technician at your house, about the flowers. Your mother's offering him scones."

"My mother can't make scones."

"He's noticed." She turned to the counter. "Bev. I need the thermos."

"It's mine," Bev said.

"Then I'll take it from you, Bev."

Bev looked at her for a long moment. Then she took a paper bag from under the counter and put the clean thermos and both clean cups into it. She folded the top over twice and handed it across.

"You were a lot more pleasant at nine," Bev said.

"I was nine." Sergeant Calloway tucked the bag under her arm. At the door she stopped. "Mr. Fairweather. Whatever you said in here before I came in, I'm going to hear about it by eight."

"Sergeant," he called, as the bell rang over her. "I'm going to be very helpful."

She didn't look back. "Don't be."

The door swung shut. A plate came down at his elbow: a slab of blueberry pie, warm, with the purple running out of it.

"I didn't order it," Archie said.

"You did," said Bev. "As a protest."

Pinkham's phone buzzed on the counter. He read it, and his mustache sank a quarter of an inch.

"That's Warren," he said. "He's calling the town attorney. Something about a clause in your sale. Says you accused him of murder in the Galley in front of witnesses."

"I said *hear me out*."

"Ayuh," said Pinkham unhappily. "We heard you."

Archie looked at the pie, and at the empty stool where Alden Gilley had been, and at the door the sergeant had gone out of. Then he picked up his fork.

"Then I'd better be right," he said.
