# Chapter 4

*Tuesday, October 28 · 6:00 a.m. · The Galley*

At six in the morning the Galley was the only lit thing on the wharf, a low gray-shingled box with steamed-up windows and a bell over the door that rang like a ship's. In front of it, at the curb, the hearse idled while two dogs on the fuel dock lost their minds.

Archie came in with Bev's thermos under his arm and the cold on his coat.

"Morning, Merrow!"

Nobody looked up. The liars' table by the kitchen door, five old lobstermen in feed caps who had been sitting there since five and would be sitting there at noon, went on not talking to him, as it had every morning since August.

"Sit down," said Bev Thibodeau.

He sat. Bev was sixty-seven, with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair and forearms like a stevedore's, and she had been unimpressed by him every day for three months. He adored her.

"I brought your thermos back." He set it on the counter. "With gratitude. The tea was a triumph. Diplomatically, I mean. As a beverage it was chamomile."

Bev picked it up without a word, unscrewed the cap, slid the two little nested cups off the top and carried the lot to the sink behind the counter. Hot water, soap, a bottle brush, rinse. She stood the thermos upside down in the rack and the cups beside it, and dried her hands.

"Everything that comes back gets washed," she said, in answer to nothing. "Coffee?"

"Please. Bev, light of my life, what's the word?" He tipped his head at the liars' table. "What's the parliament saying?"

"The word is eat your eggs."

"I haven't ordered eggs."

"Then the word is order eggs."

Chief Dale Pinkham was two stools down, in his uniform jacket that didn't close, hunched over a mug with his walrus mustache drooping into it. He looked like he'd slept in the cruiser, which he probably had.

"Morning, Mr. Fairweather," he said heavily. "State took the table. Everything on it. The cups, the bell, the trumpet. Lieutenant from Augusta came in at midnight with a van." He turned the mug in his big hands. "I've got the riverboat DVD in the cruiser. Was going to ask you to sign it." He shook his head. "Feels wrong now. And Warren Gilley's been on the phone to the town attorney since midnight. Something about a clause."

"What clause?"

"The kind with your name in it." Pinkham looked unhappy. "The sale doesn't close till the fifteenth."

"Dale. I'll sign the riverboat on Friday. I'll sign it in gold. I'll draw a little boat." Archie spun on his stool. "Hear me out, though. The producer. Teddy. Who insures a TV medium? Somebody does. Somebody always does. Eight seasons, a streaming hit, one woman in front of the camera: there's a policy on her, I'd bet the hearse. Now say she wanted out—"

"She did," said Pinkham.

Archie stopped spinning. "She did?"

"Ayuh. Fan page said so. Last season. She was going to retire." Pinkham went pink above the mustache. "I follow the fan page."

"I made that up," Archie said. "I made that up *thirty seconds ago*."

"Pie's blueberry or apple," said Bev.

"I'm in the middle of a theory. It's a *good* theory."

"There's no third pie."

A mug of coffee landed in front of him hard enough to slop. Shannon Mayo, in her Galley apron, her ponytail scraped back and her eyes swollen, set down the cream jug beside it and started to walk away.

"Shannon," Archie said.

She stopped. When she turned round, her face was flat and closed, the face of a woman who had worked a double and come back for the breakfast shift because her kids needed shoes.

"He talked to me," she said. Not loud. Down the counter, everybody went very interested in their toast. "Last night. For six minutes Tommy was there. He said it was warm. And you stood up and yelled for the lights."

"Shannon, she was dying."

"I know she was dying. I'm not stupid." Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "I'm saying you took him. You took him away from me in front of everybody, and now they're all going to say he was never there." She picked up the coffeepot. "You want eggs?"

Archie found he couldn't say anything at all. She waited a second, nodded as if that were an answer, and went down the counter filling mugs.

At the liars' table, one of the old men said, not quite under his breath, "Tommy Mayo talking through a tin horn. Lord." Somebody chuckled.

Archie got off his stool. He walked the length of the room, and stopped beside the round table, and the five feed caps turned up to him.

"Gentlemen," he said pleasantly. "I've been in Merrow three months and you haven't said a word to me, and I've respected it. Here's my first word to you. That's her husband." He let it sit. "Enjoy your eggs."

Nobody at the round table laughed. Then the oldest of them, a man with a face like a split cedar shingle, said without looking up, "She's not your business, mister. You're from away."

Archie walked back to his stool. At the far end of the counter, Shannon set down the pot.

"I don't need you to do that," she said flatly, and went into the kitchen.

The bell over the door rang.

"Morning, Bev. Morning, Dale. Morning, Shannon, love." A short, sturdy woman in a man's oilskin stamped her boots on the mat: white hair cropped like a boy's, reading glasses on a beaded chain, fingerless wool gloves. Hollis Tibbetts had taught English at the regional high school for thirty-five years and ran the Merrow Players, and half the people in the room had once handed her an essay late.

She squeezed Shannon's arm on her way past. Bev was already setting a pink pie box on the counter for her: the costume crew's breakfast.

Hollis looked Archie up and down. "Sit up straight. You look like a boy who's lost his homework."

"I didn't sleep."

"Nobody slept. Half the town was up watching that boy's video."

"Hollis." He turned on his stool to face her. "I should have cancelled it."

"Oh, don't you start."

"In September. I wanted to fight the town and call it off, and you—"

"I told you, 'Don't cancel her. Beat her. In Gus's house.'" She said it the way she'd quote back a line he'd failed to learn. "I know what I said. I don't grade my own papers twice." She picked up the pie box. "Shannon, I'll have Liam and Rosie tomorrow after school like we said. Bev. Dale." And she was gone in a gust of cold.

Archie stared at the door.

"Blueberry, please," he said to Bev. "For breakfast. As a protest."

Bev was reaching for the pie case when the bell rang again.

He knew who it was before he turned round, by the way the room changed. The liars' table looked up for the first time all morning. Pinkham sat up straight. Shannon stopped pouring.

Sergeant Calloway stood inside the door in the charcoal coat with her braid down her back and a navy State Police fleece under it, and she looked as if she hadn't slept either, and as if she'd decided not to mention it.

"Morning, Tessie," said Bev.

"Morning, Bev. Coffee. Black." She came to the counter. She didn't sit. She looked at Archie the way she'd looked at him in the hospital corridor, as if he were a statement she hadn't finished reading. "Mr. Fairweather."

"Sergeant! You came for breakfast. I'm touched. I recommend the blueberry."

"Where's the thermos you took home last night?"

"Right here. I brought it back first thing, it's Bev's—"

"Washed it," said Bev.

The Galley went completely silent.

Sergeant Calloway turned her head, slowly, and looked at the drying rack. The thermos stood upside down in it, steaming faintly, gleaming, its two little cups beside it like a pair of clean teeth.

Archie followed her gaze. Then he looked back at her. He could see exactly what she was seeing: a man who'd poured a dead woman a drink on camera, named the poison before any doctor had, and brought the cups back at dawn to be scrubbed.

There were several charming things he could say. He could feel them lining up behind his teeth.

"I brought it back at six," he said instead, "because it's Bev's, and that's what you do with things that aren't yours. Bev washed it because that's what Bev does. Everybody in this room knows that. That's not on Bev." He turned his palms up on the counter. "And I can see exactly how it looks. I'd arrest me."

Something moved at the very corner of her mouth and went away again.

He took out his phone, scrolled, and pressed a name. It rang four times. A woman's voice came through the speaker, sleep-thick and dangerous.

"Archie. It is six thirty-one in the morning. If this is not about a wedding cake, I'm going to have you killed."

"Margaret, good morning. I'm at a diner in Merrow with a State Police sergeant and a very clean thermos."

A pause. The sound of bedclothes being thrown back.

"Do not say one more word," said Margaret Okafor, the best criminal defense attorney in New England. "Not one. I'm getting in the car."

"Also, I'm writing a million-dollar check to her charity this morning."

"You are *not*."

"She's getting in the car," Archie told the sergeant.

"I heard." Calloway took the coffee Bev slid across to her. "Lieutenant Brannock watched the footage at two this morning. He'd like to meet you. Voluntary interview, nine o'clock, select board room at the town hall. You can say no." A beat. "He'd enjoy that."

"And the scarf?" Archie said. "Ivy's scarf?"

"Ms. Crane declined to hand over her coat. Without a judge, it's her coat." She drank. "There's a trooper outside her door at the Spindrift until a judge wakes up."

"I'll be there. I'll bring the best lawyer in New England. She'll bring a scowl. You'll like each other."

"There's an evidence technician at your house right now, about the flowers. Your mother's offering him scones."

"My mother can't make scones."

"He's noticed." She turned to the counter. "Bev. I need the thermos."

"It's mine," Bev said.

"Then I'll take it from you, Bev." Calloway held out her hand.

Bev looked at her for a long moment. Then she took a paper bag from under the counter, put the clean thermos and both clean cups into it, folded the top over twice and handed it across.

"You were a lot more pleasant at nine," Bev said.

"I was nine."

"Sergeant," Archie said. "Isn't this a conflict? Your hometown. Your diner. Your Bev."

"My lieutenant thinks it's an asset."

"And what do you think?"

"I think I'll find out." Sergeant Calloway tucked the bag under her arm and turned to go. Archie swiveled his whole stool to follow her.

"Sergeant," he called, as the bell rang over her. "I'm going to be very helpful."

She didn't look back. "Don't be."

The door swung shut. The liars' table went back to not talking to him. A plate came down at his elbow: a slab of blueberry pie, warm, with the purple running out of it.

"I didn't order it," Archie said.

"You did," said Bev. "As a protest."

Archie picked up his fork and looked at the door the sergeant had gone out of. "Two and a half hours till nine," he said. "Plenty of time to solve a murder."

Bev went back to her urn. Down the counter, very quietly, Pinkham said, "Ayuh," like a man placing a bet.
