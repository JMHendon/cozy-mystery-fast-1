# Chapter 4

*Tuesday, October 28 · 6:00 a.m. · The Galley*

At six in the morning the Galley was the only lit thing on the wharf, a low gray-shingled box with steamed-up windows and a bell over the door that rang like a ship's. Behind it the harbor was black glass. In front of it, at the curb, the hearse idled while two dogs on the fuel dock lost their minds.

Archie came in with Bev's thermos under his arm and the cold on his coat.

"Morning, Merrow!"

Nobody looked up. The liars' table by the kitchen door, five old lobstermen in feed caps who had been sitting there since five and would be sitting there at noon, went on not talking to him with the steady dedication of men who had not talked to him since August. Along the counter, two men from the co-op and a woman in scrubs studied their eggs.

"Sit down," said Bev Thibodeau.

He sat. Bev was sixty-seven, Franco-American, with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair and forearms like a stevedore's, and she had been unimpressed by him every day for three months. He loved her more than he could say.

"I brought your thermos back." He set it on the counter. "With gratitude. The tea was a triumph. Diplomatically, I mean. As a beverage it was chamomile."

Bev picked it up without a word, unscrewed the cap, slid the two little nested cups off the top and carried the lot to the sink behind the counter. Hot water, soap, a bottle brush, rinse. She stood the thermos upside down in the rack and the cups beside it, and dried her hands.

"Everything that comes back gets washed," she said, in answer to nothing. "Coffee?"

"Please. Bev, light of my life, what's the word?" He tipped his head at the liars' table. "What's the parliament saying?"

"The word is eat your eggs."

"I haven't ordered eggs."

"Then the word is order eggs."

Chief Dale Pinkham was two stools down, in his uniform jacket that didn't close, hunched over a mug with his walrus mustache drooping into it. He looked like he'd slept in the cruiser, which he probably had.

"Morning, Mr. Fairweather," he said heavily. "State took the table. Everything on it. The cups, the bell, the trumpet. Lieutenant from Augusta came in at midnight with a van." He turned the mug in his big hands. "I've got the riverboat in the cruiser. The DVD. I was going to ask you to sign it, this week." He shook his head. "Feels wrong now."

"Dale. I'll sign it on Friday. I'll sign it in gold. I'll draw a little boat." Archie spun on his stool. "Hear me out, though. The Spindrift. Ivy's staying at the Spindrift. You know what the Spindrift was in 1924? Rum-runners' drop. Captain's house, widow's walk, signal lamp, tunnel to the—"

"Pie's blueberry or apple," said Bev.

"I'm in the middle of a theory."

"There's no third pie."

A mug of coffee landed in front of him hard enough to slop. Shannon Mayo, in her Galley apron, her ponytail scraped back and her eyes swollen, set down the cream jug beside it and started to walk away.

"Shannon," Archie said.

She stopped. She didn't turn round right away. When she did, her face was flat and closed, the face of a woman who had worked a double and come back for the breakfast shift because her kids needed shoes.

"He talked to me," she said. Not loud. The co-op men went very interested in their toast. "Last night. For six minutes Tommy was there. He said it was warm. And you stood up and yelled for the lights."

"Shannon, she was dying."

"I know she was dying. I'm not stupid." Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "I'm saying you took him. You took him away from me in front of everybody, and now they're all going to say he was never there." She picked up the coffeepot. "You want eggs?"

Archie found he couldn't say anything at all. She waited a second, nodded as if that were an answer, and went down the counter filling mugs.

The bell over the door rang.

"Morning, Bev. Morning, Dale. Morning, Shannon, love." A short, sturdy woman in a man's oilskin over a man's cardigan stamped her boots on the mat. White hair cropped like a boy's, reading glasses on a beaded chain, fingerless wool gloves; her cardigan pockets bulged with what was probably string and keys and half a sandwich. Hollis Tibbetts had taught English at the regional high school for thirty-five years and ran the Merrow Players and kept the keys to the opera house on a ring the size of a dinner plate. She'd been the first person in Merrow to knock on Archie's door in August, with a pie and the keys and a list of everything in the building that was going to fall down.

She squeezed Shannon's arm on her way past. Bev was already setting a dented steel thermos on the counter with HOLLIS on its masking tape.

Hollis looked Archie up and down. "You look like something the cat wouldn't."

"I didn't sleep."

"Nobody slept. Half the town was up watching that boy's video." She took her thermos. "Eat something."

"Hollis." He turned on his stool to face her. "I should have cancelled it."

"Oh, don't you dare."

"In September. When I wanted to fight the town to call it off. I should have. You were the one who—"

"I was the one who told you not to. I know. I was there." She planted herself in front of him like a woman about to hand back an essay. "You want somebody to blame, I'm right here. I'm the one who wrote to those people in the first place. Back in May. That opera house roof needed forty thousand dollars, and their location fee was forty thousand dollars, and I'd have held a séance in there with the devil himself for a roof." She jabbed a gloved finger at his chest. "And in September I told you, 'Don't cancel her. Beat her. In Gus's house.' And I'd tell you again this morning."

"I did beat her," Archie said quietly.

"I know you did. I was in the booth. I could see the whole thing." Something gentled in her face. "You were wonderful, and then you were kind. That's the whole job. Now eat." She tucked the thermos under her arm. "Shannon, I'll have Liam and Rosie tomorrow after school like we said. Bev. Dale." And she was gone in a jangle of keys and a gust of cold.

Archie stared at the door.

"She's right," Bev said. "Eat."

"Blueberry, please," he said. "For breakfast. As a protest."

Bev was reaching for the pie case when the bell rang again.

He knew who it was before he turned round, by the way the room changed. The liars' table looked up for the first time all morning. Pinkham sat up straight. Shannon stopped pouring.

Sergeant Calloway stood inside the door in the charcoal coat with her braid down her back and a navy State Police fleece under it, and she looked as if she hadn't slept either, and as if she'd decided not to mention it.

"Morning, Tessie," said Bev.

"Morning, Bev. Coffee. Black." She came to the counter. She didn't sit. She looked at Archie the way she'd looked at him in the hospital corridor, as if he were a statement she hadn't finished reading. "Mr. Fairweather."

"Sergeant! You came for breakfast. I'm touched. I recommend the blueberry."

"Where's the thermos you took home last night?"

"Right here. I brought it back first thing, it's Bev's—"

"Washed it," said Bev.

The Galley went completely silent. Even the coffee urn seemed to stop gurgling.

Sergeant Calloway turned her head, slowly, and looked at the drying rack. The thermos stood upside down in it, steaming faintly, gleaming, its two little cups beside it like a pair of clean teeth.

Archie followed her gaze. Then he looked back at her. He could see, very clearly and all at once, exactly what she was seeing: a man who'd poured a dead woman a drink on camera, named the poison before any doctor had, and returned the vessel at dawn to the one woman in Merrow who would scrub it before he'd finished his first sentence.

There were several charming things he could say. He knew them all. He could feel them lining up behind his teeth.

"I brought it back at six," he said instead, "because it's Bev's, and that's what you do with things that aren't yours. Bev washed it because that's what Bev does. Everybody in this room knows that." He turned his palms up on the counter. "And I can see exactly how it looks. I'd arrest me."

Something moved at the very corner of her mouth and went away again.

He took out his phone, scrolled, and pressed a name. It rang four times. A woman's voice came through the speaker, sleep-thick and dangerous.

"Archie. It is six thirty-one in the morning. If this is not about a wedding cake, I'm going to have you killed."

"Margaret, good morning. I'm at a diner in Merrow with a State Police sergeant and a very clean thermos."

A pause. The sound of bedclothes being thrown back.

"Do not say one more word," said Margaret Okafor, the best criminal defense attorney in New England. "Not one. I'm getting in the car."

"She's getting in the car," Archie told the sergeant.

"I heard." Calloway took the coffee Bev slid across to her. "Voluntary interview. Nine o'clock. Select board room, at the town hall. Bring her."

"I'll be there. I'll bring the best lawyer in New England. She'll bring a scowl. You'll like each other."

"Bring the thermos."

"It's mine," Bev said.

"Then I'll take it from you, Bev." Calloway held out her hand.

Bev looked at her for a long moment, the way she looked at a pie crust that wasn't going to come right. Then she took a paper bag from under the counter, put the clean thermos and both clean cups into it, folded the top over twice and handed it across.

"You were a lot more pleasant at nine," Bev said.

"I was nine."

Sergeant Calloway tucked the bag under her arm and turned to go. Archie swiveled on his stool to follow her with his whole body, the way a sunflower follows the sun.

"Sergeant," he called, as the bell rang over her. "I'm going to be very helpful."

She didn't look back. "Don't be."

The door swung shut. The liars' table went back to not talking to him. A plate came down at his elbow: a slab of blueberry pie, warm, with the purple running out of it.

"I didn't order it," Archie said.

"You did," said Bev. "As a protest."
