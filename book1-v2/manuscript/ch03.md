# Chapter 3

*Monday, October 27 · 11:50 p.m. · Fairweather House*

The kitchen light was on. Of course it was.

June sat at the end of the scarred farm table with her laptop open. She was sixteen, in a thrift-store cardigan with the sleeves pulled over her hands and a pencil stuck through the dark knot of her hair. On the screen, in a small, shaky video, the house lights of the Merrow Opera House blazed white over and over. Each time, a man in a velvet jacket went around a table on his knees.

She had the sound off. She'd watched it enough.

"Dad," she said. It was the whole sentence.

"Hi, Junebug."

"Absolutely not." She shut the laptop. "It's got four hundred thousand views. The channel's called *Spectral Kev.* He's got a logo." She looked from Archie to her grandmother. Vivienne had come in behind him and was taking off her hat with both hands, slowly, as if it weighed a great deal. "Is she dead?"

"Yes," Archie said.

"Okay." She looked back up at him, and for an instant she was forty years old. "Are you okay?"

He was so grateful for the question that he couldn't answer it, so he did what he always did instead. He went to the drawer by the sink and started rummaging.

Lenny stood at the old cast-iron range in three sweaters. His reading glasses were pushed up on his bald head, and his enormous white eyebrows were down. Lenny had built every illusion Archie had ever performed. He had refused to come to the séance on the grounds that it was "a waste of a perfectly good Monday."

"There's goulash," Lenny said.

"Lenny. I love you. Where's the funnel?"

"Second drawer. Why?"

Archie found it: a dented tin kitchen funnel, wide at one end and narrow at the other.

"You weren't there, Lenny. Short version." He put the narrow end to his lips. "*Shaaan,*" he said through it, and the funnel made his voice small and strange, and June's face went very still.

"That part was horrible," he said, in his own voice. "Until then it was the best show I've ever seen."

"It's a funnel," Lenny said. "Sound goes in the small end. Comes out the big end. Is not complicated."

"Lenny, you're a poet. And you're right, that's the whole thing." Archie spun the funnel on the tabletop. "The tea was at twenty to eight. She was fine for forty minutes. Laughing, doing voices, running rings round me. Then she talks through *this* for ten minutes, and her mouth goes. It's on the mouthpiece. And there were cameras on that table from half past seven. If somebody touched that trumpet after I did, somebody filmed it." He sat back, and the thing he had been holding down since the hospital came up through him like a cork. "Somebody killed a woman with her own séance trumpet, in a circle where everybody was holding hands, in *my* theater, with two cameras running and me sitting right there doing the commentary. June. Do you understand what a—"

"Don't say gift."

"I wasn't going to say gift."

"You were going to say gift."

"I was going to say *puzzle*," Archie said, with dignity. "A woman is dead. It is a terrible, terrible puzzle."

Lenny put a plate of goulash in front of each of them and went back to the range.

"Dad," June said. "Are you going to make this a show?"

He stopped.

"Because I can see you doing it." She had her hands out of her sleeves now and flat on the table, the way she sat at debate. "You're doing it right now. You've got a funnel."

"It's a *demonstration.*"

"Here's a demonstration." The TV people had posted promo clips of the setup from before the lights went out, and she had watched those too. "You poured her a drink on camera. You named the poison before any doctor did. You put your mouth on the trumpet." She folded her hands. "Dad. You're the suspect."

"I'm the *alibi.*" He spread his hands. "I'm sitting here. I blew through that thing at seven thirty-six and I'm alive. It was clean at seven thirty-six."

"That's what you'd say if you did it. I'm saying what a cop would say."

Archie opened his mouth to argue, and his eye fell on the vase.

It was one of Gus's old blue-glass vases, crammed with tall stalks of hooded dark-blue flowers, like a crowd of tiny monks with their heads bowed. It stood in the exact center of the table, where it must have stood all afternoon. He had walked right past it with his head full of trumpets.

"Mother," Archie said, very carefully. "What is that?"

Vivienne looked at it. "Gus's monkshood. From along the fence. I cut it this afternoon." Her voice was thin; she was still somewhere else. "He always had it on the table at Halloween. Wolfsbane. He liked the name, for Halloween."

Archie began to laugh. He couldn't help it. It came up out of him, half horror and half delight. He put his face in his hands and laughed until his eyes ran.

"You've put a bouquet of the murder weapon," he said, "on the kitchen table."

"Archibald." His mother drew herself up. "I was sawn in half four thousand times by a man who knew more about poisons than you ever will, and he put that on this table every Halloween of my life. It is a *centerpiece*."

"It's both," said Lenny. "Don't touch it."

"If I were framing me," Archie said, "this is exactly what I'd do."

"Nobody's framing you," said June. "Grandma made a centerpiece."

"I'm not going to touch it." Archie took out his phone. He fished in his jacket for the card Sergeant Calloway had left with the charge nurse. He had lifted it on his way out, because it seemed rude not to. He photographed the vase from two angles, in good light, with the kitchen clock in the frame. Then he texted the pictures to the cell number on the card.

*Full disclosure: my mother's Halloween centerpiece. From my grandfather's garden. She cut it this afternoon. Nobody has touched it since. — A. Fairweather.*

It was 12:40 a.m. The reply came in under a minute.

*Don't touch it.*

"She's awake," Archie said. "She answers in complete sentences."

"That's three words," June said.

"Complete, though. Subject implied." He put the phone face down. "Mother. Who's Sparrow?"

Vivienne had been looking at the monkshood. Now she looked at him. For a moment her face did something he had never seen it do in forty-seven years. Then it stopped.

"Go to bed, Archibald," she said. She picked up her hat and went out, and they heard her on the stairs, slow, all the way up.

June turned back to him.

"Promise me you'll stay out of it."

He could make forty thousand people believe a woman had vanished. He had never once been able to lie to his daughter.

"Junebug—"

"You're doing the hands." She nodded at the quarter walking across his knuckles. He hadn't noticed it come out. "You do the hands when you're about to say something that's not true."

The coin stopped.

"I can't," he said.

June didn't say anything. She waited, which was worse, so he told her.

"Somebody did a trick in Grandpa Gus's theater tonight," he said, "with me sitting right there calling every move, and I don't know how. I know how everything *she* did was done. I don't know how this was. And she said Sparrow, and the trunk, and I want to know where she got that. And I liked her, June. She was so good."

June looked at him for a long time.

"I know," she said. "I knew when I watched the video. I just wanted to see if you'd lie to me."

"Did I pass?"

"You did the hands."

"But I didn't *lie*."

"That's why you passed." She sat up straight. "No cameras."

"No cameras."

"No show. You don't go on Kev's channel. You don't do a press conference in a cape."

"I have never done a press conference in a cape."

"Vegas. 2019."

"That was a *cloak.*"

"And I hear everything first. From you. Not from the internet, not from Grandma, not from the news."

"Done."

"And you don't drive me to school in the hearse."

"June."

"Ever."

He held out his hand across the table. She looked at it, sighed through her nose, and shook it.

"Also," she said, "Kev's comments are full of lawyers saying you owe a million dollars."

"I do."

"Dad."

"I couldn't explain how she knew. *Sparrow.* The trunk. Not yet." He took out his phone and set a reminder for nine a.m. "Rules are rules, and they're my rules. Her charity gets the check in the morning. Then I find out how she did it, and I mail them the explanation."

June looked at him for a long moment. "That's either the best or the worst thing you've ever done."

"Usually both," said Archie.

Lenny took the empty plates and went out to the carriage house without a word.

At ten past one, Archie climbed the narrow stairs to the turret. Gus's study at the top of the house was round and cold and full of moonlight off the harbor. In the corner, where it had stood for thirty-seven years, was the trunk. It was black and brass-cornered, painted with faded gold stars, with THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER stenciled on the lid. Under a yellowed square of celluloid, a card in Gus's slanting hand read: *To be opened when I'm gone. Not before. — G.F.* Seven brass letter-dials sat in a row across the lock. Lenny could have had it open in four minutes with a drill. But back in the eighties, Gus had told the whole town that forcing the lock would break a glass vial of India ink over whatever was inside. In thirty-seven years, nobody had been willing to find out if he was bluffing.

His mother was sitting on it.

She sat there in the moonlight, in her coat, with her hat in her lap, the way she must have sat on it as a girl.

"Seven letters," Archie said.

"I know how many letters it is."

"You know what it means."

"I know what it means." Her voice was perfectly steady. "He said when he's gone, Archibald. Thirty-seven years, and nobody has opened it, so he isn't gone. That's the rule. I have lived on that rule." She smoothed the hat in her lap. "A woman with a tin horn doesn't get to change it. Not in his theater. Not tonight."

He could have argued. He could have taken her argument apart in two sentences and put it back together facing the other way. He had been doing it to audiences for thirty years. Instead he stood in the doorway with his notebook in his hand. It held three months of failures in his own writing, one word a night and some nights forty. He didn't argue.

"Who's Sparrow?" he asked quietly.

"Go to bed, darling."

He went. Halfway down the stairs he stopped and looked back up. She was still sitting there in the moonlight with her hands folded, guarding a box. Down by the rocks, the bell buoy off Shag Ledge rang twice in the dark.
