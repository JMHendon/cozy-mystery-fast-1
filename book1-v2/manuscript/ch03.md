# Chapter 3

*Monday, October 27 · 11:50 p.m. · Fairweather House*

The kitchen light was on. Of course it was.

June sat at the end of the scarred farm table in a thrift-store cardigan with the sleeves pulled over her hands, her laptop open in front of her and a pencil stuck through the dark knot of her hair. On the screen, very small and very shaky, the house lights of the Merrow Opera House blazed white over and over again on a man in a velvet jacket going round a table on his knees.

She had the sound off. She'd watched it enough.

"Dad," she said. It was the whole sentence.

"Hi, Junebug."

"Don't." She shut the laptop. "It's got four hundred thousand views. The channel's called *Spectral Kev.* He's got a logo." She looked from Archie to her grandmother, who had come in behind him and was taking off her hat with both hands, slowly, as if it weighed a great deal. "Is she dead?"

"Yes," Archie said.

"Okay." She looked back up at him, and for an instant she was forty years old. "Are you okay?"

He was so grateful for the question that he couldn't answer it, so he did what he always did instead. He went to the drawer by the sink and started rummaging.

Lenny was at the old cast-iron range in three sweaters, reading glasses pushed up on his bald head, enormous white eyebrows down. He had built every illusion Archie had ever performed, and had refused to come to the séance on the grounds that it was "a waste of a perfectly good Monday."

"There's goulash," Lenny said.

"Lenny. I love you. Where's the funnel?"

"Second drawer. Why?"

Archie found it: a dented tin kitchen funnel, wide at one end and narrow at the other. He held it up to the light like a sommelier with a good year.

"You weren't there, Lenny, so I'll do the short version. Seven people holding hands, and two of them weren't. The daughter let go at seven minutes past. The medium had a hand free, and everything that floated tonight floated in that hand." He put the narrow end to his lips. "*Shaaan,*" he said through it, and the funnel made his voice small and far away and strange, and June's face went very still.

He put it down.

"That part was horrible," he said, in his own voice. "The rest of it was the best show I've ever seen."

"It's a funnel," Lenny said. "Sound goes in the small end. Comes out the big end. Is not complicated."

"Lenny, you're a poet. And you're right, that's the whole thing." Archie spun the funnel on the tabletop. "The tea was at twenty to eight. She was fine for forty minutes. Laughing, doing voices, running rings round me. Then she talks through *this* for ten minutes, and her mouth goes. It's on the mouthpiece." He sat back, and the thing he had been holding down since the hospital came up through him like a cork. "Somebody killed a woman with her own séance trumpet, in a circle where everybody was holding hands, in *my* theater, with two cameras running and me sitting right there doing the commentary. June. Do you understand what a—"

"Don't say gift."

"I wasn't going to say gift."

"You were going to say gift."

"I was going to say *puzzle*," Archie said, with dignity. "A woman is dead. It is a terrible, terrible puzzle." He lasted about two seconds. "Hear me out, though. The producer. Teddy. He was in the wing on the monitor the whole time, and when the cameras died he said 'Static, static, love it,' like he knew it was coming. What if—"

"He did know it was coming," June said. "You just told us it was the daughter's remote."

"Then he's covering for her. Which is *also* suspicious."

"It's a TV show. Covering for her is his job."

Lenny put a plate of goulash in front of each of them. "She's right," he said, and went back to the range.

"Dad," June said. "Are you going to make this a show?"

He stopped.

"Because I can see you doing it." She had her hands out of her sleeves now and flat on the table, the way she sat at debate. "You're doing it right now. You've got a funnel."

"It's a *demonstration.*"

"Here's a demonstration." June ticked them off on her fingers. The TV people had posted clips of the setup before the lights went out, for the promo; she'd watched those too. "You poured her a drink on camera. You told the police what poisoned her before any doctor did. You put your mouth on the trumpet. You bet her a million dollars." She closed the laptop. "Dad. You're the suspect."

"I'm the *alibi.*" He spread his hands. "I'm sitting here. I blew through that thing at seven thirty-six and I'm alive. It was clean at seven thirty-six."

"That's what you'd say if you did it."

"June!"

"I'm not saying you did it. I'm saying what a cop would say." She tilted her head at the counter by the back door. "Is that the thermos?"

It was: Bev's dented steel thermos from the Galley, BEV on its masking-tape label, the two little cups nested on top.

"Bev's," Lenny said. "Goes back in the morning."

"See, that's the kind of thing," June said, "that's going to look bad."

Archie opened his mouth to argue, and his eye fell on the vase.

It stood in the exact center of the table, where it had stood all evening, where he'd walked past it a dozen times: one of Gus's old blue-glass vases, crammed full of tall stalks hung with hooded dark-blue flowers, like a crowd of tiny monks with their heads bowed.

"Mother," Archie said, very carefully. "What is that?"

Vivienne looked at it as if seeing it for the first time. "Gus's monkshood. From along the fence. I cut it this morning." Her voice was thin; she was still somewhere else. "He always had it on the table at Halloween. Wolfsbane. For the name."

Archie began to laugh. He couldn't help it. It came up out of him half horror and half delight, and he put his face in his hands and laughed until his eyes ran.

"You've put a bouquet of the murder weapon," he said, "on the kitchen table."

"I didn't know it was a murder weapon, Archibald. I thought it was a *flower*."

"It's both," said Lenny. "Don't touch it."

"If I were framing me," Archie said, "this is exactly what I'd do."

"Nobody's framing you," said June. "Grandma made a centerpiece."

"I'm not going to touch it." Archie took out his phone. He fished in his jacket for the card Sergeant Calloway had left with the charge nurse, which he had lifted on his way out because it had seemed rude not to. He photographed the vase from two angles, in good light, with the kitchen clock in frame, and texted it to the cell number on the card.

*Full disclosure: my mother's Halloween centerpiece. From my grandfather's garden. She cut it this morning. Nobody has touched it since. — A. Fairweather.*

It was 12:40 a.m. The reply came in under a minute.

*Don't touch it.*

"She's awake," Archie said, delighted. "She answers in complete sentences."

"That's three words," June said.

"Complete, though. Subject implied." He put the phone face down. "Mother. Who's Sparrow?"

Vivienne had been looking at the monkshood. Now she looked at him, and for a moment her face did something he had never seen it do in forty-seven years, and then it stopped doing it.

"Go to bed, Archibald," she said. She picked up her hat and went out, and they heard her on the stairs, slow, all the way up.

June watched the door. Then she turned back to him, and her voice changed into the one she used when she'd prepared.

"Promise me you'll stay out of it."

He'd seen it coming since the drive home. He could look forty thousand people in the eye and make them believe a woman had vanished. He had never once been able to lie to his daughter.

"Junebug—"

"You're doing the hands." She nodded at the quarter walking across his knuckles. He hadn't noticed it come out. "You do the hands when you're about to say something that's not true."

The coin stopped.

"I can't," he said.

June didn't say anything. She waited, which was worse.

"Somebody did a trick in Grandpa Gus's theater tonight," he said, "with me sitting right there calling every move, and I don't know how. I know how everything *she* did was done. I don't know how this was." He turned the funnel over on the table. "And she said Sparrow. And the trunk. And I liked her, June. She was so good."

June looked at him for a long time.

"No cameras," she said finally.

"No cameras."

"No show. You don't go on Kev's channel. You don't do a press conference in a cape."

"I have never done a press conference in a cape."

"Vegas. 2019."

"That was a *cloak.*"

"And I hear everything first. From you. Not from the internet. Not from Grandma. Not from the news." Her chin came up. "If I find out something on TV, I'm moving to Montreal."

"Done."

"And you don't drive me to school in the hearse."

"June."

"Ever."

He held out his hand across the table. She looked at it, sighed through her nose, and shook it.

"Okay," she said.

Lenny took the empty plates and went out to the carriage house without a word.

At ten past one Archie climbed the narrow stairs to the turret. Gus's study at the top of the house was round and cold and full of moonlight off the harbor, and in the corner, where it had stood for thirty-seven years, was the trunk: black, brass-cornered, faded gold stars, THE GREAT FAIRWEATHER stenciled on its lid, and under a yellowed square of celluloid in Gus's slanting hand, *To be opened when I'm gone. Not before. — G.F.* Seven brass letter-dials sat in a row across the lock. Lenny could have had it open in four minutes with a drill. But Gus had told the whole town, back in the eighties, that anybody who forced it would break a glass vial of India ink over whatever was inside, and in thirty-seven years nobody had been willing to find out if he was bluffing.

He knelt in front of it, the way he had every night since August, and opened his notebook of failures to the next clean page.

His fingers went to the first dial on their own. S. Then P. Then A.

He stopped.

Seven letters. Of course it was seven letters. She'd have counted. It was the oldest move there was: hand the mark a word that fits his lock, on camera, the week of Halloween, and let him do the rest. If it opened, a dead woman had told him something true from the other side, and every clip of it would say so forever. If it didn't, her fans would say he'd spelled it wrong.

"Nice try," Archie said. "Not from you."

He spun the dials back and tried SEANCES, which he was fairly sure he had tried in September, and it didn't open, and he wrote it down anyway.

Then he sat back on his heels in the moonlight with the cold coming up through the floorboards and asked the room, "Who's Sparrow? And where did you get it?"

The house didn't answer. Down by the rocks, the bell buoy off Shag Ledge rang twice in the dark.
