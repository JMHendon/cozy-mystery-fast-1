# Chapter 1

*Monday, October 27 · 8:00 p.m. · The Merrow Opera House*

Archie Fairweather was holding his mother's hand in the dark and having the time of his life.

"Toe," he whispered.

Somewhere across the table, wood knocked on wood: three slow raps, like a polite guest at a door. Around the circle somebody gasped. Kev, on Archie's left, made a noise like a kettle.

"Did you hear that? Mr. Fairweather, did you *hear* that?"

"Right big toe," Archie murmured, "against the table leg. She's wearing ballet flats. I checked." He tipped his head toward the little red glow of the infrared camera on its tripod. "For the record, that's one."

On his right, Vivienne's fingers tightened, which meant *behave*, and then tightened again, which meant *go on*.

The Merrow Opera House was perfectly black except for a red lamp the size of a teacup in the middle of the table. It made seven faces into seven hovering masks and did nothing for anybody. Blackout drapes covered the tall windows; one at the far stage-left end had been left bare for the TV people, and through it the harbor lights trembled on the glass. Three hundred empty seats sat behind Archie in the dark. He could feel them. He'd played to bigger houses and never to one he loved more, because he owned this one. Very nearly. The town would hand him the keys on November fifteenth.

Half an hour ago, with the house lights up and two cameras on him, he had searched everything Lorelei Crane had brought. The tablecloth, the chairs, the hand bell, the tambourine, the child's slate, and the trumpet itself, a long dented aluminum cone wrapped in luminous bands, which he had held up to the lens, looked through, put to his lips at the narrow end and blown a long wet raspberry into. "No spirits in here," he'd told the camera, and the crew had laughed, and Lorelei had laughed. She had refused to let him search her. She had refused him the chair beside her. So he'd poured her a cup of chamomile from Bev's dented diner thermos, in front of everybody, and her daughter had said, "She doesn't drink anything I don't pour," and Lorelei had taken the cup anyway, and they'd toasted. The tea truce. It would play beautifully in the trailer.

The terms were simple. If anything happened in this room tonight that Archie couldn't explain before the lights came up, he would write a check for a million dollars to the charity of Lorelei's choice. The Fairweather Challenge had been open for nineteen years. Nobody had ever collected. He had never wanted anyone to collect so badly.

"Brother Amos is with us," Lorelei said.

Her voice came out of the dark exactly opposite him: smoky, amused, intimate, a voice that had been told a thousand secrets and kept the good ones. "He says there's a skeptic in the circle, and he forgives him."

"Tell Brother Amos I'm a huge fan," Archie said, aloud.

A ripple went round the table. Warren Gilley, the chairman of the select board, two seats to Archie's right, said "Shh," with the authority of a man who had once chaired a meeting about parking.

"Mr. Fairweather is narrating, Amos," Lorelei said. "Forgive him. He's from Las Vegas."

"Boston, originally. Brother Amos might know it. Lots of Irish monks."

"Honey," Lorelei said, warmly, "are you going to talk through my whole séance?"

"Somebody has to. You're doing all the voices."

Vivienne made a small, delighted sound in her throat and pinched him.

Then, at seven minutes past eight, somebody's chair creaked, and something clicked lightly against the oak tabletop: rings, Archie thought, moonstone rings on a hand that had just been let go. He leaned to his mother's ear.

"And she's free," he breathed. "Left hand. The daughter let go of her."

"You can't possibly know that."

"Ivy's chair just moved and her mother's rings touched the table. Nobody puts their rings down while they're holding hands. Watch the bell."

The hand bell rang. It rang again, closer, and then it seemed to walk across the table in little tinkling hops, and the whole circle drew breath, and Kev Duchesne whispered "Oh my *God*" with such pure joy that Archie nearly loved him.

"Telescoping rod," Archie murmured. "From Ivy's lap. Two."

At eleven minutes past, something cold and wet brushed the back of Warren's neck. Warren yelped. Then it touched Vivienne's cheek, and Archie's mother, who had been sawn in half four thousand times and had never once screamed, laughed out loud.

"A damp glove on the same rod," Archie said. "Three. I'm sorry, Mother."

"Don't be. It was a very nice glove."

At fourteen minutes past, the red standby lights on both cameras went dark at once. A murmur from the stage-right wing, where the producer sat at his monitor: "Static, static, love it, keep going."

"*Spirit static,*" Kev breathed. "That's documented. That's a documented phenomenon, Mr. Fairweather."

"It's a pocket remote. Probably Ivy's, probably in her lap, probably her left thumb. Four." Archie squeezed Kev's damp hand. "Don't worry. It's still a wonderful show."

And it was. That was the thing. Lorelei Crane was the best he had ever sat with. Every effect arrived a half-beat after you'd stopped expecting it. She let the silences run long enough to hurt. She had the room, and he had the method, and they both knew it, and in the dark across the table he could feel her enjoying him the way he was enjoying her. Two professionals. He was going to beat her on her own show, in his grandfather's theater, and afterward he was going to buy her the best lobster dinner in Maine and ask her where she'd learned that bell walk.

At seventeen minutes past eight, the trumpet rose.

The luminous bands came up off the table like something surfacing from deep water: three pale green rings, floating, turning, swinging slow arcs above the red lamp. Kev's hand clamped on Archie's. Even Archie felt it in the back of his neck: that first cold delight, the one he'd been chasing since he was four years old.

"Her left hand," he whispered to Vivienne, but not as loud as before. "She's holding it. Lips to the narrow end. Five."

Then the trumpet spoke, and the voice was not Lorelei's.

It was a young man's voice, rough and low, a voice off a boat. "*Shan,*" it said. "*Shan… it's warm here.*"

At Lorelei's right hand, Shannon Mayo made a sound Archie would remember for a long time.

"*The water's warm,*" said the trumpet. "*I'm not cold anymore.*"

"Tommy?" Shannon said. "Tommy?"

The fun went out of it all at once, like a breaker tripping. Shannon waited tables at the Galley. Her husband had gone down with his boat off Monhegan in February. Archie knew exactly how this was done, down to which newspaper the name had come from, and none of that knowledge was any use to the woman across the table, who was crying now in small hiccuping breaths and saying "I know, I know, I know, baby."

He could stop it. He could let go of two hands, stand up, and shout for the lights, and the circle would break, and the show would be ruined, and Shannon would have to hear it from him that her husband had never been here. He shifted his weight.

Vivienne's grip turned to iron. "Don't you dare," his mother whispered. "Not yet."

He didn't know what she meant until the trumpet swung toward them.

"*Viv,*" it said. An old man's voice now, dry and fond, with a hint of Maine under a lifetime of stage diction. "*Vivi.*"

His mother stopped breathing.

"*Sparrow,*" said the voice. "*The trunk, Sparrow. The answer's in the—*"

The word smeared. The voice thickened as if the old man's mouth were full of cotton. Archie, against every rule he lived by, wanted it. He wanted it so much his chest hurt. Then his professional brain caught up and admired the work: she was fading the spirit out, letting him slur away, leaving them wanting.

"Who's Sparrow?" he breathed to his mother. She didn't answer. Her hand was shaking.

The trumpet coughed. It was a real cough, wet and choking. The pale rings dipped, swung wide and wobbled.

"*My mouth,*" said Lorelei Crane, in her own voice, thick and frightened. "*I can't feel my—*"

The trumpet hit the table, bounced, and rolled clanging onto the floor.

Archie let go of both hands.

"Lights!" he shouted, already on his feet, already moving. "Hollis — *lights!*"

High at the back of the hall, in the plywood booth on the balcony, somebody slammed the board, and the house lights came up all at once, white and enormous, and the room appeared: the pressed-tin ceiling, the painted harbor on the 1899 drape, the cameras, the cables, six people at a round oak table blinking like owls.

Lorelei Crane sat slumped sideways in her chair. Ivy had grabbed back her mother's left hand. Shannon still held the right. Lorelei's mouth was open and working, and her dark-red lipstick looked suddenly too bright on a face gone the color of putty.

Archie came round the table and dropped to his knees beside her. From down there, at the level of everybody's shoes, he saw Ivy Crane let go of her mother, stoop, pick the trumpet up off the floorboards, and wipe the narrow end, quick and practiced, with the gray silk scarf from her own neck. She pushed the scarf deep into her coat pocket. It took two seconds. Nobody else was looking at her. Everybody else was looking at Lorelei.

"Lorelei." Archie took her hand. Her fingers were cold and clumsy. "Lorelei, look at me. Is it your lips? Tingling? Burning?"

"Pins," she said. It came out *pinth*. "All… my face."

He knew that. He knew it the way he knew how a coin felt in his palm. Sixteen years ago he had spent a month learning how the Borgias killed people, for eleven minutes of stage time in Vegas, and he had read the same description over and over in old physicians' notes: the numbness at the lips, the tingling spreading over the face, then the voice, then the heart.

"Dale!" Chief Pinkham was already halfway down the aisle, belly first, mustache flying. "Call it in. Tell them it could be poison. Tell them *aconite*, A-C-O-N-I-T-E. Tell the hospital somebody has to ask for it, they won't find it unless they look."

"Ayuh. Lord. Ayuh." Pinkham had his radio up.

"Ambulance is coming, Archie!" Hollis's voice, sharp and calm, from the booth. "Six minutes! I'm coming down." Keys jangled up there like a ring of bells.

Lorelei's eyes found his. Her pupils were huge. Under the fear there was something else, something wry, the look one performer gives another across a stage when the scenery falls down.

"Not…" she said. Her mouth didn't work right. "Not the spirits, honey."

"No," said Archie, holding her hand for real. "I know."

Pinkham was unrolling yellow tape from somewhere in that uniform jacket, the jacket that didn't close, looping it round the backs of the chairs. Kev was filming on his phone with both hands shaking. Shannon had her face in her palms. Vivienne stood behind her chair very straight, like a woman waiting for a curtain.

Archie looked at the trumpet in Ivy Crane's hands. Then he looked at Ivy, and Ivy looked back at him, pale as paper, and set it down on the table as if it were hot.

"Nobody," said Archie Fairweather, "touches that trumpet."
