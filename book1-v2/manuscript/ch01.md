# Chapter 1

*Monday, October 27 · 8:00 p.m. · The Merrow Opera House*

Archie Fairweather was holding his mother's hand in the dark and having the time of his life.

"Toe," he whispered.

Somewhere across the table, wood knocked on wood: three slow raps, like a polite guest at a door. Around the circle somebody gasped. Kev, on Archie's left, made a noise like a kettle.

"Did you hear that? Mr. Fairweather, did you *hear* that?"

"Right big toe," Archie murmured, "against the table leg. She's wearing ballet flats. I checked." He tipped his head toward the little red glow of the infrared camera on its tripod. "For the record, that's one."

Seven of them sat around a small oak table, close enough to bump knees. Lorelei Crane sat across from Archie, with her daughter, Ivy, on her left. On Lorelei's right sat Shannon Mayo. Archie had put Shannon there himself, because she was the one person at the table he was sure wasn't in on it. In the middle, a red lamp the size of a teacup lit nothing but itself. Blackout drapes covered the tall windows except one at the far end, left bare for the TV cameras. The harbor lights trembled on its glass. Archie had bought the building from the town in September, and on November fifteenth it would be his.

Half an hour ago, he had searched everything Lorelei brought while the house lights blazed and two cameras recorded him. He even searched the trumpet itself, a long, dented aluminum cone wrapped in luminous bands. After looking through it, he put his lips to the narrow end and blew a long, wet raspberry into it. "No spirits in here," he told the camera. Even Lorelei laughed.

Lorelei refused to be searched herself, and she refused him the chair beside her. So he poured her a cup of chamomile from a dented diner thermos, in front of everybody. Ivy objected: "She doesn't drink anything I don't pour." Lorelei took the cup anyway, and they toasted. The tea truce.

The terms were simple. If anything happened in this room tonight that Archie couldn't explain before the lights came up, he'd write a million-dollar check to the charity of her choice. The Fairweather Challenge had been open for nineteen years. Nobody had ever collected. Secretly, embarrassingly, he hoped someone would.

"Brother Amos is with us," Lorelei said from across the table. "He senses a skeptic."

"Tell Amos I'll be watching his hands," Archie said, aloud. "And if he hasn't got any, I'll be watching yours."

A ripple went round the table. Warren Gilley, on the far side of Vivienne, said "Shh."

"Warren once chaired a meeting about parking," Archie told his mother. "He's never come down."

Three more raps came out of the dark, slow and solemn.

Archie answered without letting go of anybody. He rapped four times with his heel on the floorboards, perfectly matched in tone. Then, from somewhere under the table, a tiny cracked old woman's voice said, "*Amos, it's your mother. Put a sweater on.*"

The circle came apart in helpless laughter. Kev wheezed. Even Ivy made a small sound. Across the table even Lorelei laughed, and for a second the séance was simply a room full of people having a wonderful time because of him.

"Sorry," said Archie, not sorry at all. "Professional reflex. I'll behave."

Vivienne pinched him. She knew he wouldn't.

At seven minutes past eight, somebody's chair creaked. Something clicked lightly against the oak tabletop. Rings, Archie thought: moonstone rings on a hand that had just been let go. He leaned to his mother's ear.

"And she's free," he breathed. "Left hand. The daughter let go of her."

"You can't possibly know that."

"Ivy's chair just moved and her mother's rings touched the table. Nobody puts their rings down while they're holding hands." He raised his voice a notch. "Kev. Ten dollars says the bell moves next."

"You can't bet against the *spirits*."

"I'm not betting against the spirits, Kev. I'm betting on her."

The hand bell rang. It rang again, closer. Then it seemed to walk across the table in little tinkling hops, and the whole circle drew breath. Kev Duchesne whispered "Oh my *God*" with such pure joy that Archie nearly loved him.

"Telescoping rod," Archie murmured. "Six feet of it, from Ivy's lap. You owe me ten dollars."

At eleven minutes past, something cold and wet brushed the back of Warren's neck. Warren yelped.

"Easy, Warren," Archie said. "It's a spirit, not an audit."

Then it touched Vivienne's cheek. Archie's mother had been sawn in half four thousand times without once screaming, and now she laughed out loud.

"A damp glove on the same rod," Archie murmured. "Sorry, Mother."

"Don't be. It was a very nice glove."

At fourteen minutes past, the red standby lights on both cameras went dark at once. A murmur came from the stage-right wing, where the producer sat at his monitor: "Static, static, love it, keep going."

"*Spirit static,*" Kev breathed. "That's documented. That's a documented phenomenon, Mr. Fairweather."

"It's a pocket remote. Ivy's, in her lap. One free hand: the rod, then the glove on the rod, now the remote, one after another, never a fumble. Hardest-working woman in show business." Archie squeezed Kev's damp hand. "Don't worry. It's still a wonderful show."

And it was. He was going to beat Lorelei on her own show, in his grandfather's theater. Afterward he would buy her the best lobster dinner in Maine and ask where she had learned that bell walk.

At seventeen minutes past eight, the trumpet rose.

The luminous bands rose off the table like something surfacing from deep water. Three pale green rings floated and turned, and the wide end swung in slow arcs over the red lamp. Kev's hand clamped on Archie's. Even Archie felt it in the back of his neck: the cold delight he'd been chasing since he was four.

"Oh, that's *gorgeous*," he said aloud. "Lorelei, that's the best float I've ever seen. I'm stealing it."

"Her left hand," he whispered to Vivienne, but not as loud as before. "Narrow end at her mouth, swinging the wide end round like a lighthouse."

Then the trumpet spoke, and the voice was not Lorelei's.

It was a young man's voice, rough and low, a voice off a boat. "*Shan,*" it said. "*Shan… it's warm here.*"

At Lorelei's right hand, Shannon Mayo made a sound Archie would remember for a long time.

"*The water's warm,*" said the trumpet. "*I'm not cold anymore.*"

"Tommy?" Shannon said. "Tommy?"

The fun went out of it all at once, like a breaker tripping. Shannon waited tables at the Galley. Her husband had gone down with his boat off Monhegan in February. Archie knew exactly how this was done. None of that knowledge was any use to Shannon. She was crying now in small, hiccuping breaths, saying, "I know, I know, I know, baby."

He could stop it. He could let go, stand up and shout for the lights. Then Shannon would hear it from him that her husband had never been here. He shifted his weight.

Vivienne's grip turned to iron. "Don't you dare," his mother whispered. "Not yet."

He didn't know what she meant until the trumpet swung toward them.

"*Viv,*" it said. An old man's voice now, dry and fond, with a hint of Maine under a lifetime of stage diction. "*Vivi.*"

His mother stopped breathing.

The last time Archie heard that voice on this stage, he was ten years old and sitting in the second row. It was Halloween, 1988. His grandfather took his bow, walked down to the harbor in his opera cloak and never came back.

"*Sparrow,*" said the voice. "*The trunk, Sparrow. The answer's in the—*"

The word smeared. The voice thickened as if the old man's mouth were full of cotton. Against every rule he lived by, Archie wanted it to be real. He wanted it so much that his chest hurt. Then his professional brain caught up and admired the work. Lorelei was fading the spirit out, leaving them wanting more.

"Who's Sparrow?" he breathed to his mother. She didn't answer. Her hand was shaking.

The trumpet coughed. It was a real cough, wet and choking. The pale rings dipped, swung wide and wobbled.

"*My mouth,*" said Lorelei Crane, in her own voice, thick and frightened. "*I can't feel my—*"

The trumpet hit the table, bounced, and rolled clanging onto the floor.

Archie let go of both hands.

"Lights!" he shouted, already on his feet, already moving. "Hollis — *lights!*"

Up in the plywood booth on the balcony, somebody slammed the light board. The house lights came up all at once, white and enormous. The room reappeared around them: the pressed-tin ceiling, the painted harbor on the 1899 drape, the cameras and the cables. Everyone at the table blinked like owls.

Lorelei Crane sat slumped sideways in her chair. Ivy had grabbed back her mother's left hand. Shannon still held the right. Lorelei's mouth was open and working. Her dark-red lipstick looked suddenly too bright on a face gone the color of putty.

Archie came around the table and dropped to his knees beside Lorelei. From down there, at the level of everybody's shoes, he saw Ivy let go of her mother's hand and stoop to pick the trumpet up off the floorboards. With a quick, practiced motion, Ivy wiped the narrow end with the gray silk scarf from her own neck. Then she pushed the scarf deep into her coat pocket. It took two seconds. Nobody else was looking at her. Everybody else was looking at Lorelei.

"Lorelei." Archie took her hand. Her fingers were cold and clumsy. "Lorelei, look at me. Is it your lips? Tingling? Burning?"

"Pins," she said. It came out *pinth*. "All… my face."

He knew that symptom. Sixteen years ago he had built an eleven-minute Borgia routine for his Vegas show. To write it, he spent a month learning every poison the Renaissance ever put in a cup, and this was the one that frightened him. He had read it over and over in old physicians' notes: the numbness at the lips, the tingling spreading over the face, then the voice, then the heart.

Chief Pinkham was already halfway down the aisle from the back of the hall, belly first, mustache flying.

"Dale! Call it in. Tell them it could be poison. Tell them *aconite*, A-C-O-N-I-T-E. Tell the hospital somebody has to ask for it, they won't find it unless they look."

"Ayuh. On it." Pinkham had his radio up.

"Ambulance is coming, Archie!" Hollis Tibbetts's voice, sharp and calm, from the booth. "Six minutes! I'm coming down." A moment later the house manager came clattering down the balcony stairs, short and white-haired, phone still in her hand.

Lorelei's eyes found his. Her pupils were huge. Her mouth worked, but nothing came out. Under the fear he saw a question, the one performers ask each other when the scenery falls. He knew the answer, so he gave it to her.

"Not the spirits, honey," said Archie, holding her hand for real. "Stay with me. I haven't finished beating you."

The corner of her smeared red mouth moved.

Pinkham was looping yellow tape round the backs of the chairs. Kev was filming on his phone with both hands shaking. Shannon had her face in her palms. Vivienne stood behind her chair very straight.

Archie looked at the trumpet in Ivy Crane's hands. Then he looked at Ivy. She looked back at him, pale as paper, and set the trumpet down on the table as if it were hot.

"Nobody," said Archie Fairweather, "touches that trumpet."
