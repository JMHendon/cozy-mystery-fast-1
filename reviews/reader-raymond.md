# Early-Reader Panel Report: *Nobody Fools Archie Fairweather*

**Reader:** Raymond, 67, retired insurance claims investigator, Vermont. Fifty years of mysteries. I keep a suspect list in pencil and I don't forgive cheats.

**Disclosure:** I paged straight through, start to finish. To find my place I listed the chapter headings once at the start, so I saw titles like "The Wrong Woman" and "The Method" before I got to them. They gave nothing away. ("The Trumpet Woman" in Ch 34 would have, but I'd solved it by then.)

*(Also, a man named Raymond turns up in the Sea View common room in Ch 4 and calls out the seven of hearts. I took it personally, in a nice way.)*

## 2. When I suspected Hollis, when I was sure, and why

**First suspicion: Ch 2, then real suspicion in Ch 7.**
- Ch 2's "hers are a foot taller" is the kind of line a careful author plants and a careless one writes by accident. I gave it the benefit of the doubt.
- Ch 7 made it real. The gloves, the permit that makes her the only one at the table with a reason to be there, and the camera swinging away during the slide:

> "The cameraman swung away toward the one bare window, where the lights of the moorings were coming on... 'Hollis,' came Teddy's voice from the wing, 'sorry — can you slide the horn left, it's fighting the candle.' Hollis slid the trumpet a few inches left, away from the tall candle, and went off into the wing with her empty tray. The cameraman swung back to the table."

When an author tells you the camera looked away, she's telling you where to look.

**Motive suspicion: Ch 5 plus Ch 3, firming at Ch 17 and Ch 21.** "H. Langley" on the card and "Peter Langley 2003" on the stone. At Ch 17, the son's photo, the man in oilskins and the bare bed:

> "the long border along her fence had been cut down to the ground and the bed turned over for the winter"

At Ch 21, Kev's "Trumpet Hiatus... Oh-three to oh-six." That tied the medium to 2003.

**Confident (85%): Ch 31.**

> "Oh, sweetheart. The water's never warm here."

That told me she'd heard "it's warm here" before, and it hadn't come from Shannon.

**Certain: Ch 32**, the hatbox.

I solved it about eleven chapters before Archie. I don't count that against the book. In a fair-play mystery the sharp reader *should* be able to get there. The tension then shifts from *who* to *when will he see it*, and the author mostly makes that work, partly because Hollis is so likable that you don't *want* to be right.

## 3. Fair-play audit

### What the author did right
- **The physical clue chain is honest and early.** The 7:35 raspberry fixes when the trumpet was last clean. Kev's IR footage fixes the end. Marco's card closes the middle. That's a proper locked window built from three independent sources. The forty-second gap is described in Ch 7, nineteen chapters before it's explained.
- **The poison is planted in Ch 2,** with Hollis's own patch flagged, and its symptoms are dramatized accurately: numb lips, slurring, sweat, dilated pupils, unstable arrhythmia, death about ninety minutes later.
- **Motive is documented,** not delivered by confession alone: the library card, the memorial, the photo, the Hiatus, the shorthand ledger, Walt's file. Each one can be checked against the others.
- **The seam detail is good forensic thinking.** The outside wiped clean while aconitine stays in the rolled seam explains why Ivy's wipe didn't destroy the evidence. Ivy rubbing her fingertips in Ch 9 is a quiet, honest echo. Pay it off explicitly (see change 6).

### Withheld or asserted, not shown
- **"Sparrow."** This is the book's biggest dangling thread. In Ch 10 Vivienne says nobody but Gus used the name and demands: "The interview. The magazine. If it's written down somewhere... show it to me." The book never shows it. In Ch 44 June simply *asserts*: "She found it in the Packet. In 1988. A reporter wrote it down because Grandpa Gus said it to him." Where's the clipping? June says in Ch 32 that the *Packet* is on microfilm, and Deb opens at nine. Nobody ever goes. The trunk combination rests on this, so it has to be on the page.
- **Brannock's Ch 27 claim that Ivy "laid out every prop on that table at seven-twenty, alone"** is new information introduced only to raise the stakes. Plant it in Ch 7.
- **We never learn whether Hollis placed the candle deliberately** to make the camera flare. The Ch 26 "hand's width from the trumpet" all but says so, and her confession should own it. As written, her opportunity depends on Teddy happening to ask her to move the trumpet. That's a coincidence the author could easily turn into a plan.

### Coincidences
- **The timing of Shannon's targeting.** By Hollis's own account (Ch 42), Lorelei's people messaged Shannon *in April*, and Hollis didn't write to the producer until *May*. So the con woman who destroyed Hollis's family, working out of Los Angeles, happened to start on Hollis's goddaughter's mother *before* anything brought her to Merrow. You can argue she trawls drowned-fisherman obituaries on her old Maine circuit. Still, it's the one true coincidence in the motive. **Fix: reverse the order.** Hollis writes in March after recognizing her on TV, and Ivy's pre-shoot research finds Shannon in April. Then Hollis's own letter brought the vulture to Shannon's door, which makes her guilt and her motive cut deeper.
- **The fire marshal ordering the balcony door unlocked the day before** gives Hollis her exit. It's planted fairly (Ch 31), but it's convenient. A line of hers — "I rang the fire marshal myself" — would turn luck into intent.

### Method plausibility (medical and physical)
- **Aconite on the lips, absorbed through the mouth over several minutes of talking into the trumpet,** with symptoms in about five minutes and death in about eighty: believable for a concentrated root preparation. **Say that it's a root extract.** Ch 37's "about four ounces of something" in a blue jar is too vague for a reader like me. Monkshood root is the potent part, and digging roots in October would also explain the turned-over bed. That's a lovely double-duty clue going to waste.
- **Applying it in a few seconds** with a gloved finger inside the seam while sliding the trumpet: plausible. Her black gloves rolled up beside the jar in the shed is good corroboration.
- **The loose end: Hollis cuts down the whole bed** (destroying the plants) but **keeps the jar and the gloves in her shed for six days.** Either she always meant to confess (have the letter say so) or she's careless, and Hollis isn't careless.
- **Lab turnaround:** an aconitine-specific blood result back by Monday morning, after a Saturday-night death, and the trumpet seam by Tuesday afternoon. Fast for a state lab. The book does say the trumpet was prioritized, and I'll allow it, but a line saying the blood went to a private reference lab would quiet the pedants (me).
- **Archie's swim:** four minutes in 52°F water, then a forty-foot crawl, then a show. That's heroic. It's survivable, and the fever next day is a nice honest touch.

### Police competence
Mostly good, which I appreciated.
- **Tess** reads people, orders the right screen, has the lips swabbed, Mirandizes Hollis on the dock and *stops her talking*, and finds Walt's file by thinking about how her father filed things (by complainant). That last one is the best piece of detection in the book that isn't Archie's.
- **Brannock** is tough and fair. His point that the raspberry could itself be a trick is genuinely sharp.
- **Dale** is comic but useful; his door-watch clears Warren.

The lapses:
- **The thermos.** "Once the evidence man said it was only a thermos" (Ch 10). At a suspected poisoning, with the victim's only drink in it, an evidence tech hands it back the same night. This is the one Keystone moment. Have Dale hand it back himself, before the State Police arrive.
- **Tess lets a civilian** onto a sealed scene and into a witness interview. The book has Brannock call her on it, which helps, but it happens twice.
- **The AG charging Ivy with murder** on a wiped scarf after Marco's card shows she never touched the trumpet: the book admits it's weak ("a scarf and a bad feeling"). It's acceptable for stakes, but just barely.
- Nobody on the State side ever asks **who in town grows monkshood**, even after Archie volunteers his own patch, and even though the woman who handled the trumpet in gloves has a cut-down border thirty yards from her porch light.

### Timeline and continuity errors
- **Dale Pinkham's history contradicts itself:**
  - Ch 15: "Eighty-eight. I was on my father's boat, that morning. Everybody went out." That puts him in Merrow in 1988, and his granddaughter lobsters there.
  - Ch 34: "Dale had come to Merrow in 2010, from the sheriff's office in Rockland... He knew the town's people now, but he didn't know its paper."
  - Ch 34 again, asked about Hollis Langley: "Couldn't tell you. Before my time."
  - A man who was on the water searching for Gus in '88 would know Hollis was Peter Langley's wife from 1983 to 2003. **Fix one or the other.**
- **Ivy's age:** she's 29 in 2026, so she'd have been six in 2003, not seven. Trivial, but I'm the kind of reader who notices.
- **Hollis's names:** the boat was the *Hollis T.* while she was Mrs. Langley. Fine if Tibbetts is her maiden name, which Bev implies, but one clause would settle it.
- **Ch 34 says** it took Tess "until three o'clock Thursday afternoon" to think of the files, then she finds the folder "twenty minutes past noon" — on Friday, as it turns out. Readable, but it made me flip back. Put the day on it.

### Verdict on fair play
**Clean on the killer, means, opportunity and motive.** Everything I needed was on the page, and early. It's weak on the Sparrow source and has one structural coincidence (the April/May order). That's a B-plus as a contest, which is better than most I read.

## 4. Herrings

- **Ivy** is honestly built and honestly dismissed. The wipe, the lie about the hand, the remote, the rod, the inheritance, "you're done when I say": all of it is real misconduct that the book never pretends was innocent. It just isn't murder. "I wasn't hiding anything on Saturday night except her" is a fine line. The Ch 26 tape measure, twenty-three inches, is a lovely bit of hardware detection.
- **Warren** is the best-built herring and the most painful payoff: the key, the poster theft, the whisper and his going pale, the stomach, the sale. He's cleared by an alibi (Dale on the door) that was on the page in Ch 7, and the reason he went pale is Carol. The Galley scene in Ch 19 is the moral hinge of the book. Fair, and devastating.
- **Teddy** has the insurance policy and the withheld camera card. His motive is cleared by explaining what a key-person policy is, which is correct. As an old claims man I appreciated that the author knows the policy pays the company, not him.
- **Kev** has the hoodie pocket, the prop-room visit and the money. He's cleared by the raspberry. It's played mostly for comedy, and it's fair.
- **Archie and Vivienne** are the police's suspects. That gave Brannock's pressure in Ch 27 real teeth, without asking the reader to believe it.
- **Bev's thermos** carries tension well and is dismissed by the seam finding.

Every herring was dismissed by evidence, not by authorial fiat.

## 5. Climax and confession

**The skiff at Shag Ledge** is earned. The bell buoy has rung through the whole book, and Peter's drowning is the reason. The book had already tied Hollis to the water and to "come see me on the water."
- Her "*Honey, where's the ladies' room?*" is the best single line of motive in the book. Lorelei didn't remember any of them.
- Archie's answer, "She remembered Peter... *God forgive me*," is exactly the right blow and it *changes her*. A climax should do that.
- The ten seconds of her pushing him *toward* the boat is first-rate.
- The cloak folded on the gunwale before he goes over pays off the Ch 2 plant and the Gus mystery at once. I said "oh" out loud.

**The confession** has two parts.
- The dock: short, Mirandized, cut off by Tess. Procedurally correct.
- The hospital, Ch 42: it's long and tidy, but it's after her statement and her lawyer, and it's in character. She's a schoolteacher; "beginning, middle, end, no adjectives you couldn't defend." The September escalation (Shannon told that Tommy "missed her on the water") is the right trigger.
- "Ask me in a year" is honest.
- The detail that she taught *How the Grieving Are Fooled* for sixteen years because of Peter recasts a running joke as a wound. Very well done.

**What's melodramatic:**
- The crawl under the stage and the wet-magician reveal in the Empty Chair is pure theater. It's a magician's book, so I allow it, but it tips into the sentimental. Lenny's "You came back" earns it. The foil "cloak made of mirrors" description doesn't need help.
- Walking away from a seventy-three-year-old mother, a sixteen-year-old and a show to go out on the water against a detective's direct order, after sitting on a sealed confession for five hours, is consistent with Archie's flaw. The book makes Tess say so plainly in Ch 43, and I respected that.
- Bev's "ten o'clock" deal plus Archie hiding the envelope is the one stretch where the lead's choices felt dictated by the plot's need to get Hollis onto the water.

## 6. Engagement

**Where my attention flagged:**
- **Ch 14:** the Baja/fake-death theory. It makes Archie look foolish rather than impulsive, and after Ch 9's sharp aconite call it's a step backward.
- **Ch 15:** hauling is charming (Harold is a girl!), but it's the second slow chapter in a row.
- **Ch 29** runs long on the Chair mechanics. It's needed for the payoff, but it could lose a page.
- **Ch 23–24:** the arrest and the family meeting tread water once you know Ivy didn't do it.

**Where I couldn't stop:**
- Ch 8, the séance: the best set piece in the book. The "Sparrow" moment and the slurred "answer'sh—in—" are genuinely frightening.
- Ch 19, the Galley.
- Ch 21, Kev in the crawlspace.
- Ch 26, Forty Seconds. June calling "*table, table, table*" is detection as I like it.
- From Ch 31 to the end, straight through: Rosie on Hollis's knee, the hatbox decoding (June working out the shifted relation numbers and dating the books by weekday), Bev sitting down behind her own counter, the garden at Pudding Lane, the doves landing on a dark booth. That last image is the best "something's wrong" beat I've read in years.

## 7. The lead and the warmth

I liked spending time with almost all of these people, and I didn't expect to.

- **Archie** is a show-off, and the book knows it. Hollis's diagnosis is the thesis: "You're a show-off, which is a different thing entirely, and it isn't fatal." His flaw costs real people real things (Warren in the Galley, Ivy and the scarf, June kept in the audience), and the book makes him *pay* rather than charming its way out. The count of days at the liars' table (fifty-nine, sixty-one, sixty-six) is a lovely running clock, and Elwin's "Morning, Archie," which he doesn't hear, broke my heart a little.
- **June** is the real detective of the family: the poster alert, the cookbook question signed "W.", the weekday arithmetic for the notebooks, "It's arithmetic." Her arc from "It's mine" to the oath on the jack of clubs to "You're late" is the spine of the book. Best character.
- **Tess** reads hands, not faces, which mirrors Ivy and turns the theme around. The poker reveal ("It's your hands. When you've got something, they stop.") is a great character beat. Her lifting the watch at the end is delightful. The hint that she wrote to the magician at twelve, with two stamps, is the right slow-burn hook.
- **Hollis** is the best-drawn killer I've met in a cozy in a long while. You love her before you suspect her, and you still love her after. "Nobody looks at the stagehand, dear."
- **Lenny**: "You didn't praise a hinge. You oiled it." "You came back." Perfect economy.
- **Bev:** "There's no third pie."
- **Warren:** "You are not going to buy my wife."
- **Lionel** standing up and saying his name: absurd, and I teared up anyway.
- **Vivienne** is a little too quippy in places; every line is a zinger. She's at her best when the quips stop: "Ozzie says you did this... It's folded exactly the way my father used to fold it."
- **Teddy** is the one character who stays a type. That's fine; he's useful.
- **Lorelei** gets one superb chapter (Ch 6) and casts a long shadow. "To the vanishing man."

This is a warm book that doesn't go soft on consequences. That's rarer than it should be.

## 8. Would I read Book 2?

**8 out of 10.**
- In favor: the Gus thread (the 1996 Key West postcard, Walt's boat, the wink), Tess, Brannock's "the weird ones," and June learning the trade.
- Holding me back: I'd want the author to show me the evidence for the next Sparrow instead of having a character assert it.

## 9. Star rating and ten changes

**Rating: ★★★★ (4 of 5).** As a puzzle it's a strong 3.5: fair, but solvable too early by an old hand, with one coincidence and one unshown source. As a novel it's a 4.5. The warmth carries it.

**The ten changes that would most improve the mystery:**

1. **Ch 32 / 44: show the Sparrow source.** Send June and Archie to the *Packet* microfilm on Friday morning (set up in Ch 5 and Ch 32) and put the November 1988 quote on the page. Vivienne asked to be shown in Ch 10, and the trunk combination depends on it. Better still, have the clipping hint who else would know the word, which feeds the postcard mystery.
2. **Ch 42: let Hollis own the candle.** One line: she watched Marco flare on the trumpet at Thursday's walkthrough and set the pillar "a hand's width" from it on purpose, knowing someone would ask the woman with the match to move the trumpet. That turns the one stroke of luck in her opportunity into the cleverest thing in the book.
3. **Ch 3 / 13 / 42: reverse the April/May order.** Have Hollis write to Marchand first (after recognizing Sister Lori on television) and Ivy's research find Shannon afterward. That removes the motive coincidence and gives Hollis a second layer of guilt: she brought the woman to Shannon.
4. **Ch 34: fix Dale's backstory.** He can't have searched for Gus from his father's boat in 1988, have a lobstering granddaughter in Merrow, *and* say "Before my time" about Hollis Langley as a 2010 arrival.
5. **Ch 17 / 37: make the bed a root harvest.** Have Ch 17 note that the bed was *dug*, not just cut. Then in Ch 37 make the jar a root tincture and have Hollis's letter explain why she kept it and the gloves ("so Tess would have it"). That clears the cut-the-plants-but-keep-the-jar contradiction and makes the dosage credible.
6. **Ch 22 / 42: pay off Ivy's fingertips.** Have the lab or Tess mention aconitine traces on Ivy's right fingertips from the wipe, consistent with transfer and not application. Ch 9's clue deserves its reward, and it would genuinely help clear her.
7. **Ch 10: move the thermos release off the State Police.** Have Dale hand it back before Tess's people arrive, then catch hell for it. That keeps the plot point and protects the competence of the lab tech and Tess.
8. **Ch 7: plant Ivy setting the props at 7:20.** Brannock reveals it in Ch 27 as if we'd seen it. One sentence in Ch 7 makes it fair.
9. **Ch 14: cut or shrink the Baja theory.** Archie should be wrong in an *interesting* way here, not a silly one. A theory about Teddy's insurance alone, properly reasoned and properly dismissed in Ch 25, would serve better.
10. **Ch 16 / 27: have the police ask who grows monkshood.** One scene where Brannock's people canvass the town, and Hollis cheerfully tells them hers is cut down for the winter. It hides the clue in plain sight *on the record* and makes the police look diligent rather than blinkered.

Two small ones: Ivy should be "six" in Ch 30, and Ch 34 needs a day on its noon.

— Raymond
