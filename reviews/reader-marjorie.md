# Early-Reader Report: *Nobody Fools Archie Fairweather*

**Reader:** Marjorie, 58, retired school librarian, Ohio. 80+ cozies a year; runs a 2,000-member Facebook cozy club.
**Read:** the whole manuscript, in order, over two sittings (I'll tell you where the sittings broke).

I'll say the headline first so you can calibrate the rest: this is the best cozy manuscript I've been sent in three years, and I guessed the killer in Chapter 17. Both of those things are true, and the second one is the only reason it isn't a flat five.

## 2. The lead and the cast

**Archie.** Yes, I fell for him, and not in Chapter 1 — in Chapter 4, when he tells Tess "Do you know how long it's been since anybody looked at me and didn't know the ending?" He's a show-off with a conscience, and the book knows the difference between those two things better than he does. What made me love him: he lets Shannon cry in the dark rather than win the bet; he says "Okay" in the library and nothing else; he puts a quart of chowder down and sits in the chair against the wall; he folds the cloak without knowing. What made me want to shake him: the Galley with Warren (which is the point, and it works), and the fact that he names four wrong suspects with total confidence (Warren, Lorelei-in-Baja, Kev, Teddy) before he's right once. Twice is a character trait. Four times is a sitcom beat. He never felt false. He did, by Chapter 25, feel a little *predictable* in his wrongness. Would I have coffee with him? I'd have coffee with him and lose six dollars and five cents.

**Tess.** My favorite. "People who are telling the truth answer the question you asked." She never softens for him; she pushes her chair six inches from the heater; and she lifts a watch off a drowning man with one hand. The two-stamps letter is a seed that'll carry three books. The romance is exactly as slow as it should be — one wrist, one "Don't tell anyone" — and I'd riot if Book 2 rushed it.

**June.** The best teenage character I've met in a cozy, and I say that as someone who has read a great many bad ones. She's not precocious; she's *methodical*, and the book respects that (the day-of-week dating, "we don't *guess*"). "It's mine." "Everything after the blotter is you." "You were only going to stand there and look surprised." She earns every line she gets.

**Vivienne.** "I'm not a soufflé." "She doesn't need you to be wonderful." "That child is not a volunteer from the audience." She's the moral adult in the house and she's seventy-three in a war hat. Ozzie carrying the lobster roll "as if it were a corsage" is the loveliest late-life courtship I've read in years.

**Lenny.** Four sweaters, "No," gaffer tape returned, "You came back." The hand on the arm under the trap is the single best line in the manuscript and he gets about forty words total. Do not give him more. He's perfect at this weight.

**Bev.** "There's no third pie." I would read a book that is only Bev. Her sitting down behind her own counter is the moment the mystery breaks, and the author trusts a gesture to do it. That's craft.

**Pinkham.** A dear. "I was the *accomplice*." "Bring one anyhow." The riverboat theory. He's comic without being a fool — he watches the stair door, he measures the lobster, he brings the cat up in a box. Kayla with her bobby pin and her traffic cone is a nice bonus.

**Hollis.** The book's heart, and its killer, and it manages both. "You're a show-off, which is a different thing entirely, and it isn't fatal." "I'll want them back by Friday. My hands get cold." She is not a cardboard villain; she's the opposite, and "You can't debunk a widow" is the most honest thing anybody says in 44 chapters. My reservation is in §3.

**The town.** Merrow is the real thing: the liars' table count, the Galley bell, the memorial stone, the fire marshal's door, Deb and her sign-out cards, the whole harbor laughing on channel 16. The paint day with the drapes coming down is the found-family chapter I'll quote to my club. Lorelei, Ivy, Kev, Teddy, Warren — every secondary has a want and a wound. Nobody is furniture.

## 3. The mystery

**When I suspected Hollis:** a flicker in Chapter 5 — the author shows me the library card, then has Deb "draw breath" and Archie's eye "already gone past" an "H. Langley." That's a flashing arrow; an experienced cozy reader notices what the detective is hurried past. Chapter 3 had already put "Peter Langley 2003" on the stone. But I *wrote it down* in Chapter 17: three tells stacked in one chapter — the uncharacteristic venom ("that woman was a vulture"), "Danny. My son," followed by Hollis turning away to the costume rack, and "the long border along her fence had been cut down to the ground and the bed turned over" right after Vivienne's "hers are a foot taller" in Chapter 2. And then Chapter 24 sealed it: Vivienne laughing "Hollis Tibbetts isn't poisoning anybody. She's doing my hems" is the double-bluff every member of my club can spot from orbit. The one character the family vouches for in a joke is the one.

So I was ahead of Archie for roughly sixteen chapters. The book survives that because the wrong turns are character-rich (Warren's confession, Kev's crawlspace, Ivy's hairbrush), but it's the structural weakness.

**Was the solution fair?** Scrupulously. The clues are all on the page: Hollis invited the show in May; she sat in the booth for Gus in 1988; the séance unit "How the Grieving Are Fooled"; the fire permit and "nobody else was striking a match"; the gloves; "slide the horn left"; Rosie's godmother; "the water's never warm here"; the garden turned over in October; her son's photo on the piano. "Nobody looks at the stagehand" is set up by the book's own thesis about hands versus faces. I never felt cheated — I felt *shown*, which is the thing I ask for.

**Cheats, coincidences, implausibilities:**
- Toxicology. Monday morning Dale says the aconite came back from the blood; Tuesday the trumpet; Wednesday the scarf. Then in Chapter 27 Brannock says the lab "is running six weeks behind." It can't be both. Cozies get fast labs; just don't have the lieutenant say the opposite in the same speech.
- Kev's thirty-nine-dollar camera happens to hold the horn in frame for seventeen uninterrupted minutes, and Marco's handheld happens to swing away for exactly forty seconds. Both conveniences are at least thematically *the trick itself*, so I forgave them; a skeptic in my club won't.
- Lorelei says "Twenty years on the road" at 58 having started at 26. Arithmetic.
- The Tess-POV chapter (34) is the only POV switch in the book. I liked being in her head — but a single switch at the thirty-four-chapter mark reads like the author needed a way to get the basement files on the page. Either give her two or three chapters across the book or fold it into Archie's.
- Hollis's suicide attempt on the ledge is the darkest thing in the book. No gore, no cruelty; but club members who want "cozy" to mean "nobody we love gets hurt" will flinch. I think it's earned. I'm flagging it.

**The police.** The best I've read in a cozy. Brannock is fair, tired, and right about everything ("Your whole career is getting a camera to record something that didn't happen"). Tess insisting on the lawyer warning for the tape. Dale watching the stair door. The one indulgence — letting Archie onto a sealed scene in Chapter 11 — is called out and paid for by Brannock himself, which is how you buy that.

## 4. The ending

**Catharsis: 9/10.** Chapters 36–40 are a single held breath, and Chapter 40 is as good as this genre gets: the voice through the floorboards, "Twelve minutes." / "I said seven," Lenny's hand on his arm, the foil blanket "like a cloak made of mirrors," "You're late." / "I came the long way." I cried, and I don't.

**Hardest-hitting:** "You came back." Hollis holding out the keys: "Somebody has to run the lights." "I know my rights, Theresa. I taught you to spell them." Bev pulling the blanket closed "like a mother at a school bus." Ivy laying the check in front of Shannon. Vivienne: "It's folded exactly the way my father used to fold it."

**Saccharine?** Paint Day flirts with it (the whole town, the light, the lobster rolls) but it's earned by sixty-four days of being ignored. "Did not vanish" at the end of Chapter 40 is one half-step on the nose; I'd still keep it.

**Rushed?** No. **Overlong?** The denouement is four chapters (41–44) and three separate landings — the diner, the hospital, the wharf, the turret. Each is good. Together they're a long taxi. Chapter 41 could lose a third (the Warren posters beat, lovely as it is, could move to the wharf or be a sentence).

**The postcard.** A crowd-pleaser and a hook, and I grinned — "That old *showman*." Two small worries: it slightly undercuts the clean closure of the Chair by making the real series engine "Gus is alive"; and "Don't let your mother sell the house" raises a question nobody's asked in 44 chapters. Keep the card; consider the line.

## 6. Prose

Does it read like an experienced, successful human author with a good team? Mostly, yes — the voice is confident, specific, genuinely funny, and it trusts gestures (Bev sitting down; the fork lined up with the plate) over explanation. It's better sentence-by-sentence than most of what's on the front table at my Barnes & Noble.

What a good editor would catch is *regularity*. The rhythms are so polished that they recur like a metronome:

- **Chapter endings.** Almost every one lands on a quiet one-line paragraph and an image of Merrow Light or the bell buoy. By my count the Light "swept its green flash every six seconds" in Chapters 2, 14, 15, 20, 24, 28, 29, 32, 36, 43 and 44, and the bell buoy closes or nearly closes 2, 3, 13, 14, 15, 20, 32, 33, 34, 38, 39, 43. A leitmotif, yes — but by the twentieth time it's wallpaper. Vary four or five of them.
- **"It wasn't X. It was Y."** "It wasn't a decision." "It wasn't a question, and it wasn't a guess." "It wasn't a trick. It was just the shape of things." "It wasn't the words. They were ordinary words." Lovely once a chapter; it's closer to once a scene.
- **"the way you'd [verb] a [noun]."** "the way you might touch a horse," "the way you'd wipe a spoon," "the way you'd describe how to change a tire," "the way you'd set down something heavy," "the way you'd hand someone a sleeping child," "the way you'd grip a man to keep him from falling." Dozens. It's a signature; cut a third.
- **Hands acting on their own.** "He didn't decide to. His hands were already doing it." "He didn't remember standing" (Chs 19, 22, 26). "The grin arrived before his brain had finished voting" / "before he could do a thing about it" / "came up out of him before he could do anything about it" / "started somewhere around his ribs." The laugh "came out without permission," eyes "went without permission," the thing "came up through him without permission." I love the idea (the book is *about* hands versus faces). The phrasing needs four different coats.
- **"very X and very Y."** "very small and very clear" (June, Ch 37; Hollis, Ch 39), "very quiet and very steady," "very beautiful and suddenly very old," "very still," "very level." A find-and-replace pass on "very."
- **Repeated gags/images across chapters:** "the whole room in a second and a half" (Chs 4, 9, 37); "pushed straight through the crack/it" (Shannon twice, then June "the way Shannon had"); "folded it in half, and in half again" (Warren's napkin, Bev's cloth, Bev with the sign, Kev's receipt); "since the Carter administration" three times (floorboard, shed roof, gloves) plus Obama, Johnson, Reagan; the year 1994 as a punchline four times (Lenny's last laugh, Ozzie's hat, Walt's Red Sox, Pickle's last words); "said nothing at all, which was worse" at least three times; "Hear me out." / "No." four times. Each of these is good the first time. Pick one.
- **Recap.** Chapter 23's opening paragraph re-narrates the end of Chapter 22.

Nothing here read "machine-made" to me so much as *un-roughened* — a writer who has found eight wonderful moves and is making all eight every chapter. One sharp pass would fix it.

**Lines I'll be quoting to the club:** "You didn't praise a hinge. You oiled it." "Darling, at our age it's the same thing." "Lips to the small end!" "She doesn't need you to be wonderful." "You can't debunk a widow." "I know my rights, Theresa. I taught you to spell them." "There's no third pie." "You came back."

## 7. Star rating and club review

**★★★★ (4 of 5)** — a 4.5 rounded down, because I had the killer by Chapter 17 and the middle third replays the same twenty-four minutes five times. If the manuscript tightens 22–27 and buries two of Hollis's tells, it's a five and the best cozy debut I've read.

**Club review:** *A retired Vegas magician, his sharp teenage daughter, his sawn-in-half mother and a four-sweater engineer move into a haunted Maine opera house and have to catch a TV psychic — then catch whoever killed her — in a town that won't speak to them for sixty days. Funny, kind, scrupulously fair, and the last five chapters had me crying over a trapdoor; I guessed the who, and I didn't care, which is the highest praise I've got.*

## 8. The ten changes

1. **Ch 5 — hide "H. Langley" in plain sight.** Right now Deb "draws breath" and Archie's eye "already goes past" the name; that's an arrow. Let Deb read the whole card aloud, chattily, six names with a story each, and let Langley be the fourth. Same clue, no arrow.
2. **Ch 17 — unstack the tells.** The "vulture" speech, "Danny, my son," and the garden bed turned over all land in one chapter. Keep Danny's photo; move the cut-down border to a glancing mention in Ch 13 (Shannon's lane) or Paint Day; soften "vulture" to something Hollis would say in front of sophomores.
3. **Ch 24 — cut Vivienne's "Hollis isn't poisoning anybody."** It's the oldest double-bluff in the genre. Let Lenny say "the girl or nobody" and let Hollis's name simply not come up.
4. **Chs 22, 26, 27 — merge and halve.** Fold "Forty Seconds" and "Narrowed Down" into one chapter; cut the timecode litany to the two timestamps that matter (19:44 Ivy, 19:51 the swing-away); turn Brannock's rain lecture from four enumerated points into two lines and a look.
5. **Ch 23 — delete the opening recap paragraph.** Start on "He knew what was on Water Street."
6. **Retire the running gags after their second use.** "Hear me out / No" (keep Ch 11 and Ch 14, cut Ch 20 and Ch 25's setup); "since the Carter administration" (keep the floorboard); the year 1994 (keep Lenny's last laugh).
7. **Reconcile the lab.** Either Dale's Monday call is "preliminary — they're calling it aconite" and the confirmation lands with the trumpet on Tuesday, or lose Brannock's "six weeks behind" in Ch 27.
8. **Vary a third of the chapter closers.** Count the bell-buoy / green-flash endings; keep the ones that matter (Ch 2, Ch 33, Ch 43) and end five or six others on a line of dialogue or a cut.
9. **Tess's POV — commit or fold.** If Ch 34 stays, give her one earlier chapter (after poker night, driving back to Augusta, would be natural) so the switch feels designed. Otherwise move the basement files into a scene Archie witnesses.
10. **Ch 41–44 — tighten the landing.** Trim Ch 41 by a third (the Warren posters can be a paragraph at the wharf); in Ch 44, consider cutting "Don't let your mother sell the house" — the postcard needs only "Still working on the trick." Also fix Lorelei's "twenty years on the road" (she's been at it thirty-two) and give the "very X and very Y" / "the way you'd…" constructions one hard editorial pass.

— M.
