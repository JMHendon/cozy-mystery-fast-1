# Craft Audit — *Nobody Fools Archie Fairweather*, Draft 1

## Verdict in one paragraph

This is a good book with a real voice, a killer who is genuinely hidden in plain sight, and three or four chapters (19, 32, 38, 40) an acquiring editor would read aloud to colleagues. What stops it reading like a finished, single-author novel is not the seams between hands — those are mostly well hidden — but **a shared house habit that every hand caught from the lead's opening chapters and then amplified**: the manner clause, the "X stopped." paragraph, the crafted exit line, and the explaining of a beat the page has already landed. On top of that sits the structural problem a cold reader will feel as "this is long": **the method of the murder is explained five times and the quarter-hour before the séance is reconstructed eight times.** Fix those two things and the draft loses 9,000 words and reads like one hand.

### 1b. What the reader feels at each boundary

**Ch 9 → 10 (LEAD → H2).** The cleanest seam in the book. H2 is the plainest hand: shortest sentences, most dialogue, fewest manner clauses, and the most *literal* answers (Lenny: "Rod or a hand. You showed the whole diner."). It is the closest to the style guide's "commercial middle register." Its one drift is **Tess**, who acquires Archie's polish in Ch 11 — "A lock is a thing." / "Then nobody stole the rum." / "Would you like to be arrested now, or after the tour?" — three quips in one scene from a card that says *deadpan rather than witty; never performs.* The other H2 tic: every chapter closes on a crafted last line of dialogue (Ch 10 "That's not what that was." · 11 "Yes, Sergeant." · 14 "She doesn't need you to be wonderful." · 16 "Somewhere I can find you."). Four in a row is a pattern a reviewer notices.

**Ch 16 → 17 (H2 → H3).** The register warms and gets more atmospheric (fog 9.2/10k; the opera house and Hollis's front room are the book's best-described interiors). H3 introduces an **interior-reflex construction the lead never used in Act One**: "He filed it. He felt himself file it." (20) · "Archie heard himself not breathe." (19) · "He hadn't known he'd stood up." (19). H4 then copies it verbatim — "He was on his feet. He didn't remember standing." appears in **both Ch 22 (H3) and Ch 26 (H4)**, and H2 had already used "He didn't remember deciding to do it. He never did." (10). That is three hands converging on one gesture for Archie's body acting before his mind. One instance is characterization; six is a tic (§2.6).

**Ch 22 → 23 (H3 → H4).** The most audible seam, and it is structural, not tonal: **Ch 23 opens by recapping the last 300 words of Ch 22** ("He had spent the afternoon in the select board room giving a formal recorded statement to a trooper with a head cold… two cars turn off Main Street… He knew what was on Water Street."). The reader just watched the cars turn. H4 is the procedural hand — Brannock's speeches grow (Ch 27 is a 700-word monologue in the rain), the harbor disappears (bell buoy 0.9/10k, fog 0.9/10k for six chapters), and the hand's own tic is "found (that) he" (4.4/10k) plus explanation-by-recap (§4). Ch 25 (Owls Head) and the oath on the scaffold in 28 are H4's best work and should not be touched.

**Ch 28 → 29 (H4 → H5).** Warm, confident, and the hand with the best feel for June. Its tic is the **silence beat**: "She didn't answer." / "Okafor's pen paused. Ivy said nothing." / "Neither of them said anything for a moment." / "The kitchen was very quiet." — 7.9/10k, double the lead. Also "of a (man|woman) who" at 5.9/10k ("the expression of a man waiting for a bus he did not expect to enjoy" — good; "the hands of a woman who had spent seventeen years doing difficult things in the dark" — explaining). One register slip: "It looked, he thought, rather wonderful" (29) — *rather* is Penn-series British; Archie says *wicked*, never *rather*.

**Ch 33 → 34 (H5 → LEAD).** The lead returns in Tess's POV with a three-paragraph expository opening on Walt's filing habits before anyone moves. From here to the end, the lead's own register drifts **away from the lead's Act One**: manner clauses roughly double (Ch 34: 5, Ch 40: 5, Ch 43: 6 — against Ch 1 at 1), "the way you'd [verb] a child/sleeping child/man" appears five times in five chapters (38, 38, 40, 40, 42), sentences lengthen (9.3% over 35 words), and — the thing the style guide forbids outright — **the narrator starts diagnosing Archie** (Ch 34, 37; §2.12). The late lead is the most *moving* writing in the book and the most *mannered*.

### 1c. Which register is the target

**Narration: the lead's Ch 3–9.** Oriented, physically textured, one judgment per paragraph, cappers earned and spaced ("Bev took her hand back from the chief and looked at it. 'Well,' she said, 'that's disgusting,' and went back behind the counter."). Ch 8 and 9 are the model for how serious material keeps Archie's voice without jokes.

**Dialogue rhythm: H2's Ch 14–15.** Flat answers, literal answers, people continuing a task ("Fold," said Bev, without even looking at her own cards.), the room laughing *at* Archie. This is the style guide's "at least a quarter of exchanges are flat" actually happening.

The late lead (34–44) should be brought *back to the lead's own Act One density*, not to anyone else's. H3/H4/H5 need one sweep each for their named tic and are otherwise close.

## 2. Prose tells — every recurring tic, with counts, examples, chapters, and a rule

Totals are whole-manuscript unless noted. "Worst" lists the chapters carrying the most instances.

### 2.1 The manner clause — 90 "the way X…" + 45 "as if/as though" + 17 "of a man who" + 11 "like a man/woman who" + 9 "the way you'd…" + 5 "with the air/look/confidence of" = **~177, or one every 500 words book-wide; one every 330 words in Ch 34–44.**
Worst: 43 (6), 33 (5), 34 (5), 40 (5), 7 (4), 8 (4 + 6 "as if"), 35, 36.
Examples the vehicle adds nothing to:
- "She lifted one hand against the glare, the way you'd shade your eyes from the sun at a picnic" (38) — the gesture *is* shading her eyes.
- "The talk stopped the way it does in a theater" (36); "the cold came up out of the ground all at once, the way it does in Maine at the end of October" (35); "the liars' table rose as one, the way it always did" (41); "the way people did" (42) — **vehicles that repeat the verb they modify** (line-edit rule 7, "kill on sight"). Ten of these: 5, 5, 7, 14, 34, 35, 36, 41, 42, 43.
- Ch 43's final paragraphs stack four in 120 words: "the way she had from the back of the opera house… the way she'd watched him across her father's wheelchair… the way she said them… the way a name had stopped him… the way his hands had stopped… the way it did on a calm night."
Examples worth keeping (vehicle carries information): "the way you'd wipe a spoon" (8 — physical precision on the clue); "as though it had been taken out of her with pliers" (15); "like a man opening a velvet rope" (11).
**Rule:** style-guide budget is ≤1 per ~500 words and ≤1 per scene. Cut to budget; delete first every vehicle that restates its verb, then every one welded to an already-complete action. Ch 34–44 need to lose roughly 25 of their ~40.

### 2.2 "X stopped." as a one-line paragraph — ~20.
"Archie stopped moving." (1) · "Archie stopped breathing." (8) · "He stopped." (5, 9, 14, 29) · "Archie stopped." (10, 16) · "Kev stopped breathing." (21) · "Kev stopped." (22) · "Archie stopped grinning." (22) · "The half-dollar stopped." (24) · "Okafor's pen stopped." (30) · "June's pencil stopped." (32) · "Archie stopped reading aloud." (32) · "The cloth stopped." (33) · "He stopped crawling." (40). Every hand uses it; it is the house's default beat for *a line has landed*.
**Rule:** keep the three that are plot (hands stop in the straitjacket, 1; breath stops at "Sparrow," 8; the cloth stops under Bev's hand, 33 — these are the book's motif of the body knowing first). Convert the rest to the next action or delete.

### 2.3 "for a long moment / a long time" — 39; "looked at him for a long moment" verbatim 14 times (2, 3, 4×2, 9, 14, 16, 18, 20, 26, 35×2, 43, 44).
Worst: 4, 35, 41 (3 each).
**Rule:** maximum one per chapter; prefer what the looker *does* with the moment (Bev "wiped her hands on her apron, came around the end of the counter" — Ch 3 already does this right).

### 2.4 "very + still / quiet / steady / level / small / clear / white" — 66.
Worst: 4 (6), 5, 9, 12, 37 (4 each). "Her voice was very small and very clear." (39) · "very still and very careful" (9) · "very quiet and very steady" (44) · "very white and very set" (36). The doubled-*very* pair is the lead's signature and appears ~12 times.
**Rule:** one *very* per page; never two in a sentence. The pair "very X and very Y" is banned outright.

### 2.5 "Something went / passed / moved / happened across (his|her) face" — 9; "(his|her) face did something" — 4; "went white/pink/red/the color of" — 15.
"Something went across her face, too quick to read, and was gone." (4) is the textbook tell. Also 7, 8, 9 ("Something happened to her eyebrows"), 30, 38, 41×2; "face did something" 4, 7, 12, 25. Kayla "went pink" three times (11, 28×2).
**Rule:** name the muscle or cut. "Her mouth tightened" / "He put his hand over his eyes" (41 — good) / nothing. "Something went across Warren Gilley's face" (41) should be *what Warren does with the tube*, which the next sentence already gives.

### 2.6 The body-before-mind reflex — "He didn't remember standing / deciding" 6, "It came out (before he could stop it)" 12, "without permission / asking / deciding / meaning to" 16, "He heard himself" 6, "He couldn't help it" 4 = **~44.**
"He was on his feet. He didn't remember standing." (22 **and** 26, verbatim) · "He hadn't known he'd stood up." (19) · "It came out of him the way the laugh had on the wharf, without asking." (22) · "His hands did it on their own." (28) · "His hands were already doing it." (38). H3 and H4 worst.
**Rule:** this is Archie's *actual* characterization (the hands know first; cf. the cloak), so it must be rationed to the moments that pay — the straitjacket, "Sparrow," the lift in Ch 20, the cloak in 38. Elsewhere, he just stands up.

### 2.7 "found (that) he…" — 24 (worst H2/H4). "He found he didn't feel any better" (2) · "found that he was grinning" (17) · "found that what he mostly felt, God help him, was an itch" (11) · "found he had nothing to say, which happened to him perhaps twice a year" (27).
**Rule:** delete "found that" and state the feeling. (Also "which happened to him perhaps twice a year" is the narrator explaining the joke of a speechless Archie — he is speechless in 5, 11, 12, 19, 20, 27, 29, 42, 43; it is not rare.)

### 2.8 Epigrammatic paragraph cappers / gnomic welds. The lead's Act One earns most of its own ("You didn't praise a hinge. You oiled it."; "There was no arguing with a roof."; "Nobody watched his hands. That was the whole point of the face."). The habit spreads:
- Duplicated cappers: "Nobody watched his hands. That was the whole point of the face." (1, then **again verbatim** in 14, then explained for a paragraph); "Nobody looks at the stagehand" (42, after 22's "In the pictures, it's always the stagehand").
- Welded aphorisms (line-edit rule 1): "Good methods always did." (25); "Every method ever invented was smaller than its trick. It had never once stopped hurting to watch." (25); "it was the only thing he had ever known how to do with a silence" (20); "Enjoyment is not intimacy"-style summaries after a scene has shown it.
- Count of chapter-final paragraphs that end on an aphorism or a theme-adjacent capper rather than an action: **27 of 44**.
**Rule:** style guide — "Not every paragraph needs a capper. Plain endings make the bright ones bright." Keep one capper per chapter maximum; let at least half the chapters end on an action or a fact (Ch 13 ends on "and then he went home" — the model).

### 2.9 "which was worse / much worse" — 3 (3, 20, 24), all the same joke (silence is worse than speech). **Rule:** keep Ch 3's.

### 2.10 "at all" as sentence-final intensifier — 41 ("didn't say anything at all," "no expression at all," "didn't change at all"). Worst: 3 (5), 27, 38 (3). **Rule:** cut on sight; it never adds.

### 2.11 "the whole (room / town / hall / Galley / harbor)" — 40; "exactly" — 45; "for the first time" — 14 (+13 "the first time"). **Rule:** halve each. "Exactly" in particular is doing the work of a comma ("It was folded exactly the way my father used to fold it, Archibald. Exactly." — 40; keep that one, it's Vivienne; cut the twenty in narration).

### 2.12 Narrator diagnosing the wound / explaining the emotion after showing it. The style guide's hardest rule ("Archie never diagnoses his own wound in narration").
- Ch 9: "Here was the thing he hated about himself… He wanted to be the man who just sat there and was kind" — names the mechanism.
- Ch 37: "understood that he had kept her in the audience all day… He had kept it from her exactly the way he'd kept every method from her for sixteen years, and told himself it was a gift." — this is the card's **Need** sentence, pasted into narration.
- Ch 37: "He had spent his whole life giving people a show. He had never once in his life let anybody give him one." — theme stated.
- Ch 34 (Tess POV): "a man who couldn't walk past a closed door without trying the handle, and couldn't walk past a friend in trouble without trying to save her, and didn't seem to understand that sometimes those were the same mistake." — the narrator explains Archie through Tess.
- Ch 40: "The audience thought it was the trick. June knew it wasn't." — after the preceding paragraph already showed June knowing.
- Ch 43: the three-paragraph gloss on "Don't tell anyone" ("It wasn't the words. They were ordinary words…") explains a callback the reader has only half the key to.
- Ch 44: the folded-cloak deduction is spelled out four times ("He'd done it without thinking. Because that was what you did…" / "Thirty-seven years ago, at dawn…" / "Folded." / "A man who goes over the side by accident in the dark doesn't stop to fold his cloak.").
- Ch 24: "It was the first funny thing anybody had said in his kitchen in days." (explaining the laugh). Ch 11: "He would think later that it was the most disciplined moment of his career." Ch 14: "the laugh had been for the room, not for him."
**Rule:** when the scene has shown it, stop. Cut the Ch 37 pair entirely; cut Ch 34's diagnosis sentence; reduce Ch 44's folded-cloak explanation to the one word "Folded." and the Sea View exit.

## 3. Ambient wit and ensemble flatness

**Who is too clever.** The style guide allows Archie plus *at most one* sparring partner per scene. The draft routinely gives polished exits to everyone:
- **Hollis** lands a crafted line in every scene she's in ("Plenty of vultures are funny. Have you ever watched one walk?" · "I can hold it till Christmas." · "Then you'll be very popular." · "I taught you to spell them." · "That's no excuse for slouching."). Her card permits this (brisk, dry), and it works as camouflage for the killer — but in Ch 17 and 31 she, Archie *and* a third voice (Lenny / Mattie / Vivienne) are all polished at once.
- **Tess** is the problem. Card: *deadpan, understatement, never performs.* Draft: "A lock is a thing." · "Then nobody stole the rum." · "Would you like to be arrested now, or after the tour?" · "He couldn't spy on a houseplant." · "I'm a cop. I keep everything." · "That's what calling me is for." Her best lines in the book are the flat ones ("Coffee. Black." never appears; "Go home, Archie." does, and is better than all the above). Give her back silence and literalism; she wins more by not answering.
- **Cameos with cappers**: Deb ("Your mother's name starts with a V"), Okafor ("Who's dead?" / "They never do." / "God help me"), Doucette ("It's not a hairpin"), Pru ("I'll make it anyway"), Marco via Lenny, Kayla ("you're going to go far" is Archie's, fine), Brannock ("Then take it out paid for." / "Somewhere I can find you." / "Each word sounded expensive."), Elwin ("Correct answer."), Teddy ("I'm a casualty."), Ivy ("You tell the truth, and then you stand there waiting to be thanked."), Mattie ("He's chumming" — keep; it's hers). **Nearly every exit in the book is somebody's crafted final word.** Light-energy §"Ambient wit" names exactly this.

**Who is doing it right.** Lenny (literal, "No."), Dale (literal, "Then what do I tear?"), Ozzie ("Won't need twenty."), the liars' table (silence as a running gag that pays in 33 and 41), Bev (plain speech: "You're eating pie."), Shannon (plain, furious, earned). The book's flat exchanges are its best — Ch 14 and 15 prove the standard can be met.

**Who has no felt personality.**
- **Kayla Bissonnette**: four appearances, three of them "went pink to the ears." She has a function (holds doors, carries gloves) and no want. Give her one thing she wants (Tess's notice; to be the one who finds something) and one thing she does about it.
- **Dr. Rao, Marisol, Pru, Doucette, Lavoie, Gagnon**: correctly cameo-sized; fine.
- **Ozzie** is almost there — the one-line courtship and "Won't need twenty" — but he has no scene of his own with Vivienne on the page. One exchange (he brings the bread in 44 and "stood in the doorway holding it until somebody told him to sit down" is nearly it).
- **Brannock** has personality but **gives the same speech three times** (16, 22, 27): "you fool cameras for a living." The third time it's the narrator's point, not his.
- **Tess's anger** is the real flatness-by-repetition: the "stop / you didn't call me / you used my witness" rebuke is delivered at the same pitch in 14, 20, 27 and 43, and in each case the exit line is hers. The relationship *resets* to the same position four times instead of moving (Q10). Ch 27's version is the best; Ch 43's should be shorter and different in kind — she is not angry in the same way after hauling him out of the water.

**Archie's own voice.** Alive everywhere except the stretch 22–27, where he becomes a man who sits in rooms being told things. Ch 25 restores him. Note that "Holy Houdini" (budget two) is used once (22) and "wicked" (card: under stress) never — he never swears, which is right, but the stress-vocabulary the card promises is missing.

## 4. Structure per the twenty questions (cozy-revision-protocol) — specific chapters

### 4a. Interview chains / briefings (anti-pattern 9)
- **Ch 22 (Evidence):** Brannock re-questions Kev about what Ch 21 showed, replays the footage "an eighth time," then lists the case against Ivy. The only *new* event is the lab call and "Is that still your statement?" — which is the real scene and is excellent. 2,062 words to deliver 600 words of event.
- **Ch 27 (Narrowed Down):** a 700-word Brannock monologue listing (a) the case against Ivy (already in 22), (b) the four people in the forty seconds (already in 26), (c) the tampering warning. Archie's only action is to answer "The airport." Tess's exit is the scene; the rest is a briefing the reader could have had in a clause.
- **Ch 30 (Visiting Hours):** Ivy recites the whole method (hairbrush, "left hand under, right hand over," remote up the sleeve, Teddy knows) — the fifth explanation. The chapter earns itself only when it reaches the Hiatus and the hatbox.
- **Ch 42 (Keys):** a confession monologue retelling Danny, Peter, the notebook, Walt, the library — all of which the reader assembled in 32–34 and heard again on the boat in 38. What is *new* and must stay: the May letter was meant to expose her; Shannon in September ("You can't debunk a widow"); "I thought it would look like her heart"; being sick in the green-room sink; the two days about Ivy; "Ask me in a year"; the three bequests; the wink. The first 400 words are retell.
- **Ch 12 (Captain's Suite)** and **Ch 16 (Person of Interest)** are interviews in form but each turns on an action (Archie's "She was going to stop"; the espresso machine) and changes options. Keep.

### 4b. Retelling events the reader saw ("Reused events must change something now")
Count of full reconstructions of the 7:35–8:00 window: **Ch 7 (live), 11, 20, 24, 26, 27, 33, 42 — eight.** Of these only 7, 26 (the footage — the real evidence) and 33 (the click) earn their length. Count of the Langley backstory: **32, 33, 34, 38, 42 — five**; 32 and 34 are the discoveries and earn it; 33 is Bev's human version and earns it; 38 is Hollis's own and earns it; 42 does not.
- **Ch 10:** June's three-finger list re-explains the method and who knows it (the reader saw 3, 5, 7, 8). The *function* — Archie at the top of the list — is good; the method paragraph before it ("A hand. Her left — Shannon had her right. She put the small end to her mouth…") is the retell.
- **Ch 23:** opening paragraph recaps Ch 22's close.
- **Ch 24:** the legal-pad timeline re-walks 7:35–7:59 for the third time before Lenny's "Who touched it after you?" The chapter's actual scene is the third camera + Luis/Dwayne + June's door. Lead with Lenny's question.

- **Ch 37:** the paragraph "Out past the end of the point… where Gus Fairweather's skiff had been found… Where a lobsterman named Peter Langley…" retells both deaths at the moment of maximum tension.

### 4e. Premature moral closure (Q18, light-energy "Check premature moral closure")
- Mostly avoided, which is a real achievement: Ch 19→20 (Warren) does *not* reform him — he pivots to Kev within a paragraph, which is exactly right. Ch 24 (Vivienne's rebuke) → he goes to the turret and doesn't open it — earned.

### 4f. Variation (three consecutive units in one location)
- Opera house: **26, 28, 31** (with 27 in the town-hall lot a hundred yards away, and 29 in the carriage house between) — four of six chapters in or beside the same building. Then **36, 37, 40** (earned by the show; 38–39 break it).
- Kitchen table: 2, 10, 24, 32, 44 — five family-at-the-farm-table chapters, four of which open on Vivienne setting something down and Lenny entering in four sweaters. The *scenes* differ; the *staging* is identical. Vary the entrance, the seat, the time of day.
- Galley: 3, 19, 33, 41 — four; all open with the bell, the liars' table, the count. Fine as a frame, but 41 and 33 are back-to-back in reading memory and both open on Archie at the end stool being handed pie.

### 4g. Where the reader can't picture it (Q11)
Mostly excellent (the opera house, the Galley, Sea View, Hollis's front room, the carriage house are all buildable). Gaps: **the Spindrift's Captain's Suite closet/hatbox geography** (12) is told, not shown — matters later; **the select board room** gets its description twice (16, 22); **the town wharf's relation to the fuel dock and the float** is assumed from 3 and must be clear by 38–39; **June's room** is the one portrait that follows the age→hair→clothes template for a room instead (desk, bed, boots, thumbtacks — fine, but the thumbtacks are a plant that never pays; either the poster goes up in 44 or cut them).
